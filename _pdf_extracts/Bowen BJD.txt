1.0  Purpose and scope
The overall objective of the guideline is to provide up-to-
date, evidence-based recommendations for the man­
agement of squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) in situ. The 
document aims to:
•	 offer an appraisal of all relevant literature up to 13 August 
2021, focusing on any key developments
•	 address important, practical clinical questions relating to 
the primary guideline objective

2.0  Methodology
This set of guidelines has been developed using the BAD’s 
recommended methodology.3 Further information can be 
found in Appendix K (see Supporting Information) with 
reference to the AGREE II instrument (www.agreetrust.
org)4 and GRADE (http://www.gradeworkinggroup.org). 
Recommendations were developed for implementation in 
the UK National Health Service (NHS).
The Guideline Development Group (GDG) consisted of 
six consultant dermatologists, two dermatology specialist 
trainees, two patient representatives and a technical team 
(consisting of a guideline research fellow, an information 
scientist and a project manager providing methodological 
and technical support). The GDG established several clini­
cal questions pertinent to the scope of the guideline and a 
set of outcome measures of importance to patients, ranked 
according to the GRADE methodology5 (Section 2.1; and 
Appendix A; see Supporting Information).
A systematic literature search of the PubMed, MEDLINE, 
Embase and Cochrane databases was conducted by the 
technical team to identify key articles on SCC in situ to 13 
August 2021; search terms and strategies are detailed in 
Appendix L (see Supporting Information). Additional ref­
erences relevant to the topic were also isolated from cita­
tions in the reviewed literature. Data extraction and critical 
appraisal, data synthesis, evidence summaries, lists of 
excluded studies and the PRISMA diagram were prepared 
by the technical team. The overall certainty of evidence from 
the included studies was rated according to the GRADE sys­
tem (high, moderate, low or very low).
Recommendations are based on evidence drawn from 
systematic reviews of the literature pertaining to the clinical 
questions identified, following discussions with the entire 
GDG and considering all four factors that would affect its 
strength rating according to the GRADE approach (i.e. bal­
ance between desirable and undesirable effects, overall cer­
tainty of the evidence, patient values and preferences, and 
resource allocation). All GDG members contributed towards 
drafting and/or reviewing the narratives and information in 
the guideline and Supporting Information documents. When 
there was insufficient evidence from the literature, informal 
consensus was reached based on the specialist experience 
of consultants on the GDG.
The Supporting Information contains the summary of

3.0  Summary of recommendations
There are few RCTs other than for photodynamic therapy 
(PDT) to support the following guidelines for the manage­
ment of SCC in situ.
The following recommendations and ratings were agreed 
upon unanimously by the core members of the GDG and 
patient representatives. For further information on the 
wording used for recommendations and strength of recom­
mendation ratings see Table 1. The evidence for recommen­
dations is based on the studies as listed (for details and 
discussion of the evidence see Appendices B–G). The GDG 
recommendations relating to referral pathways are based on 
discussion and specialist clinical experience among consult­
ants on the GDG, as evidence-based details are not available 
at the time of writing.
The GDG is aware of the lack of high-quality evidence 
for some of these recommendations, and therefore strong 
recommendations with an asterisk (*) are based on available 
evidence, as well as consensus and specialist experience 
among consultants on the GDG. Good Practice Point (GPP) 
recommendations are derived from informal consensus 
among consultants on the GDG.
In practice, most dermatologists will instigate treatment 
without first resorting to a biopsy. In the ‘real-world’ treat­
ment of SCC in situ, clinicians may select several different 
types of treatment to discuss with a patient. Decisions may 
be influenced by several factors, including the lesion itself 
(size and thickness), the equipment available and patient fac­
tors (e.g. general health, immunosuppression, or the poten­
tial for poor wound healing at sites such as the lower leg). 
Consideration should also be given to the patient’s previous 
experience of treatment and cosmetic awareness. Lesions 
are considered large if > 2 cm and are considered high risk 
at periocular and digital (and penile) sites. These considera­
tions should be discussed with the patient, as pertaining to 
the possible complications and risk of incomplete clearance 
and/or recurrence.
Treatments are presented in a sequence with the least 
invasive and topical therapies first, then surgical approaches, 
and finally treatments that require more complex or expen­
sive equipment and are not widely available. 
General management
R1 (↑) Consider punch biopsy or diagnostic excision when 
clinical and dermoscopic features are not sufficient for diag­
nosis of SCC in situ.
R2 (GPP) Curettage may be performed, for both treatment 
and diagnosis, depending on the clinical scenario and oper­
ator judgement. Histology specimens should include the 
deep margin if an assessment of potentially invasive tumour 
is required.
R3 (GPP) Provide educational material or a patient informa­
tion leaflet on SCC in situ and for any proposed treatment 
modality, for example www.skinhealthinfo.org.uk/a-z-­
conditions-treatments.
R4 (GPP) The majority of people with SCC in situ can 
be discharged safely following completion of treatment. 
Practitioners should exercise clinical judgement when decid­
ing to offer a follow-up appointment. Education should be 
provided on skin surveillance and sun protection prior to 
discharge, and the patient should be advised to return to 
their healthcare professional if there is any suggestion of 
recurrence, lack of response or a new skin cancer.
R5 (GPP) Consider following up people with SCC in situ on 
a case-by-case basis, based on clinical judgement, factoring 
in lesion size, treatment modality, anatomical location and 
immunosuppression.
No treatment
R6 (GPP) Consider conservative measures in people in 
poor health and with multiple SCC in situ lesions, especially 
on the lower legs, where there is a greater risk of long-
term ulceration after treatment. This includes a moisturizer 
(preferably urea based) and skin surveillance, proceeding to 
biopsy if SCC is suspected.

5.0  Background
5.1  Definition
Bowen disease is a form of intraepidermal (in situ) SCC, 
originally described in 1912,6 although the original lesions, 
located on sites that were not sun exposed, were possi­
bly arsenic induced. In total six patients were described by 
Bowen7,8 and Darier,9 all with disordered epidermal architec­
ture on histology. In 1911, Queyrat described three men with 
red lesions limited to the glans penis, applying the French 
term erythroplasie.10 The histology was identical to that 
described by Bowen and Darier.
Current practice is to consider Bowen disease as synon­
ymous with SCC in situ for lesions located at nongenital 
sites. In line with reduced use of eponyms, we have used 
the term SCC in situ throughout this updated guideline. The 
management of genital lesions is not specifically covered in 
this guideline.
5.2  Incidence and aetiology
The most recent data are from the Netherlands, where the 
incidence rates in 2017 were calculated from a nationwide 
cancer registry.11 In men and women these were 68 and 
72 cases per 100 000 person-years, respectively, with a 
statistically significant increase over time. The number of 
patients with SCC in situ treated by dermatologists doubled 
between 2005 and 2015. In Canada, annual incidences in 
men and women of 27.8 and 22.4 per 100 000, respectively, 
were reported in the period 1996–2000.12
Peak incidence of the disease occurs in the seventh dec­
ade of life and most studies have shown a slight female 
preponderance.11,13–16 The majority of studies report that 
SCC in situ occurs mainly on sun-exposed sites, with more 
recent studies suggesting the most common being the head 
and neck (29–54%).11,15–18 However, the lower limb seems 
to be affected more in women than in men.13,16,19 Older, 
UK-based studies have reported that the majority of patients 
(60–85%) have SCC in situ on the lower legs, which may 
indicate that the sun exposure pattern is different in coun­
tries with lower rates of sunshine.13,14 Less common vari­
ants include pigmented, subungual, periungual, palmar and 
verrucous SCC in situ. Variants exist on genital and perianal 
locations, and are termed ‘penile intraepithelial neoplasia’ 
and ‘anal intraepithelial neoplasia’, respectively, each with 
their own specialist treatment pathway.
Aetiological factors for SCC in situ include:
•	 Irradiation: ultraviolet radiation (solar, iatrogenic and 
sunbeds),19,20 radiotherapy.
•	 Carcinogens: arsenic (lesions may arise in sun-pro­
tected areas).21
•	 Immunosuppression: in particular, therapeutic.22–24

6.0  Diagnosis and investigation
6.1  Clinical presentation
SCC in situ typically presents as a well-demarcated, asymp­
tomatic, erythematous hyperkeratotic plaque with an irreg­
ular border, on sun-exposed sites of a light-skinned person. 
A pigmented variant accounts for 1.7–5.5% of cases.42–44 
This has been more frequently described in male patients 
with darker skin types, and on sun-protected areas such as 
lower limbs and intertriginous areas.45–47
6.2  Investigation
In routine clinical practice the diagnosis is made on clini­
cal grounds. Dermoscopic examination commonly reveals 
surface scaling, coiled vessels (‘glomerular’ vessels) and/
or red clods (‘globular’ vessels), with the pigmented variant 
displaying small brown dots and globules.42,48–52 If there is 
diagnostic doubt, or confirmation is required before under­
taking a certain type of treatment, a punch biopsy can be 
performed to show full-thickness epidermal dysplasia on 
histology.
If a curette biopsy is performed, this should include the full 
thickness of the epidermis and dermis, to establish whether 
there is any invasive disease amounting to a cutaneous SCC. 
Curettage may therefore be used for both treatment and 
diagnosis, and is perhaps preferable to a punch biopsy in 
certain clinical settings. This will depend on the suspected 
differential diagnoses (e.g. superficial basal cell carcinoma), 
the suspicion of invasive malignancy and the skill level of 
the operator.53–55 To avoid a scenario where the histology 
report cannot assess the deep margin and ‘invasion cannot 
be excluded’, a punch biopsy or excision is best practice in 
cases of diagnostic doubt.
Histology should be obtained before using a destructive 
modality, such as laser or radiotherapy, unless the diagnosis 
is clear. Specimens should be representative of the entire 
lesion, with large lesions perhaps requiring several punch 
biopsies or a large incisional biopsy. A repeat biopsy should 
be considered for lesions that are resistant to treatment or 
evolved in clinical appearance.
6.3  Referral to secondary care
SCC in situ that is small (< 2 cm) and at a low-risk site (such 
as the trunk and limbs) may be managed in primary care by a 
general practitioner who is capable of establishing a clinical 
diagnosis and initiating treatment options as per the guide­
line in the first instance. Surgical modalities (such as curet­
tage) will usually be performed by a general practitioner with 
a specialist interest in dermatology, who has undergone a 
period of specialty training, and is linked to a local skin can­
cer multidisciplinary team. Such individuals will be trained 
to perform a full-thickness biopsy to establish a diagnosis, 
where appropriate.
Patients should be referred to secondary care where (i) 
there is diagnostic uncertainty, (ii) lesions are large or at high-
risk sites such as the face or digit, and (iii) the clinician is not 
experienced in managing SCC in situ. The use of telederma­
tology services, where available, may allow a diagnosis to 
be made by a secondary-care physician using high-quality 
images, with management subsequently initiated in primary 
care. Lesions that exhibit features suggestive of invasive 
disease (rapid growth, pain, bleeding) should be referred 
urgently on to a pathway for suspected skin cancer.

In the last 20 consecutive patients treated for SCC in situ, is 
there clear documentation that
1.	
There has been a discussion on the choice of therapy 
with the patient and the agreed treatment regimen?
2.	
The patient was provided with information on SCC in 
situ and the treatment(s) offered?
3.	
Where surgical excision was used, was the SCC in 
situ excised completely?
4.	
For lesions on the lower leg, were the treatment 
parameters modified from those conventionally 
used, for example a shorter duration for cryotherapy 
or shorter regimen for topical therapy? In any case, 
was complete clearance achieved?
5.	
The patient has been educated on recurrence, either 
at the initial appointment, during treatment or prior 
to discharge?
6.	
If followed up, the patient was satisfied with the out­
come of treatment, including cosmesis?
The audit recommendation of 20 cases per department is 
to reduce variation in the results due to a single patient and 
to allow benchmarking between different units. However, 
departments unable to achieve this recommendation may 
choose to audit all cases seen in the preceding 12 months 
(Appendix M; see Supporting Information).
