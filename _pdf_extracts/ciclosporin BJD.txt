1.0 Purpose and scope
The overall objective of the guideline is to provide up-to-date,
evidence-based recommendations for the safe and effective use
of oral ciclosporin in the ﬁeld of dermatology. The document
aims to
•
Offer an appraisal of all relevant literature since 1970
focusing on any key developments
•
Address important, practical clinical questions relating to
the primary guideline objective
•
Provide guideline recommendations with some health eco-
nomic implications, where appropriate
•
Discuss potential developments and future directions.
The guideline is presented as a detailed review with high-
lighted recommendations for practical use in the clinic (see
section 14), in addition to an updated patient information
leaﬂet (PIL) [available on the British Association of Dermatolo-
gists (BAD) website, www.bad.org.uk/leaflets].

6.0 Background
6.1 Deﬁnition
Ciclosporin is a neutral, hydrophobic, cyclic polypeptide con-
taining 11 amino acids with a molecular weight of 1203 Da
(Fig. 1). It was ﬁrst isolated from the soil fungus Tolypocladium
inﬂatum in 1970. Initial applications of ciclosporin were mainly
in organ transplantation and enabled this branch of medicine
to expand on a scale that had not previously been possible. In
1979, it was reported that ciclosporin improved psoriasis in
patients with psoriatic arthritis.6 Subsequently, this drug has
been used for a wide range of autoimmune and immune-
mediated skin conditions. However, controlled clinical trials in
dermatology have largely related to treatment of psoriasis and
atopic dermatitis (AD).
Ciclosporin can be a highly effective drug but has a rela-
tively narrow therapeutic index. Therefore, careful considera-
tion
is
required
when
using
ciclosporin,
to
ensure
an
appropriate balance between beneﬁt and toxicity. The most
important hazards are nephrotoxicity and hypertension. As a
result, ciclosporin is reserved for severe disease and is gener-
ally regarded as a second-line agent, especially when treatment
is likely to be prolonged beyond 2 or 3 months.
6.2 Mode of action
Ciclosporin is a potent, noncytotoxic immunosuppressive
agent. The principal mode of action is suppression of the T-
helper (Th) cell response (Th1/Th2) through inhibition of
lymphocyte activation.
In the early phase of the immune response, T-cell receptor
activation
releases
intracellular
calcium
that
binds
onto
calmodulin to activate calcineurin. The calcineurin complex
then dephosphorylates the nuclear factor of activated T cells,
which in turn enters the nucleus and promotes the transcrip-
tion of lymphokines including interleukin (IL)2, c-interferon,
granulocyte-macrophage-colony-stimulating factor, IL-3, IL-4,
tumour necrosis factor (TNF)-a and others.7,8 Ciclosporin
binds to cytoplasmic cyclophillin, a family of cis-trans iso-
merases and inhibits calcineurin phosphatase,7 reducing lym-
phocyte activation and production of lymphokines (Fig. 2).
Other properties of ciclosporin include an ability to inhibit
the function of antigen-presenting cells,9 inhibition of the
release of mast cell mediators including histamine and prosta-
glandins,10 and the inhibition of keratinocyte proliferation11
and keratinocyte cytokine secretion.12

seded by a microemulsion formulation, which demonstrates

7.0 Effective use of ciclosporin: review of the
evidence
Although the drug is only licensed in dermatology for treat-
ment of psoriasis and AD in patients ≥16 years old, dermatol-
ogists have used ciclosporin for a wide range of inﬂammatory
and autoimmune skin conditions including other eczematous
dermatoses, Behcet disease, chronic spontaneous (idiopathic)
urticaria, lichen planus, connective tissue diseases, immunob-
ullous diseases, photodermatoses and pyoderma gangrenosum.
It is also used in children and young people (< 16 years of
age), most frequently in those with AD (see section 81).
7.1 Licensed indications (patients aged ≥16 years)
7.1.1 Severe psoriasis
There is substantial evidence for efﬁcacy of ciclosporin in sev-
ere psoriasis vulgaris (see section 7112) (level of evidence 1+;
strength of recommendation A; see Appendix 1). Severe psoriasis in
this context is not deﬁned by Psoriasis Area and Severity Index
(PASI) and Dermatology Life Quality Index, but rather psoria-
sis that is considered severe by the clinician and patient. The
drug has also been reported to be effective in erythrodermic
psoriasis,27–30 generalized pustular psoriasis,31–34 palmoplantar
pustulosis,35,36
acrodermatitis
continua
of
Hallopeau,37–39
psoriatic nail dystrophy40–43 and psoriatic arthropathy.44–47
For psoriasis vulgaris, the ‘standard’ regimen comprises an
initial dose for inducing remission of 25–5 mg kg1 per day
given orally, in a single dose or two divided doses. The higher
initial dosage of 5 mg kg1 per day can be given when rapid
control is required. When time is less critical, a lower dose
may be used initially and increased if the psoriasis is not
clearly improving after 1 month. Treatment should be discon-
tinued if sufﬁcient response is not achieved within 6 weeks
on a dosage of 5 mg kg1 per day, or if the effective dosage
produces toxicity (see section 104).
7.1.1.1 Alternative dosing regimens
Intermittent short courses can be used as an alternative to con-
tinuous therapy, with a view to reducing cumulative exposure
to the drug and the potential risk of renal toxicity,48 and can
maintain satisfactory, although less consistent, control for
some patients.49–52
An alternative, simpliﬁed regimen has been proposed using
a ﬁxed, preprandial, dosage of 100 mg per day either as a sin-
gle dose or divided into two doses of 50 mg, rather than
weight-related dosing.53 A 50% reduction in PASI (PASI 50)
was achieved in over 80% of patients in both groups by 12
weeks. The single dose yielded a signiﬁcantly higher propor-
tion of patients achieving PASI 50 at 6 weeks.
A 12-week, prospective, open-label study assigned 61
patients with severe psoriasis to a 25 mg kg1 per day
starting dosage and an increasing regimen (standard regimen)
or a 50 mg kg1 per day starting dosage and a decreasing
regimen (step-down regimen) group. The PASI 50 response
rate at 12 weeks was similar in both groups. The percentage
of patients achieving PASI 75 response at 12 weeks was higher
in the step-down regimen group. The mean time to PASI 50
or PASI 75 was shorter in the step-down regimen group. Rates
of adverse events requiring intervention were similar in the
two groups.54
7.1.1.2 Comparison with other agents
A small number of randomized comparative studies have been
performed comparing the efﬁcacy of ciclosporin with other
modalities. In 60 patients treated with either ciclosporin or a
dithranol/ultraviolet (UV)B regimen, the median time to
clearance was 6 weeks with ciclosporin (treated with 5 mg
kg1 per day) and 8 weeks with dithranol/UVB.55 There was
no signiﬁcant difference between times to relapse following
cessation of treatment. The PASI score was reduced by 71%
with ciclosporin compared with 47% with etretinate in 210
patients.43
A third study compared ciclosporin with methotrexate in
88 individuals;56 the initial dosages were 3 mg kg1 per day
and 15 mg per week, respectively. Treatment continued for
16 weeks. There was no signiﬁcant difference in the reduction
in PASI. Methotrexate was withdrawn in 12 individuals owing
to reversible elevation of liver enzymes and a single with-
drawal was required in the ciclosporin group owing to ele-
vated bilirubin (Gilbert syndrome). Two patients receiving
ciclosporin required treatment for hypertension.
A further study compared methotrexate 75–15 mg weekly
with ciclosporin 3–5 mg kg1 per day. Overall, 84 patients
were randomized, but only 68 commenced treatment and
were included in the analysis. At the 12-week end point there
was a signiﬁcantly greater reduction in PASI (P = 0003) fol-
lowing treatment with ciclosporin compared with methotrex-
ate (72% vs. 58%).57
A ﬁfth study comparing ciclosporin with methotrexate in the
treatment of nail psoriasis in 37 patients showed improvement
in Nail Psoriasis Severity Index (NAPSI) of 37% and 43%,
respectively; however, neither treatment was signiﬁcantly supe-
rior.58
A
sequential
efﬁcacy
study
replaced
ciclosporin
with
mycophenolate mofetil 1–15 g twice daily, in eight patients
with severe psoriasis.59 In ﬁve patients, the PASI increased by
more than 70%, while disease control deteriorated slightly in
the other three patients. Ciclosporin was signiﬁcantly more
effective than mycophenolate mofetil after 12 weeks in an
open-label study.60
A recent meta-analysis identiﬁed 48 eligible randomized con-
trolled trials totalling 16 696 patients (11 178 randomized to
biologics, and 1888 to conventional treatments, including
ciclosporin) and concluded that biological therapies, notably
inﬂiximab, adalimumab and ustekinumab, were more effective
than conventional therapies, including ciclosporin. They noted

7.1.1.3 Combination therapy
Ciclosporin can be used as monotherapy or in combination
with topical treatment and/or other systemic drugs to spare
the dose of each agent and reduce toxicity.62–64
Combinations of systemic medications for psoriasis have
been used less often in the U.K. since the introduction of bio-
logical therapies. Broader recommendations regarding the use
of systemic therapies in psoriasis are made by the National
Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE).65 A concomi-
tant weight-reducing diet augmented the response of psoriasis
to ciclosporin in a controlled trial.66
7.1.1.3.1 Combining ciclosporin with topical agents
Patients receiving ciclosporin should be encouraged also to
apply topical treatment. Effective topical modalities may pro-
vide a dose-sparing effect. Although some patients prefer the
more convenient approach of using the systemic medication
only, it is potentially advantageous to reduce the dosage of
ciclosporin in this way.
In two studies, topical calcipotriol was compared with pla-
cebo in patients taking ciclosporin and both the speed and the
level of response to ciclosporin were increased by the active
topical treatment.67,68 Furthermore, in a study by Grossman
et al. clearing or 90% reduction in PASI was achieved in 50%
of patients using ciclosporin at the very low dosage of 2 mg
kg1 per day by combining ciclosporin with calcipotriol.67
A similar beneﬁcial potentiation of the response to very low
dosage (2 mg kg1 per day) ciclosporin has been demon-
strated with the combination of calcipotriol and betametha-
sone dipropionate with ciclosporin.69
Dithranol, in a half-body study, improved the response of
seven patients with psoriasis who had severe disease showing
incomplete clearance with ciclosporin 5 mg kg1 per day.70
Clobetasol propionate accelerated the response to ciclos-
porin in a small trial.71 An 80% reduction in PASI was
achieved in 6 weeks using ciclosporin alone, and in 35 weeks
using the combined regimen.
7.1.1.3.2 Combining ciclosporin with other systemic agents
Treatment regimens using combinations of systemic antipsori-
atic drugs are employed when no single treatment is sufﬁ-
ciently effective, or in order to reduce
the cumulative
exposure to each compound by using both at lower dosages.
The level of immunosuppression is increased when these
drugs are used in combination.
Methotrexate can be combined effectively with ciclosporin in
psoriasis and psoriatic arthritis and there is greater experience
of this drug combination in the treatment of rheumatoid dis-
ease. This combination may allow lower dosages of each drug
to be used and ciclosporin did not appear to cause additive
hepatotoxicity.72 In another study, 72 patients with psoriatic
arthritis and psoriasis who had an incomplete response to
methotrexate were randomized to receive either additional
placebo or ciclosporin.73 There was a signiﬁcant improvement
(P < 0001) in the response in both the PASI and the
arthropathy following the combination therapy at 12 months.
This combination has also been reported as beneﬁcial in other
uncontrolled observations.74–76
Korstanje et al. reported less favourable results;77 methotrex-
ate was added to the regimen in four patients showing inade-
quate
response
to
ciclosporin.
Three
of
these
patients
experienced worsening of their psoriasis, which occurred fol-
lowing a dose reduction of ciclosporin owing to side-effects.
As both drugs are immunosuppressants it is of concern that
there is no good-quality, long-term, safety data.
Retinoids have generally been considered less useful in com-
bination with ciclosporin, although the combination has
been well tolerated and there is some evidence that the reti-
noid may suppress keratinocyte cancers in recipients of trans-
plants78 and patients with xeroderma pigmentosum.79 Early
reports did not suggest that this treatment combination is
synergistic.80–83 This combination has been reported to be
helpful in three cases of erythrodermic psoriasis that had not
responded to monotherapy with one of these drugs,84 but
was not useful in another series of three cases of erythroder-
mic disease.85
Acitretin and ciclosporin may increase plasma lipids, which
should be monitored. Ciclosporin metabolism can be inhibited
in vitro by acitretin, therefore ciclosporin blood levels should
be monitored if the drugs are used in combination. Blood
ciclosporin level rose in a single patient with psoriasis when
acitretin was added to ciclosporin treatment.86
Hydroxycarbamide
(Hydroxyurea),
used in
combination
with
ciclosporin, has been reported as highly beneﬁcial. The results

Fumarates do not appear to interact adversely with ciclos-
porin. Although the long-term safety is unknown, and pub-
lished experience with this combination remains limited, the
addition of fumarates to ciclosporin therapy can improve the
control of psoriasis and may also allow a reduction in ciclos-
porin dosage.88
Mycophenolate mofetil has been reported to be useful in combi-
nation with ciclosporin in nine patients. The combination was
well tolerated and more effective than ciclosporin alone.89
Sirolimus (rapamycin) has been investigated in combination

The use of phototherapy has been reported in combination

have substantial previous exposure to phototherapy.

This combination can be useful when transitioning between

In a small-scale open-label study of patients with refractory

case of generalized pustular psoriasis induced by the with-

treatment with ciclosporin 400 mg twice daily and etanercept
50 mg twice weekly.34
The efﬁcacy of therapy with ciclosporin and adalimumab
was investigated in a nonrandomized open-label study in 170
patients with active psoriatic arthritis that was refractory to
methotrexate treatment.101 After 12 months, a PASI 50
response was achieved by 95% of patients receiving combina-
tion therapy, compared with 85% of patients receiving adali-
mumab alone and 65% of those receiving ciclosporin alone

Ciclosporin showed an additive effect when used in combi-

immunosuppressant agents such as TNF-a inhibitors, should
be monitored closely for development of infections and malig-
nancy. A report of lymphoma developing in a patient receiv-
ing ciclosporin after using inﬂiximab would suggest that there
would be a need for caution if such potent immunosuppres-
sive therapies were to be combined.105
7.1.2 Severe atopic dermatitis
Ciclosporin is a highly effective treatment for severe AD (level
of evidence 1+; strength of recommendation A). A systematic review
conﬁrmed that 11 studies on the use of ciclosporin in AD
consistently demonstrated efﬁcacy.106 An additional review of
15 studies and a meta-analysis of 12 studies (which partially
shared authorship with the aforementioned systematic review)
concluded, somewhat more cautiously, that short-term use of
ciclosporin can decrease the severity of atopic eczema in
patients whose condition cannot be adequately controlled with
conventional therapies. However, there was some evidence of
publication bias, so these ﬁndings should be interpreted with
caution. The effectiveness of ciclosporin is similar in adults
and children; however, tolerability may be better in children.
There was insufﬁcient data to evaluate the long-term effective-
ness
and
safety
of
ciclosporin
in
patients
with
atopic
eczema.107
The onset of clinical beneﬁt is rapid (within days) and a
marked improvement is usually achieved within the ﬁrst 2
months. Ciclosporin has also been shown to improve quality
of life in adult patients.108
The recommended dosage range is 25–5 mg kg1 per day
given orally in single or divided (into two) doses for a maximum
of 8 weeks.109 If a starting dosage of 25 mg kg1 per day does
not achieve a good initial response within 2 weeks the dosage
may be increased rapidly to a maximum of 5 mg kg1 per day.
In very severe cases prompt and adequate control of disease is
more likely with a starting dosage of 5 mg kg1 per day.
Atopic

has

disease should be managed in collaboration with an ophthal-

AD can sometimes ﬂare up equally rapidly on cessation
of ciclosporin. To avoid this, a gradual discontinuation regi-

dose, and implementation of additional therapy is recom-
mended.111
Bacterial cutaneous infection is a common complication of
AD, which raises the theoretical concern that these infections
may be exacerbated by immunosuppression with ciclosporin.
In practice, this is not often observed, so the reduced risk of
bacterial infection owing to improvement in the condition of
the skin and reduced excoriation may outweigh any increased
risk of infection resulting from immunosuppression.

7.1.2.1 Comparison with other agents
Ciclosporin (27–40 mg kg1 per day) daily for 6 weeks was
signiﬁcantly more effective than prednisolone (initial dosage
05–08 mg kg1 per day) for 2 weeks in inducing stable
remission of severe AD in adults.114 Stable remission was
achieved in one of 21 patients on the prednisolone regimen
vs. six of 17 on the ciclosporin regimen (P = 0031).
Ciclosporin has also been compared with mycophenolate
mofetil in a study of 55 patients with AD. Ciclosporin
proved more rapidly effective, and its action ceased more
rapidly after discontinuation, but the treatments appeared
equally satisfactory during a 30-week phase of maintenance
therapy.115
The efﬁcacy of ciclosporin at a dose of 25 mg kg1 per
day proved comparable with that of methotrexate 75 mg
weekly over the course of 12 weeks.116

In one case, the successful use of ciclosporin in combination
with topical pimecrolimus has been reported.117 Although no
conclusions can be drawn from such limited experience, this
sort of combination may offer a potential dose-sparing effect.

9.2 Hypertension
Hypertension will occur in approximately 20% of patients on
ciclosporin
and
can
develop
at
any
time
during
treat-
ment.41,386,387 Hypertension generally improves when the
drug is discontinued. It is usually not possible to predict
which patients will develop hypertension, but it is commonly
seen in patients over 45 years of age. Patients with pre-exist-
ing or borderline hypertension are at greater risk.
The pathogenesis is not well understood but is thought to
be secondary to a vasoconstrictive effect on vascular smooth
muscle, particularly in the kidney. A study in transplant recipi-
ents conﬁrmed a ciclosporin-induced nitric oxide (NO) system
upregulation, and possible ciclosporin-induced production of
superoxide leading to NO destruction and inducing vasocon-
striction and hypertension.388
The development of hypertension does not automatically
necessitate cessation of ciclosporin therapy, as it is often possi-
ble to reduce the dose of ciclosporin, or treat the hyperten-
sion, to maintain satisfactory blood pressure. Treatment of
hypertension preferably includes calcium antagonists such as
nifedipine,389–391
isradipine,
felodipine
or
amlodipine,392
which do not interact with ciclosporin metabolism and are
also nephroprotective. Calcium antagonists have the disadvan-
tage of inducing gingival hyperplasia, possibly adding to this
effect of ciclosporin. Verapamil and diltiazem inhibit ciclos-
porin metabolism and are best avoided unless ciclosporin
blood level can be monitored. Angiotensin-converting-enzyme
(ACE) inhibitors and thiazide diuretics are also used in the
treatment of ciclosporin-induced hypertension.
Recommendations
•
Reduce ciclosporin dosage if hypertension occurs, and/or
treat the hypertension.
•
Discontinue ciclosporin if a satisfactory blood pressure
cannot be maintained.
9.3 Infections
Minor skin infections are common in dermatological patients but
do not appear to be increased by ciclosporin.381,393 In a report on
data from 631 patients in early trials, 48% had skin infections
and 76% developed upper respiratory tract infections.381 How-
ever, occasional severe infections have been reported, for example
a case of cryptococcal meningitis.394 Vigilance is required and
patients should be asked to seek advice in the event of illness. Viral
hepatitis B or C infections may become more active following
immunosuppression, most notably following bone marrow trans-
plantation,395,396 but this does not seem to be a common event in
organ transplantation.397 Somewhat paradoxically, ciclosporin
also inhibits replication of hepatitis C virus in vitro.398 Successful
treatment of psoriasis with ciclosporin has been reported in a
patient with hepatitis C;399 however, hepatitis B should normally
be treated before starting ciclosporin.

9.4 Malignancy
Ciclosporin is neither considered to be a mutagenic nor a
cytotoxic drug, and it does not induce chromosomal damage
or mutations in DNA. The increased incidence of neoplasia
which has been associated with ciclosporin is more likely to
arise indirectly as a result of immunosuppression inhibiting
the host response to neoplasms. The results of this have been
most apparent in the ﬁeld of transplantation and it is clear that
a wide spectrum of lymphoproliferative disease and solid
tumours occurs with increased frequency in this popula-
tion.401 It is likely that the intensity of immunosuppression in
patients who have received a transplant is signiﬁcantly greater
than
in
dermatological
patients
receiving
ciclosporin
as
monotherapy.
Apart from patients who have received a transplant, no
increased risk of malignancy has been established in dermato-
logical patients treated with ciclosporin, with the exception of
nonmelanoma skin cancer in those who have previously
received high doses of UV irradiation.402 This is relevant to
many patients with severe psoriasis. In a cohort of 1252
patients followed for up to 5 years (mean 19 years), the
expected increase in cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma was
observed, but there was no increase in internal malignancy.403
A study of 272 patients who had received ciclosporin for
treatment of inﬂammatory skin disease used the Finnish Cancer
Registry to establish the incidence of malignancy. All partici-
pants had used ciclosporin for at least 1 month (median 8
months) and the median follow-up was 109 years. The study
demonstrated no signiﬁcant increase in the incidence rates for
lymphoma or skin cancer relative to the general population.404
Notwithstanding the limited reassurance that can be derived
from these studies, there are several case reports of dermato-

and
lym-
phomas.105,406–411

•

9.5 Hyperlipidaemia
Serum triglyceride and cholesterol levels tend to rise with
ciclosporin therapy. The increase is related to dosage, occurs
within 2 weeks of starting treatment, and persists while treat-
ment continues.310,412
Occasionally, the hyperlipidaemia may be clinically signiﬁcant
and require treatment. An initial trial of dietary restriction of
cholesterol and saturated fat intake is recommended. Depending
on the nature of the hyperlipidaemia, ﬁbrates and/or statins can
be given cautiously. There are potential interactions of these
drugs with ciclosporin, because ﬁbrates and most statins, with
the exception of pravastatin, are metabolized by CYP450 (3A4
and other isoenzymes). This leads to inhibition of metabolism
of the statin, increasing the risk of myopathy and rhabdomyoly-
sis. Fibrates have also been reported to impair renal function in
a reversible manner,413 but are helpful in the treatment of
hypertriglyceridaemia. Statins (HMG-CoA reductase inhibitors)
reduce both triglycerides and cholesterol and are often used fol-
lowing organ transplantation to treat hyperlipidaemia.
Recommendations
•
Check serum lipid levels at baseline and monitor throughout
treatment, particularly in those at higher risk (e.g. patients
with diabetes and those with pre-existing hyperlipidaemia).
•
The initial treatment of clinically signiﬁcant hyperlipi-
daemia should be a restriction of dietary intake of choles-
terol and saturated fat in order to minimize the need for
medical treatment with the risk of drug interactions.

9.10 Drug interactions
Drug interactions with ciclosporin are numerous (Table 3).
Ciclosporin is metabolized by cytochrome P450, particularly
P450 3A4. Drugs that induce P450 reduce ciclosporin levels,
whereas drugs that inhibit P450 will increase ciclosporin
levels. These interactions can produce large changes in levels
of ciclosporin in the blood. Such effects may be very signiﬁ-
cant; as ciclosporin has a narrow therapeutic index, small
changes in blood levels can markedly inﬂuence efﬁcacy and
toxicity. Concomitant treatment with drugs associated with
renal toxicity can be expected to augment the risk of nephro-
toxicity. Concurrent administration of nifedipine with ciclos-
porin may result in an increased risk of gingival hyperplasia.
Ciclosporin has the potential to produce marked increases in
serum levels of statins, with a concomitant increase in the risk
of rhabdomyolysis. Ciclosporin may reduce digoxin clearance
and cause digoxin toxicity.
It is recommended that an up-to-date reference, such as the
British National Formulary (https://www.evidence.nhs.uk/for
mulary/bnf/current) or the Summary of Product Characteris-
tics
(http://www.mhra.gov.uk/spc-pil/)
is
consulted
regarding possible drug interactions whenever a concomitant
medication is used.
In addition to prescribed drugs, self-medication with herbal
remedies, notably St John’s wort, interact with ciclosporin.424
Grapefruit juice, and possibly other citrus juices, can inhibit
cytochrome P450 3A4 in the gut and liver and therefore may
increase blood levels of ciclosporin. Likely active constituents
include furanocoumarins and ﬂavonoids.425–428 The effect is
variable between individuals, and because it may be long-last-
ing, it is advisable to avoid grapefruit and grapefruit juice
completely while on ciclosporin (not just to avoid ingestion
of ciclosporin at the same time as grapefruit juice).
Recommendations
•
Check for possible drug interactions whenever the patient
is taking other medication.
•
Advise the patient not to eat grapefruit or drink grapefruit
juice while taking ciclosporin.
9.11 Vaccination
During treatment with ciclosporin, vaccination may be less
effective.429,430 Live-attenuated vaccines should be avoided
during treatment with ciclosporin. A recommendation from
the Department of Health advises that live vaccination should
not be undertaken until at least 3 months after discontinuation
of ciclosporin,431 and that there should be an interval of at
least 4 weeks between the vaccination and commencing
immunosuppression.

10.1 Pretreatment screening and counselling
10.1.1 Baseline assessment
It is recommended that baseline screening prior to com-
mencing ciclosporin should include a history and physical
examination in order to exclude any of the risk factors dis-
cussed above. Adult patients should be encouraged to comply
with national cervical cancer screening programmes. Con-
comitant medication should be noted and checked for poten-

obtained.

10.3 Monitoring
In the ﬁrst 2 months, it is recommended that serum creatinine
and blood pressure be measured at fortnightly intervals fol-
lowed by monthly measurements thereafter. More frequent
monitoring is indicated should there be a rise in blood pres-
sure or creatinine. Less frequent monitoring is sometimes
acceptable in cases where clinicians are conﬁdent that the risks
are low (e.g. in otherwise healthy young patients with mini-
mal previous exposure to NSAIDs or other nephrotoxic drugs).
Monitoring at 2–3-monthly intervals is adequate when these
parameters appear to be stable on long-term treatment after 4
months.
The serum creatinine level is related to muscle mass. There-
fore, some individuals with large muscle mass and healthy
kidneys will commence treatment with a serum creatinine at,
or above, the upper end of the normal range. Conversely,
other patients with low muscle mass may develop signiﬁcant
nephrotoxicity even though the serum creatinine remains well
within the normal range. For this reason, it is important that
changes in serum creatinine are assessed in relation to the
patient’s baseline values, and not the normal range for the
general population.
Serum creatinine may ﬂuctuate spontaneously, so single
measurements should always be interpreted with caution, as
only sustained changes are clinically important. In the case of
a serum creatinine rise to more than 30% above the patient’s
baseline value the test should be repeated within a fortnight.
If the rise is sustained at this level, then the ciclosporin dosage
should usually be reduced by at least 1 mg kg1 per day for
at least 1 month. If the value then falls back to less than 30%
above baseline, then ciclosporin can be continued at the
reduced dosage. However, should the creatinine level not
decrease below the 30% above the patient’s baseline value
then ciclosporin should usually be discontinued. Reintroduc-
tion of ciclosporin may be tried when renal function returns
to within 10% elevation over the patient’s baseline value, but
if elevation of 30% recurs it should usually be discontinued
permanently.
In ciclosporin use over 2 years, renal ﬁbrosis becomes more
common and may not be detected using creatinine measure-
ments. Supplementary information on renal function may be
obtained by measurement of isotope GFR every 2 years, or
more often in patients with deteriorating renal function.
Persistent elevation of blood pressure can be managed either
by reducing the dosage of ciclosporin or by introducing anti-
hypertensive drug therapy. NICE guidance recommends that
blood pressure in adults aged up to 79 years should be main-
tained below a target of 140/90 mmHg,436 (130/80 mmHg
for patients with diabetes who have eye, renal or cerebrovas-
cular involvement)437 and this level has also been adopted as
a threshold for intervention during trials of ciclosporin in pso-
riasis.
Additional investigations consisting of fasting lipids, liver
function tests, potassium and urate levels can be monitored at
less frequent intervals, depending on the results.
It is advisable to repeat a physical examination at approxi-
mately 6-month intervals in patients on prolonged treatment.
Intensive screening for malignancy is not indicated but
patients should be advised not to ignore standard recommen-
dations regarding screening procedures for breast and cervical
cancer.
Patients at high risk of HIV, hepatitis B and hepatitis C should
be monitored clinically during therapy and retested if the his-
tory, physical signs or blood tests suggest this is necessary.
Routine monitoring of ciclosporin blood trough levels has
not proved helpful in dermatology. This investigation may be
helpful to investigate compliance or drug interactions or in
cases with an unexpected deterioration in renal function on
long-term treatment. A blood ciclosporin level measured at
2 h after dosing appears to correlate with response and may
be a useful investigation when the response is poor.438
10.4 Contraindications (Level of evidence 4; strength of
recommendation D)
Most ‘contraindications’ are relative, rather than absolute, but
there are several situations in which particular caution is
advised when using ciclosporin.439
Normally, ciclosporin should not be used for patients with
uncontrolled hypertension or abnormal renal function. Severe
hepatic dysfunction may impair ciclosporin metabolism.440,441
Serious infections of any type will usually be contraindications.
Use of ciclosporin is considered more hazardous in patients
with pre-existing primary or secondary immunodeﬁciency.

12.0 Economic considerations
12.1 Cost of drug
Psoriasis can be treated topically, at signiﬁcantly lower cost, but
this approach is usually inadequate in patients with severe disease.
Biological agents, e.g. etanercept, adalimumab and ustek-
inumab, are now widely used for treating severe psoriasis and
are signiﬁcantly more expensive, averaging £9500 per annum,
although less costly biosimilars are now becoming available.442
A U.S. study evaluated the costs of a range of systemic ther-
apies for psoriasis including the overall costs of monitoring
and supervision of the treatments. The annual cost of ciclos-
porin treatment for psoriasis at a dosage of 5 mg kg1 per
day was estimated to be $10,021. This study also compared
ciclosporin with the costs of methotrexate ($1,330), acitretin
($21,736), etanercept ($24,503), adalimumab ($24,049) and
ustekinumab ($22,657).443
There are a number of different formulations for ciclosporin
available in the U.K. In March 2018 these varied in price
between £1275 and £1825 for 60 9 10 mg tablets, between
£1114 and £1837 for 30 9 20 mg tablets, between £2560
and £3597 for 30 9 50 mg tablets and between £4159 and
£10993 for 30 9 100 mg tablets. The latest prices are available
at https://bnf.nice.org.uk/medicinal-forms/ciclosporin.html.
There is a lack of good-quality health economic evaluations
comparing total treatment costs of, and beneﬁts from, ciclos-
porin with comparators.
13.0 Future directions
Ciclosporin has been the subject of intensive research over the
last ﬁve decades. The pharmacological effects and hazards of
this drug are now fairly well established.
In dermatology, the use of ciclosporin has been most thor-
oughly investigated in the treatment of psoriasis and AD.
There is a need for further investigation of efﬁcacy and tolera-
bility in other indications, for example rigorous trials in con-
nective tissue diseases.
