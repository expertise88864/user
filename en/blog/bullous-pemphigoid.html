Bullous Pemphigoid Explained: Causes, Diagnosis, Treatment and Care

Bullous pemphigoid is a common autoimmune blistering disease of older adults. Causes, drug triggers, diagnosis, treatment options and blister care, from basics to clinical detail.

Key points in 30 seconds

Key points
  • Bullous pemphigoid is an autoimmune disease in which antibodies attack the skin's anchoring proteins. It is not contagious and mostly affects people over 60.
  • It often starts with itch and eczema- or hive-like patches before tense blisters appear; about 1 in 5 patients start without blisters, and many of them blister later.
  • Diagnosis usually rests on a skin biopsy with direct immunofluorescence, supported by blood antibody tests; a positive antibody blood test alone does not confirm it.
  • Treatment is based on potent topical or low-dose oral corticosteroids, often with doxycycline or other steroid-sparing drugs; it usually lasts 9–12 months or longer, and relapses happen.
  • Some diabetes drugs (DPP-4 inhibitors), diuretics and cancer immunotherapy are linked to pemphigoid; discuss with the prescriber rather than stopping a medicine on your own.

What is bullous pemphigoid?

The outer skin layer (epidermis) is anchored to the dermis by the basement membrane zone, which contains two key anchoring proteins called BP180 and BP230. In bullous pemphigoid (BP) the immune system makes antibodies against these proteins; the resulting inflammation separates the epidermis from the dermis, and fluid collects as a blister [1][2].

In Europe it is the most common autoimmune blistering disease (in places such as Thailand and Malaysia pemphigus may be commoner). It is an immune problem, not a bacterial or viral infection, and no specific infection has been shown to cause it [2]. Pemphigus, despite the similar name, is a different disease: the split is within the epidermis, so blisters are thin and break easily; pemphigoid blisters are thick and tense [2][3].

Who gets it?

  • Older adults: most patients are over 60; in a UK study of 869 patients the median age was 80, and children are rarely affected [2]. In Europe the incidence is about 4–22 per million people per year, rising to 189 per million in those over 80 in a German study, and it appears to be increasing [2]. We found no nationwide incidence figure for Taiwan, but a National Health Insurance database study found stroke, dementia and Parkinson disease to be markedly more common among patients with pemphigoid [9].
  • Neurological disease: dementia, Parkinson disease, stroke and multiple sclerosis are associated, possibly because similar proteins exist in the brain and the skin [3].
  • Some medicines: the most often reported links are with DPP-4 inhibitors (gliptins) for diabetes, diuretics (especially aldosterone antagonists such as spironolactone, and furosemide) and immune checkpoint inhibitors for cancer (such as nivolumab and pembrolizumab); more than 90 drugs have been reported, mostly with uncertain strength of association [1][2][4].
  • Others: sites of radiotherapy or injury, and lichen planus, occasionally trigger it locally [3].

Symptoms: itch first, then rash, then blisters

Many people have a prodromal phase of weeks to months before blisters: intense itch, scratch marks, or eczema- or hive-like red plaques. Unlike true hives, these plaques are not transient [3].

  • Typical blisters: about 1–3 cm and tense, on red or normal-looking skin; common on the trunk, limb flexures, armpits and groin [3].
  • Broken blisters leave moist raw areas and crusts that usually heal without scarring [3].
  • Itch is common and can be severe [3][4].
  • About 10–30% have mouth sores, mostly inside the mouth [3][4].
  • Non-blistering form: about 1 in 5 patients have only itch, eczema-like patches or firm nodules, and some develop blisters later [3][4]. This form is easily mistaken for eczema; see Elderly eczema that won't clear: could it be pemphigoid?
  • Localized form: up to about 30% involve one area, such as the lower legs, anogenital skin or around a stoma; it may stay localized or spread [3].

How is it diagnosed?

Diagnosis combines the clinical picture with laboratory tests [3]. The usual work-up is:

  1. Two small punch biopsies (about 4 mm each): one from the edge of a blister to look at the tissue, and one from intact skin next to a blister for direct immunofluorescence, which shows antibodies deposited in a line along the basement membrane. Direct immunofluorescence is the key test [3][4].
  2. Blood tests: BP180 and BP230 antibodies (ELISA) and indirect immunofluorescence support the diagnosis and help track response [3][4].
  3. A general health check before treatment (blood count, glucose, liver and kidney tests, blood pressure, bone health), because the medicines can be demanding for older people [5][6].

A positive ELISA alone does not confirm the diagnosis: about 7% of people without pemphigoid also test positive for BP180 or BP230 antibodies, so results are read together with the biopsy [3].

How is it treated?

The goals are to stop new blisters, relieve itch and heal the skin while keeping side effects to a minimum. Because patients are often older with other illnesses, much of the harm comes from comorbidities and treatment rather than the disease itself, so the aim is the least medicine that works [5][6].

  • Potent topical corticosteroid (clobetasol) applied to the whole body (including unaffected skin; the face is spared until control). Daily amounts are measured in tens of grams, so help from family or carers is usually needed. In a large randomized trial, whole-body clobetasol gave better survival and fewer severe complications than high-dose oral steroid in extensive disease [1][6].
  • Oral corticosteroid: usually low-dose prednisolone (0.5 mg per kg body weight), tapered slowly once controlled; this is safer than the high doses used in the past [1][6].
  • Steroid-sparing medicines: the antibiotic doxycycline (used here for its anti-inflammatory effect), methotrexate, mycophenolate, azathioprine or dapsone, chosen to suit the patient [1][6].
  • Biologics: dupilumab was approved by the US FDA for bullous pemphigoid in 2025 [6]; omalizumab, rituximab and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) are used for difficult cases [1][6]. In Taiwan, the TFDA licence data (checked 18 November 2026) list adult bullous pemphigoid for the 300 mg dupilumab product, but the NHI reimbursement rules (2026-09-22 version) do not cover it, so it is self-paid [20][21].

Treatment usually lasts 9–12 months and sometimes years. Relapses during tapering or after stopping are common, so please do not stop or reduce medicines on your own [1][5].

If a medicine may be the trigger (for example a DPP-4 inhibitor), the dermatologist will discuss alternatives with the prescribing doctor. Do not stop diabetes medicines, diuretics or cancer treatment on your own [1][4][5].

Care at home: blisters, wounds and side effects

  • Leave small blisters intact. Large ones can be drained by staff or trained carers with a sterile needle at the lowest point, keeping the blister roof as a natural dressing [1][5].
  • If a blister has burst, trim only the loose skin and cover raw areas with non-stick dressings to reduce pain and infection [1][5].
  • Signs of infection: pus, spreading redness or increasing pain need prompt review [5].
  • On oral steroids: watch blood glucose and blood pressure, and take calcium and vitamin D; long-term use may need bone-protecting treatment [1]. Ask before any vaccination, especially live vaccines [1].
  • Patients who are malnourished may need nutritional supplements; discuss poor appetite with your doctor [1].

When to seek care promptly

  • Blisters keep appearing, or itch keeps getting worse.
  • Sore, itchy or burning eyes or vision changes; difficulty swallowing; hoarseness or nasal blockage. These may need eye, gastrointestinal or ENT assessment [5].
  • Signs of wound infection.
  • Marked rise in blood glucose or blood pressure on steroids [1].

Frequently asked questions

Is pemphigoid contagious?

No. It is an autoimmune disease, not a bacterial or viral infection, and no specific infection has been shown to cause it; just keep wound care clean [2].

Will it go away? Can it come back?

It is a chronic condition that waxes and wanes over months to years; some people go into remission within a few years. Relapse in the first year after stopping is common, so follow-up continues after treatment ends [1][3][5].

Is it the same as pemphigus?

No. Both are autoimmune blistering diseases, but the target, the blisters, the treatment intensity and the outlook differ; pemphigoid mostly affects older people and its blisters are tense [2][3].

Do I really need so much cream?

Whole-body potent steroid cream is supported by randomized trials, but the amounts are large and hard for many families. If it is not manageable, tell your doctor; low-dose oral steroid or other combinations are alternatives [1][6].

Is pemphigoid dangerous?

Patients are often elderly with other illnesses and on long-term treatment, and overall mortality is about two to three times that of people of the same age; severe infection and heart disease are the commonest causes of death, and much of the harm comes from comorbidities and treatment rather than the disease itself [1][4][5]. Early diagnosis, the least medicine that works and good wound care reduce the risk.

For healthcare professionals

For family physicians, pharmacists, nurses and other health professionals outside dermatology

Epidemiology and triggers

European incidence is 4–22 per million per year; 189 per million over 80 in Germany and 163 per million over 90 in Switzerland, and French incidence in 2000–2005 was three times that of 1986–1992 [2]. The EADV guideline cites mortality two to threefold that of the age- and sex-matched population [1]; UpToDate gives reported one-year mortality of 11% to 48% (Europe 19–48%, US 11–23%) [5], whereas the 2025 JAAD review labels the same range three-year mortality [4]. Poor prognostic factors: advanced age, neurological disease, poor general condition and long-term high-dose corticosteroids [1]. A global meta-analysis gives a pooled cumulative incidence of 5.6 per million people in Asia and 10.3 in Europe (not comparable with the annual rates above) [11]. In Taiwan's National Health Insurance data (3,485 patients vs 17,425 controls), BP was associated with dementia (OR 4.81), epilepsy (3.97), Parkinson disease (3.49), stroke (3.30), schizophrenia (2.56) and psoriasis (2.02) [9]; in another study, 40.7% of 12,300 patients had a serious infection within 2 years (adjusted HR 2.01 vs 49,200 controls), with systemic corticosteroids the strongest risk factor [10]. We found no Taiwanese nationwide incidence figure.

DPP-4 inhibitors: in an Israeli case-control study (82 patients with BP and diabetes vs 328 matched diabetic controls) the adjusted OR was 3.2 (95% CI 1.9–5.4), highest for vildagliptin (OR 10.7) and linagliptin (OR 6.7), not significant for sitagliptin; associations were strongest in those under 70 and in men, mucosal involvement was commoner, and patients who stopped the drug seemed to respond better [2][12]. The latency for gliptins and checkpoint inhibitors may exceed one year [1]. A meta-analysis of case-control studies gives a pooled OR of 1.92 (95% CI 1.55–2.38) [13]. In Taiwanese insurance cohorts, DPP-4 inhibitor users had an aHR of 2.382, highest for vildagliptin (aHR 2.849) [14], and a study matching 124,619 users 1:1 found HR 2.15 with 6-year cumulative incidence of 0.74 vs 0.38 per 1,000 [15]. In an Israeli study of 1,458 patients the risk peaked 1–2 years after starting the drug (OR 2.66) and stayed raised beyond 6 years [16]. In a Japanese nationwide retrospective study, gliptin-associated cases were more often non-inflammatory (33.3% vs 14.6%), and 17.6% remitted after stopping the drug without systemic therapy [17].

Immune checkpoint inhibitors: BP affects about 0.3–1% of patients, typically 6–12 months after starting, often with a long itch-only prodrome, and about 40% have mucosal involvement; 87% have their immunotherapy interrupted, yet stopping does not always halt the disease. The 2025 JAAD review advises joint decisions with oncology, aiming to continue immunotherapy where possible [4].

Whether to stop a suspected drug: EADV makes no firm recommendation either way; if the link is plausible and stopping is harmless, it advises an interdisciplinary decision, and switching to another class of glucose-lowering drug may be considered [1].

Clinical variants

  • A prodrome of weeks to months with itch and eczematous, papular or urticarial lesions [3].
  • Non-bullous presentations in about 20% (eczematous, urticarial, prurigo-like or itch alone); the 2025 JAAD review estimates about two-thirds later blister [3][4].
  • Localized disease in up to 30%; rarer forms include dyshidrosiform (palms and soles, often haemorrhagic), vegetans and lichen planus pemphigoides [3].
  • Mucosal involvement in 10–30%, mostly buccal mucosa and soft palate; these patients tend to be younger, with more extensive skin disease, and need more intensive therapy [4].

Diagnostic details

  • H&E biopsy: an intact vesicle or the edge of a bulla with adjacent skin, in formalin; shows a subepidermal blister with eosinophils. Histology supports but does not establish the diagnosis [3][4].
  • Direct immunofluorescence (DIF): intact perilesional skin within 1–2 cm of a blister, not in formalin (Michel's medium, or saline gauze sent promptly); prefer the trunk, as lower-limb samples give more false negatives. Linear IgG and/or C3 along the basement membrane in over 90% according to UpToDate; sensitivity 88.3% and specificity 99.2% in the Meijer study. Repeat if negative and suspicion remains [3][4].
  • Indirect immunofluorescence on salt-split skin: BP antibodies bind the roof (epidermal side); floor (dermal) binding suggests epidermolysis bullosa acquisita, bullous lupus, anti-p200 or anti-laminin 332 pemphigoid [3][4].
  • ELISA: BP180 NC16A sensitivity 72–90% and specificity 90–99%; BP230 about 61% and 96%; combining both improves detection. BP180 levels track disease activity, but about 7% of people without BP test positive, so serology alone is not diagnostic [3].
  • Minimal criteria (Meijer et al, two of three): pruritus and/or predominant blisters; linear IgG/C3 on DIF; epidermal-side IgG on salt-split skin indirect immunofluorescence [3].
  • Peripheral eosinophilia and raised serum IgE are common but not diagnostic [3].

Severity and pre-treatment assessment

EADV grades severity with BPDAI: <20 mild, 20 to <57 moderate, ≥57 severe; or ≥10 new blisters per day at several sites is severe; localized means one body site [1]. Before treatment: blood count, liver and kidney tests, electrolytes, fasting glucose, albumin; hepatitis B, C and HIV serology and TB assessment before immunosuppression; G6PD before dapsone, optional TPMT before azathioprine (the UpToDate authors also test NUDT15); exclude IgA deficiency before IVIG; bone density and eye checks if long-term systemic steroids are planned; age-appropriate cancer screening and a neurological assessment [1][5][6].

Treatment: the EADV 2022 ladder

  • Topical clobetasol 0.05%: 20–30 g/day for mild-moderate and 30–40 g/day for extensive disease, once or twice daily to the whole body (sparing the face until control, and in severe disease the anogenital area); continue the same dose for 15 days after control, then taper: daily in month 1, alternate days in month 2, twice weekly in month 3, weekly from month 4, for 4–12 months in total. Localized disease: 10 g/day to the affected area [1]. A 312-patient trial compared a milder regimen (10–20 g/day for moderate and 20–30 g/day for extensive disease, stopped within 4 months) with 40 g/day tapered over 12 months: control by day 21 was similar (98% vs 100%), but one-year disease-free survival was lower with the milder regimen (30% vs 45%; it also stopped earlier); after adjustment, moderate disease had fewer deaths and life-threatening events on the milder regimen [6].
  • Oral corticosteroid: prednisone/prednisolone 0.5 mg/kg/day for both mild-moderate and severe disease; if not controlled in 1–3 weeks, increase to 0.75 mg/kg/day or add topical steroid. High-dose 1 mg/kg/day carries higher mortality and side effects and is not recommended initially. Start tapering 15 days after control, aiming for minimal therapy (0.1 mg/kg/day) within 4–6 months [1].
  • Doxycycline 200 mg/day: in the BLISTER trial (253 patients) treatment success at week 6 was 74% vs 91% with prednisolone 0.5 mg/kg/day, meeting the prespecified noninferiority margin; severe, life-threatening or fatal events over 52 weeks were 18% vs 36% [6]. EADV considers it for mild-moderate disease when oral steroids or immunosuppressants are unsuitable, with limited benefit in severe BP [1]. Photosensitivity and GI upset are the main side effects [6].
  • Conventional immunosuppressants (added for steroid-dependent or relapsing disease): methotrexate 5–12.5 mg weekly (watch renal function and white cells), mycophenolate mofetil 1–3 g/day, azathioprine 1–3 mg/kg/day (guided by TPMT); dapsone 1–1.5 mg/kg/day (max 150 mg; monitor for haemolysis and methaemoglobinaemia) [1][6].
  • Dupilumab: 600 mg subcutaneously, then 300 mg every 2 weeks [6]. In the pivotal LIBERTY-BP ADEPT trial (106 adults, with oral steroids tapered off by week 16), sustained remission at week 36 was 18.2% vs 4.0% on placebo in the primary analysis (p=0.025), but 18.3% vs 6.1% in the re-analysis requested by health authorities (95% CI of the difference −0.8 to 26.1, not statistically significant); a meaningful itch reduction (≥4-point NRS fall) occurred in 39.8% vs 10.5%; cumulative oral steroid was 2.8 g vs 4.1 g by median comparison (not significant by mean analysis) [18]. As of 18 November 2026 we found no peer-reviewed publication of the efficacy results on PubMed. The US approved it in 2025 [6]; in the EU the company withdrew its application in June 2026 after a divergence of views with the CHMP on whether the data were sufficient [19]. No routine laboratory monitoring is needed; conjunctivitis and arthralgia are notable side effects [6]. EADV 2022 still lists it for recalcitrant disease, whereas the more recent UpToDate topic and the 2025 JAAD review place it among preferred options [1][4][6].
  • Other options for recalcitrant disease: rituximab (rheumatoid arthritis protocol, 1 g IV twice, 2 weeks apart) gives complete remission in about 67–90%, weighed against relapse and serious infection in frail patients; omalizumab suits urticarial lesions with high IgE; IVIG 2 g/kg per 4-weekly cycle is costly; immunoadsorption only in experienced centres [1][4][6]. Ciclosporin is not recommended [1]. Other targets (IL-5, eotaxin, complement, FcRn, IL-17 and others) remain in trials, and some trials have stopped for lack of efficacy [8].

Monitoring, prevention and stopping

  • Visits: every 1–2 weeks until controlled, monthly for the next 3 months, then every 2 months to three times a year until treatment stops [1].
  • Bone: baseline assessment if systemic steroids will exceed 3 months; calcium and vitamin D from the start; consider a bisphosphonate in postmenopausal women and men over 50 on steroids for more than 3 months [1]. Extensive topical steroid can also suppress the adrenal axis [1][6].
  • Vaccines: patients on prednisone >20 mg/day for over 2 weeks or on immunosuppressants should receive influenza and pneumococcal vaccines; live vaccines are contraindicated; vaccinate ideally when quiescent and before immunosuppression [1]. Pneumocystis jirovecii prophylaxis is optional [1].
  • Relapse: after control, ≥3 new lesions a month, one large (>10 cm) eczematous or urticarial plaque not healing within a week, extension of lesions, or daily itch [1][5]. Extensive disease (HR 2.37) and dementia (HR 2.09) predict relapse within a year, as does a smaller fall in BP180 antibody over the first 60 days [5].
  • Stopping: after 1–6 months of complete remission on minimal therapy (prednisone 0.1 mg/kg/day, clobetasol 10 g weekly or an immunosuppressant); a BP180 ELISA below 23 IU/mL before stopping carries about a 90% probability of no relapse. A visit in the third month after stopping detects most relapses [1].

For primary care: when to refer

  • Refer adults over 60 with itch or eczematous/urticarial eruptions that fail several weeks of standard treatment, or with tense blisters, for biopsy and DIF [3][4].
  • Eye symptoms to ophthalmology, dysphagia to gastroenterology, hoarseness or nasal obstruction to ENT [5].
  • Shared care: glucose and blood pressure, bone health, infection and vaccination; discuss switching a DPP-4 inhibitor with the dermatologist [1][5].
