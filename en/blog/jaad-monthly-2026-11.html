Five key points this month

Key points
  • Severe pediatric AD: in a post hoc analysis of a dupilumab trial, 31.3% vs 15.5% on placebo gained ≥5 height percentiles at week 16 (children below the 40th percentile at baseline). The message is "control the disease and monitor growth", not "the drug makes children taller". → Paper 1
  • AAD guideline: when adult AD fails to respond, re-examine the diagnosis first, with biopsy (multiple biopsies if cutaneous lymphoma is suspected), patch testing and scraping/KOH as indicated, before switching to another advanced therapy. → Paper 2
  • Alopecia areata: real-world ritlecitinib in adolescents reached SALT ≤20 in 38.8% at week 36, so response takes time; retinal abnormalities had a risk ratio of 3.88 but absolute rates of only 2.0% vs 0.7%, which does not support routine fundus screening. → Papers 6 and 7
  • Think of nonbullous pemphigoid in elderly patients with intensely itchy "eczema" that does not respond: neurological comorbidity had an odds ratio of 12.64; order DIF together with BP180/BP230 ELISA, and a negative ELISA does not exclude it. → Paper 9
  • PJP prophylaxis with rituximab: incidence was 1.98% without prophylaxis vs 0.32% with it. Not every patient needs prophylaxis, but those with added risk factors such as high-dose corticosteroids should be assessed. → Paper 10

How to read this report

This report is written for dermatologists. Each month we select the most clinically relevant studies from the current issue of the Journal of the American Academy of Dermatology (JAAD) and appraise them in depth: design, key numbers, limitations, clinical interpretation, relevance to Taiwan and funding, with the DOI of each paper so readers can go to the full text. All numbers are taken from the papers; where a paper's text and tables disagree, the conflicting number is not used and the discrepancy is noted.

Evidence labels: "High" = randomized trials, meta-analyses or guidelines with explicit grading; "Moderate" = well-designed observational studies, post hoc analyses or guidelines with limited evidence; "Preliminary" = small, single-centre or retrospective studies, research letters or expert opinion. The label is our judgement of the study design, not the authors' own grading.

For general readers: this report uses a lot of technical language. For plain-language information about the conditions, see our patient articles, such as the atopic dermatitis overview and the alopecia areata guide.

Inflammatory skin disease

1. Dupilumab and linear growth in children with severe AD: height percentiles and BALP

Evidence: Moderate (exploratory post hoc analysis of the phase 3 RCT LIBERTY AD PEDS and its open-label extension PED-OLE; all P values nominal)

Source: Irvine AD, et al. Dupilumab treatment improves linear growth and bone biomarkers in children with atopic dermatitis. J Am Acad Dermatol. 2026;95(5):1324-1334. doi:10.1016/j.jaad.2026.07.006

Design and population: Children aged 6–11 years with severe atopic dermatitis (AD) were randomized to dupilumab (300 mg every 4 weeks or 200 mg every 2 weeks) or placebo for 16 weeks, all with topical corticosteroids (TCS), then all received dupilumab to week 52. In all, 304 children (153 girls, 151 boys) were analyzed. Height was converted to age-specific percentiles on CDC charts; the main outcome was the proportion of children below P50/P40/P30/P25 at baseline who gained ≥5 height percentiles. Bone alkaline phosphatase (BALP) was measured retrospectively in stored samples.

Main results: At baseline, children with severe AD were over-represented in the lower height percentiles, and mean BALP (about 60–64 μg/L) sat near the lower end of the reference interval (reference means for ages 8–11 about 115–130 μg/L). Key numbers (children below P40 at baseline):

Limitations: Post hoc, with several cut-offs and unadjusted P values; at the P50 cut-off the week-16 difference was not reported as significant (29.0% vs 15.7%). Week 52 has no control group, and the paper is inconsistent about whether week-52 gains are measured from baseline or from week 16. Height measurement was not standardized across sites, and neither pubertal stage nor familial (parental) height potential was assessed. Between P25 and P40, 5 percentiles equal only about 0.13–0.15 SD (our normal-distribution conversion), so measurement error matters. BALP rose but stayed well below the reference mean, and other bone markers had very few samples. At week 52 the BALP for the 200 mg q2w arm is 82.1 in the text but about 85 in Fig 3, and only 19 children in that arm had data.

Clinical interpretation: The strongest piece is the randomized week-16 comparison, and the TCS-adjusted odds ratios were similar, which suggests the effect is not only steroid sparing; the data cannot separate a direct IL-4/IL-13 effect from indirect gains in sleep and inflammation. The direction matches the 2025 TriNetX cohort by Chen et al (dupilumab, vs conventional systemic immunomodulators, was associated with lower risk of falling below height percentiles), but neither study supports prescribing to promote growth. In practice, record height and weight on a growth chart at each visit in children with severe AD; faltering growth is a reason to reach effective disease control sooner and avoid repeated oral steroids. BALP does not need to be a routine test.

Taiwan applicability: NHI §13.17.2 reimburses dupilumab for children aged 6 to under 12 years with EASI ≥16, IGA ≥3, and failure of topical therapy (medium-potency or stronger TCS plus a TCI for 12 weeks) and of at least one systemic immunosuppressant at full dose for 12 weeks; it requires a dermatologist or a paediatrician with allergy/immunology/rheumatology expertise and prior review. The weight-tiered doses include both regimens studied here (300 mg every 4 weeks for 15 to under 30 kg; 200 mg every 2 weeks for 30 to under 60 kg). Only about 28%–35% of trial participants had prior systemic therapy, so the trial population differs from the NHI-eligible one.

Funding and conflicts: Sponsored by Sanofi and Regeneron; 10 authors are employees of the two companies, most academic authors have consulting or speaker ties to them, and medical writing was sponsor-funded.

2. AAD guideline: diagnostic testing in adults with presumed refractory AD

Evidence: Preliminary (AAD guideline issued as a GRADE Good Practice Statement; the literature search found no direct evidence, so it rests on indirect evidence and expert consensus)

Source: Sidbury R, et al. Guidelines for diagnostic testing in adults with presumed atopic dermatitis refractory to treatment. J Am Acad Dermatol. 2026;95(5):1370.e1-1370.e14. doi:10.1016/j.jaad.2026.07.039

Design and population: A multidisciplinary workgroup ran a scoping search in July 2024 (updated April 2025) and issued a single Good Practice Statement: for adults with suspected AD that does not respond to management, consider further workup. The precondition is confirming that treatment is guideline-optimized and that the patient is adherent (for example, irregular use driven by steroid phobia). Data cited by the guideline suggest that more than 20% of adults with refractory AD on systemic medication may be misdiagnosed or have a coexisting skin disease.

Main results: Key tests and numbers in the guideline:

  • Patch testing: consider it for adult-onset disease with atypical distribution (hands, face, eyelids), disease unresponsive to or worsening despite standard therapy, or new/worsening facial dermatitis; use an expanded series, ideally before systemic therapy, although it can be done during therapy with dose or timing adjustments. Reported overlap between AD and allergic contact dermatitis ranges from 7% to 91%.
  • Skin biopsy: one or more biopsies may be indicated for patches, plaques, papules or tumours, or erythroderma with enlarged lymph nodes, to look for cutaneous T-cell lymphoma (CTCL); repeated biopsies, immunohistochemistry or clonality studies may be needed. In the literature, 1–4 biopsies (median 2) were needed to confirm an alternative diagnosis. Blistering should prompt consideration of biopsy, including direct immunofluorescence.
  • Skin scraping: sensitivity is 43%–90% (median 45%) for scabies and 59%–91% for dermatophytes, so sample several sites; watch for tinea incognito, which improves at first and then worsens on steroids.
  • Delay to best management: median 6 months (3–12) for ACD; median 24 months (8–144) for CTCL, including a median of 6 months (1–96) on systemic therapy for presumed AD.

Limitations: No direct evidence and no data on diagnostic yield or predictive values in refractory AD; "non-response" has no operational definition or assessment time point; skin prick testing was not included; delay estimates come from the authors' review of the misdiagnosis literature, the main text does not state the study types (they are in supplemental tables not included in the PDF), and published cases may skew toward severe presentations.

Clinical interpretation: The conclusion is not new; its value is in making diagnostic reassessment formal guidance, with a table of differentials and tests. In adults responding poorly to dupilumab or a JAK inhibitor, ask three questions before switching to a second advanced therapy: is the treatment really being used as prescribed, did the disease start in adulthood or old age without an atopic history, and has the morphology or distribution changed? Any red flag warrants biopsy (several sites or repeated over time if needed), patch testing and KOH. A research letter in the same issue adds that TCS vehicles can themselves contain allergens: of 358 US prescription TCS, 77.7% contained at least one ACDS core allergen, most often propylene glycol (56.4%), and ointments carried fewer (0.78 per product) than creams (2.93). Taiwanese products may differ, but this is worth considering when a patient worsens on treatment.

Taiwan applicability: Under NHI §13.17, EASI counts only red eczematous areas, not simple dryness, scaling or excoriations; patients previously approved for a psoriasis biologic may apply only two years after psoriasis has resolved, or after a biopsy excludes psoriasis and a dermatologist confirms the diagnosis. The NHI rules themselves therefore demand diagnostic accuracy. Available patch-test series vary between hospitals; arrange testing or referral according to local feasibility.

Funding and conflicts: Funded entirely by internal AAD funds; several authors have consulting, speaker or research ties to industry, and some declared none.

Further reading: Contact dermatitis and patch testing, Cutaneous T-cell lymphoma, Topical steroid guide, Fitzpatrick Ch.24 Allergic contact dermatitis, Fitzpatrick Ch.119 Cutaneous lymphoma

3. A dual Th17/type 2 transcriptomic endotype in Taiwanese psoriasis (NTUH group)

Evidence: Preliminary (cross-sectional transcriptomic study; skin RNA sequencing in only 11 patients and 9 healthy controls)

Source: Chen CH, et al. Uncovering a dual T helper 17/type 2 transcriptomic endotype in psoriasis. J Am Acad Dermatol. 2026;95(5):1335-1343. doi:10.1016/j.jaad.2026.06.131

Design and population: National Taiwan University Hospital enrolled 50 patients with plaque psoriasis (37 men, 13 women; mean age 42.4 ± 14.3 years) and 9 healthy volunteers. Paired 4-mm truncal lesional and non-lesional biopsies from 11 patients underwent bulk RNA sequencing (differential expression: FDR-adjusted P < .05 and |log2 fold change| > 2), and 59 blood samples were run on a 65-plex cytokine panel. Correlations with PASI were post hoc and exploratory; Th2 genes were also compared with the Western public dataset GSE54456 (92 lesional, 82 normal samples).

Main results:

  • Lesional vs healthy skin showed 4694 differentially expressed genes (1874 up, 2820 down); IL-23A (log2FC 3.4) and IL-17A (5.5) were up, IL-36G rose by 5.5 (P = 1.1×10−55, the most significant among the genes quoted in the text), and the whole IL-36 family was elevated.
  • Type 2-related: IL-4R (log2FC 2.0), CCL17 (2.0), TSLP, OX40 and STAT6 were up; barrier genes KRT77 (−3.2), GJB4 (−3.5), FLG2 and CLDN1 were down. Compared with GSE54456, lesional up-regulation of IL4R, STAT6, GATA3 and CCL13 was significantly greater in the Taiwanese cohort.
  • Negative correlations with PASI (n = 11): IL-36RN r = −0.70 (P = .02), amphiregulin (AREG) −0.66 (P = .03), corneodesmosin (CDSN) −0.66 (P = .03); AREG plus CDSN R = −0.89 (P = .002). Blood Th2-related chemokines such as CCL11 and CCL26 were also higher; the authors state that IgE and eosinophils were not raised but give no figures.

Limitations: Very small, single-ethnicity sample; prior systemic treatment is not described. The "type 2" signal rests mainly on receptor, chemokine and transcription-factor mRNA; up-regulation of IL-4/IL-13 transcripts themselves is not reported and there is no tissue protein validation. AREG is also a keratinocyte-derived EGFR ligand, so calling it a Th2 marker needs caution. Two-marker correlations in 11 patients are prone to overfitting, and cross-dataset comparisons carry platform and batch effects. There are internal inconsistencies: 828 DEGs are reported for non-lesional vs healthy skin, yet 20 up plus 525 down do not add up; IL-4R and CCL17 have log2FC of exactly 2.0, which does not strictly meet the "> 2" threshold.

Clinical interpretation: These are rare whole-transcriptome data from Taiwanese psoriatic lesions; the strong IL-36 activation and barrier-gene loss are the more robust findings, while the "dual endotype" is a hypothesis. The authors' suggestion that type 2-targeted drugs might help IL-17 non-responders was not tested; risk in the opposite direction is already documented, for example in a 2026 systematic review devoted to dupilumab-induced psoriasis and psoriasiform eruptions. In practice, when a psoriasis patient develops eczematous lesions or responds poorly to IL-17/IL-23 blockade, biopsy to reconfirm the diagnosis and look for overlap rather than switching straight to a type 2 agent. The next useful step is a larger Taiwanese cohort sampled before and after treatment to test whether this signal predicts response.

Taiwan applicability: The study population is Taiwanese. NHI rules for dupilumab (§13.17) cover AD only; patients previously approved for a psoriasis biologic can apply for AD therapy only two years after psoriasis has resolved, or after a biopsy excludes psoriasis and a dermatologist confirms the diagnosis, which matters in psoriasis–eczema overlap cases.

Funding and conflicts: Funded by Taiwan's National Science and Technology Council, National Taiwan University Hospital, National Taiwan University and the E-Da Health System–NTUH joint research programme; no conflicts declared.

Further reading: Psoriasis overview, Systemic treatment of psoriasis, Fitzpatrick Ch.28 Psoriasis

4. Dupilumab vs JAK inhibitors in real-world AD: reduced-dose JAK inhibitors underperform

Evidence: Preliminary (research letter; retrospective cohort from the Chinese multicentre CORNERSTONE registry with propensity score matching; the dose-reduced group had only 10–31 patients per time point)

Source: Wang R, et al. Comparative effectiveness of dupilumab and JAK inhibitors in atopic dermatitis: dose reduction compromises efficacy of JAK inhibitors—the CORNERSTONE study. J Am Acad Dermatol. 2026;95(5):1483-1485. doi:10.1016/j.jaad.2026.07.024

Design and population: In all, 7871 patients with AD started dupilumab, abrocitinib 100 mg/day or upadacitinib 15 mg/day between August 2021 and December 2025 (6965 dupilumab, 906 JAK inhibitor). The paper states that 772 patients were included after matching and lists 439 abrocitinib and 333 upadacitinib users in parentheses (which alone sum to 772); the matched dupilumab number is not given separately. Dose reduction meant 2 days on/1 day off, alternate-day or as-needed dosing. Outcomes were EASI-75, EASI-90 and a ≥4-point PP-NRS reduction (PP-NRS4) to week 12.

Main results: The matched primary results appear only in the supplement. The main-text table (patients without and with dose reduction) at week 12 is shown below; in addition, week-2 EASI-75 was 51.4% with dupilumab vs 29.0% with standard-dose JAK inhibitors (P < .001), while PP-NRS4 favoured JAK inhibitors from week 2 to week 10 (week 8: 68.9% vs 41.2%, P < .001).

Limitations: The dose-reduced group was tiny, the paper does not say whether it was matched, nor when or why these patients reduced their dose (it only notes that safety, cost or good itch control are common reasons), so confounding by indication and reverse causation are serious concerns. Denominators vary widely by week. At week 2, dupilumab EASI-90 (48.6%) was almost equal to EASI-75 (51.4%), an unusual pattern that suggests selective follow-up or recording bias. The text says dupilumab had higher EASI-90 and lower PP-NRS4 at week 12, but neither difference is significant in the main-text table, and the primary analysis cannot be checked from the main text.

Clinical interpretation: Faster itch relief with JAK inhibitors and similar 12-week skin responses for both drug classes match general experience, but these data are not strong enough to change drug choice. The signal that dose reduction lowers effectiveness is plausible and consistent with faster relapse after stopping JAK inhibitors, yet the evidence is weak, and the intermittent, alternate-day and as-needed schedules used are not regimens in the NHI rules. If exposure needs to be reduced for safety or other reasons, do so after stable control, consider adjustment within approved dosing, and monitor closely; patients should not switch themselves to as-needed dosing.

Taiwan applicability: NHI §13.17.1 allows upadacitinib 15 mg daily (EASI 16–20) or 30 mg daily (age ≥18 and EASI ≥20) and abrocitinib 100 mg or 200 mg once daily; the study's "standard dose" corresponds to the lower NHI tier. Only one of upadacitinib or abrocitinib may be used, switching only for intolerable adverse effects, and neither may be combined with cyclosporin. Treatment is paused when EASI ≤16 after 1 year of dupilumab or 2 years of a JAK inhibitor; the rules contain no intermittent dosing schedules.

Funding and conflicts: Supported by the National Natural Science Foundation of China and Peking University First Hospital research funding; no conflicts declared; generative AI was used only for language editing.

Further reading: Systemic treatment of AD, Dupilumab stopping, tapering and maintenance, Fitzpatrick Ch.22 Atopic dermatitis

5. Dupilumab for prurigo nodularis: 52-week real-world data from the BioDay registry

Evidence: Preliminary (prospective multicentre Dutch registry; single arm, no control; n = 69)

Source: van der Gang LF, et al. Dupilumab in prurigo nodularis: 52-week effectiveness and safety from the prospective multicenter BioDay registry. J Am Acad Dermatol. 2026;95(5):1505-1508. doi:10.1016/j.jaad.2026.07.046

Design and population: 69 adults with prurigo nodularis (PN) who started dupilumab between February 2023 and February 2025: mean age 60.6 ± 17.6 years, disease duration 12.6 ± 14.2 years; 42.0% had AD, 75.4% had prior systemic immunomodulators, 47.8% had ≥2 prior systemic therapies, 50.7% had cardiovascular disease and 23.2% diabetes. Baseline PP-NRS was 8.2 ± 1.6. In all, 49.3% would not have met phase 3 PRIME trial eligibility. Missing data were multiply imputed, and most discontinuations were counted as non-response.

Main results: Patient-reported symptoms improved quickly, whereas IGA PN-S improved gradually up to week 52; effectiveness did not differ by atopic comorbidity or AD status.

Ocular surface disease counts are inconsistent: 14 patients in total, but 5 with and 8 without AD.

Clinical interpretation: This older, comorbid, heavily pretreated cohort resembles difficult clinic PN more closely than the PRIME trial population. Three points stand out: itch usually improves faster than nodules; week-52 results exceeded the 24-week PRIME figures (a cross-study comparison with different handling of missing data and discontinuations), suggesting that the full effect may take longer to appear, so non-response should not be called too early; complete remission occurred in only about one in six, so agree on control, not clearance, as the goal before starting; and ocular surface disease and arthralgia were more frequent than in PRIME, so ask about them and involve ophthalmology when needed. Benefit regardless of atopic background supports a role for the IL-4/IL-13 pathway in PN.

Taiwan applicability: According to a 2025 Taiwanese package-insert excerpt, dupilumab (Dupixent) is labelled for prurigo nodularis, but NHI rules cover AD only, so PN is not reimbursed. Patients with coexisting AD still have to meet §13.17 criteria on the basis of their AD.

Funding and conflicts: Investigator-initiated and sponsored by Sanofi-Genzyme/Regeneron; the BioDay registry has been or is sponsored by AbbVie, Eli Lilly, LEO Pharma, Pfizer and Sanofi-Genzyme/Regeneron. The authors state the sponsors had no role in design, analysis or writing; several authors have consulting or speaker ties to these companies.

6. Real-world JAK inhibitors in alopecia areata: baricitinib in 330 Chinese patients including children, and ritlecitinib in adolescents

Evidence: Preliminary (two single-centre, retrospective, single-arm research letters without a control group)

Source: Ran X, et al. Long-term real-world outcomes and tolerability of baricitinib in alopecia areata, totalis, and universalis. J Am Acad Dermatol. 2026;95(5):1490-1493. doi:10.1016/j.jaad.2026.07.031; Starace M, et al. Real-world effectiveness and safety of ritlecitinib in adolescents with severe alopecia areata. J Am Acad Dermatol. 2026;95(5):1460-1461. doi:10.1016/j.jaad.2026.07.009

Design and population: The baricitinib study included 330 consecutive patients with alopecia areata (AA) treated at West China Hospital between August 2020 and June 2024, aged 2–63 years; 135 (40.9%) were ≤17 years old, and 10 had alopecia totalis (AT) and 50 alopecia universalis (AU). The dose (2 or 4 mg/day) was at the clinician's discretion (321 with dose data: 234 on 4 mg, 87 on 2 mg), and conventional therapies were allowed alongside; baseline Severity of Alopecia Tool (SALT) scores were recorded for only 193 patients. The primary endpoint was SALT ≤20, with nonresponder imputation (NRI) for missing data. The ritlecitinib study included 49 adolescents (12–17 years) in Bologna, Italy, with SALT ≥50 on 50 mg daily, assessed at weeks 24 and 36 for scalp, eyebrows, eyelashes, nails and trichoscopic features.

Main results: In the ritlecitinib cohort, mean SALT fell from 81.2 at baseline to 59.5 at week 24 and 44.7 at week 36; at week 24, eyebrows and eyelashes improved in 34.7% and 40.8%, and nails improved in 33.3% of those affected. On trichoscopy, short regrowing hairs rose from 22.4% to 67.3% and black dots fell from 28.6% to 12.2%. In the baricitinib cohort, vellus regrowth was visible on trichoscopy as early as 4–5 weeks.

Limitations: Neither study had a control group; subgroup comparisons are descriptive, and the ritlecitinib letter reports no P values or confidence intervals. The baricitinib study does not state when the primary endpoint was assessed. If NRI had counted patients lost to follow-up as non-responders at a fixed time point, at most 199 of 321 patients (321 minus 122 lost) could have responded, which is barely compatible with 197 responders (our calculation); the 61.4% more likely means "reached SALT ≤20 at any time during follow-up", which overstates sustained response. Its adverse-event rates are strikingly low (acne 0.6% versus 10.2% in the ritlecitinib cohort), which may indicate under-recording in retrospective charts. The letter is also internally inconsistent: Figure 1 lists 49 patients aged 12–17 years but the age-specific outcomes in Table I use 47, and the ethics statement cites a waiver of consent while the text reports written consent.

Clinical interpretation: Both letters add data in groups that randomised trials cover less well, but they do not change the level of evidence. The ritlecitinib data match the currently approved population and dose; the practical message is the time course: about 40% reached SALT ≤20 by week 36, but only 16.7% of AT/AU patients did (a descriptive comparison without statistical testing), suggesting that patients with extensive disease (AT/AU) may need longer to respond, which can be explained to patients in advance. The 61.4% in the baricitinib cohort should be read as an optimistic upper bound; despite the large paediatric subgroup, laboratory monitoring and adverse-event capture are not described, so the data do not support routine use in children. The better response with shorter disease duration is untested and possibly confounded by severity; it can inform discussions about timing but is not established. The 31 patients switched to tofacitinib reflect real-world switching, but post-switch outcomes were not reported.

Applicability in Taiwan: In Taiwan, baricitinib is approved for adults ≥18 years with severe AA (SALT >50%), so its use at ages 2–17 in this study would be off-label here; ritlecitinib is approved for severe AA from age 12 at 50 mg daily, matching the Italian cohort's population and dose; upadacitinib is also approved in Taiwan for severe AA from age 12; tofacitinib is off-label for AA. National Health Insurance (NHI) does not currently reimburse any JAK inhibitor for AA, so treatment is self-paid.

Funding and conflicts of interest: The baricitinib study reports no funding and no conflicts, but thanks a medical affairs staff member of Eli Lilly, the manufacturer of baricitinib, for "medical support", and states that AI was used for language editing. The ritlecitinib study reports no funding; senior author Piraccini has received consulting fees from companies including Pfizer, the manufacturer of ritlecitinib, and Eli Lilly.

7. Retinal abnormalities in alopecia areata: a systematic review and meta-analysis

Evidence: Preliminary (systematic review and meta-analysis of observational studies; definitions and methods of retinal assessment vary widely)

Source: Ameri K, et al. Retinal abnormalities in alopecia areata: a systematic review and meta-analysis. J Am Acad Dermatol. 2026;95(5):1448-1450. doi:10.1016/j.jaad.2026.06.137

Design and population: PRISMA-based review registered in PROSPERO (CRD420261362924). Ovid MEDLINE, Embase, Web of Science and Cochrane CENTRAL were searched to August 2025 for observational studies reporting retinal findings in people with and without AA; non-English studies were excluded. Risk ratios were pooled with a Mantel–Haenszel random-effects model. Nine studies with 145,615 people were included: 10,832 with AA (mean age 30.5 years) and 134,783 controls (mean age 36.0 years); the AA subtype was unspecified in 97.1%.

Main results:

  • At least one retinal abnormality: 214 patients with AA (2.0%) vs 940 controls (0.7%).
  • Meta-analysis (7 studies, 109,693 participants, 363 retinal findings): RR 3.88 (95% CI 2.20–6.87; P < .001; I² = 46.1%); sensitivity analyses were consistent and no major publication bias was found.
  • The findings most frequently reported in AA were retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) dysfunction on electro-oculography (34), pigmentary abnormalities (31) and retinal detachment (13); retinal vascular occlusion (11), drusen (11) and a heterogeneous "other retinopathy" group (63) were also recorded.
  • Of 7 studies assessing severity, 6 found no association with retinal abnormalities; the 2 studies assessing age or disease duration found none either.

Limitations: The authors acknowledge sparse reporting of functional (visual) outcomes, heterogeneous outcome definitions and methods, reliance on observational data, and no systematic capture of comorbidities. We would add that "retinal abnormality" lumps together RPE electrophysiology with peripheral changes that are common in the general population, such as lattice degeneration, paving-stone degeneration and white without pressure, whose clinical significance differs widely; studies that actively examined patients with AA are pooled with large code-based databases, so detection bias may inflate the relative risk; the 2 studies left out of the meta-analysis contributed 35,922 participants (our calculation; only 533 with AA), and the reason for their exclusion is not stated in the text; and excluding non-English studies may have missed Asian data.

Clinical interpretation: The relative risk is about fourfold, but absolute proportions are only 2.0% vs 0.7%, and the findings are a mixture (some, such as lattice degeneration, are common peripheral changes, while others, such as detachment or vascular occlusion, can threaten vision); 6 of 7 studies assessing severity found no association (one found higher prevalence with more extensive disease), and only 2 assessed age or duration, both without association. This does not support routine fundus examination for every patient with AA. A pragmatic approach is to ask about visual symptoms (reduced vision, field loss, floaters or flashes) and refer symptomatic patients to ophthalmology, urgently if retinal detachment is suspected. Shared collapse of immune privilege in the hair follicle and the eye remains a hypothesis, and the review did not analyse the effect of JAK inhibitors or other treatments.

Applicability in Taiwan: No drug approval or NHI reimbursement issue is involved. The countries and populations of the included studies are not listed in the main text (supplementary material only), so generalisability to Taiwanese patients cannot be judged.

10. Primary Pneumocystis pneumonia prophylaxis in patients receiving rituximab for immune-mediated skin disease: a meta-analysis

Evidence: Moderate (systematic review and meta-analysis of 22 studies, random-effects model; nonrandomized and randomized studies included; certainty of evidence rated low by the authors)

Source: Zhang SY, et al. Effect of primary prophylaxis for Pneumocystis pneumonia in patients on rituximab for immune-mediated dermatologic conditions. J Am Acad Dermatol. 2026;95(5):1453-1455. doi:10.1016/j.jaad.2026.07.002

Design and population: MEDLINE, EMBASE and Web of Science were searched on June 30, 2024 (PRISMA) for patients receiving rituximab for immune-mediated skin disease (keywords include autoimmune blistering disease, vasculitis and lupus), comparing those with and without primary prophylaxis against Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PJP); trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (TMP-SMX) was the most common agent. Risk of bias was assessed with ROBINS-I and RoB 2. The main text does not give the total sample size; back-calculation from the event counts and percentages suggests roughly 2,800 patients (our calculation).

Main results:

  • 44.4% of patients received prophylaxis. PJP incidence was 1.98% (31 cases) without and 0.32% (4 cases) with prophylaxis; risk ratio (RR) 0.156 (95% CI 0.05–0.46; P = .0031), an 84% relative risk reduction, number needed to treat (NNT) 60. Heterogeneity I² = 37.1% (Cochran Q = 17.47, df = 11, P = .095).
  • PJP accounted for about 1.6% of deaths; the RR for PJP-related death was 0.40 (95% CI 0.02–9.21), not statistically significant.
  • Prophylaxis-related adverse events occurred in 7.45% and all resolved after stopping or switching; only 1 patient (TMP-SMX-related aseptic meningitis) required admission. Number needed to harm (NNH) 66; likelihood to harm/help ratio 0.91.

Design and population: Trager et al (Memorial Sloan Kettering) combined US FDA labels, a PubMed search (July–September 2025) and meetings of 4 Mohs surgeons and 2 medical oncologists (October 2025 to January 2026) into class-by-class perioperative recommendations for dermatologic surgery, each graded for level of evidence. Rachofsky et al (Albert Einstein/Montefiore dermatology and oncology) summarised, in a clinical review, how antibiotics, systemic corticosteroids, JAK inhibitors, dupilumab and other immunomodulators may affect the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), without describing a systematic search.

  • Trager et al: with dabrafenib plus trametinib, consider holding the MEK inhibitor 48 h preoperatively, resuming dabrafenib on day 1 and trametinib on day 7; EGFR inhibitors, oral corticosteroids (stress dosing generally unnecessary for minor procedures), hormonal therapy and KRAS/NTRK/ALK inhibitors can usually continue; chemotherapy and bortezomib call for avoiding count nadirs; with antibody-drug conjugates, elective surgery is delayed for severe neutropenia or active skin toxicity. Routine prophylactic antibiotics are not recommended.
  • Rachofsky et al, antibiotics: in a systematic review of 41,663 patients, any antibiotic exposure was associated with worse OS (HR 1.61, 95% CI 1.48–1.76) and progression-free survival (HR 1.45, 1.32–1.60), most strongly within 60 days before or after ICI initiation. Other studies cited by the authors link courses >14 days, fluoroquinolones and piperacillin-tazobactam to stronger associations.
  • Systemic corticosteroids: ICI trials generally excluded prednisone ≥10 mg/d; in a study of 13,086 patients, >5 mg/d was associated with roughly 20% and >75 mg/d with roughly 40% lower OS, and use for ≥7 days with roughly 35%; the effect was greatest within 1 month of ICI initiation (TR 0.49, 95% CI 0.44–0.54). Topical corticosteroids are regarded as low risk. Dupilumab: 53 patients treated for cutaneous immune-related adverse events (cirAEs) showed no survival difference versus two control groups.

Limitations: Neither is a systematic review. Trager et al reflect one cancer centre, with hold times inferred mostly from US labels and half-lives rather than dermatologic-surgery outcome data; there are several typographical issues (dabrafenib half-life "8 h, range 4.8–5.2", "levatinib" for lenvatinib, the units of the lenalidomide thresholds), and the cabozantinib advice (about 3 weeks) is shorter than the label it cites (at least 28 days); for lenalidomide, platelets <50,000 lead to postponement in the table but to consideration of transfusion in the text. Rachofsky et al rely largely on retrospective observational data and acknowledge that patients needing these drugs may simply be sicker; the progression-free survival HR for second-line immunosuppression, 1.34 (0.99–1.82), crosses 1.

Clinical interpretation: Most biopsies, excisions and Mohs procedures carry low bleeding and infection risk; the task is to recognise the few drugs that genuinely require coordination: antiangiogenic agents (bevacizumab, VEGFR-targeted drugs), MEK inhibitors, and haematologic drugs that cause cytopenias, for which a preoperative CBC is central. Trager et al do not advise routinely pausing ICIs for surgery; if anything, drugs dermatologists prescribe (antibiotics, systemic steroids) have been associated with worse cancer outcomes, although these data are mostly retrospective and causality is unresolved. Within the window around ICI initiation, before starting prolonged doxycycline for an acneiform eruption or bullous pemphigoid, or high-dose systemic steroids for a cirAE, consider topical therapy, shorter courses or alternatives and talk to oncology. These are expert opinions; holding and restarting cancer therapy should always be decided with oncology, and patients should not stop drugs on their own.

Applicability to Taiwan: NHI reimbursement rules for dupilumab cover atopic dermatitis only (§13.17.1, §13.17.2), so use for cirAEs falls outside reimbursement. For the other anticancer drugs and JAK inhibitors in this setting, follow the Taiwan FDA-approved indications and NHI rules; Trager et al rely on US FDA labels, which have not been checked item by item against Taiwanese labels.
