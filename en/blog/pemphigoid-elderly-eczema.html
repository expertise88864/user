Elderly “Eczema” That Won't Clear: Could It Be Pemphigoid Without Blisters?

An older adult's itchy rash that lasts for months is not always eczema. Pemphigoid can occur without blisters; a 2026 JAAD study shows the warning signs.

Key points in 30 seconds

Key points
  • Pemphigoid is the most common autoimmune blistering disease of older adults, but some patients never develop blisters. They have only intensely itchy red patches, bumps or scratch marks, which are easily mistaken for eczema.
  • A 2026 study compared 97 patients with blister-free pemphigoid and 291 patients with eczema. The pemphigoid patients averaged 71.7 years of age, their current flare had lasted 30.1 months on average (4.9 months for eczema), the rash often covered more than 30% of the body, and neurological illness was more common.
  • Appearance alone cannot tell them apart: red patches, bumps and severe itch were about equally common in both groups. Diagnosis relies on a skin biopsy with direct immunofluorescence; blood antibody tests help, but a negative blood test does not rule it out.
  • In this study, blister-free pemphigoid treated with systemic corticosteroids alone achieved disease control much less often than eczema (small samples). When response is unusually poor, simply intensifying eczema treatment may not help; getting the diagnosis right comes first.
  • If an older person's eczema has not improved after several weeks of proper treatment, keeps spreading, or starts to blister or affect the mouth, see a dermatologist and bring a complete medication list. Do not stop or change prescribed medicines on your own.

What is pemphigoid, and how is it different from eczema?

Bullous pemphigoid (BP) is an autoimmune skin disease. The immune system mistakenly targets the structures that anchor the outer skin layer (epidermis) to the layer beneath (dermis) and produces antibodies against them. The result is intense itch and, in more severe cases, separation of the two layers, forming tense blisters [3]. It is the most common autoimmune blistering disease of the elderly and mostly affects older adults [5][6].

Pemphigoid is not an infection. It cannot spread to family members, and it is not caused by poor hygiene.

The key point is that not every patient with pemphigoid develops blisters. The 2022 European guideline notes that some patients have only scratch marks, prurigo-like lesions, or eczema-like or hive-like red patches [5]. When blisters are absent, the condition is called nonbullous pemphigoid (NBP); in this article we call it the "blister-free" form. In data from a Dutch referral centre, 74 of 343 patients with pemphigoid (21.6%, about 1 in 5) had no blisters at presentation [7].

Because it looks so much like eczema, many of these patients are treated for eczema or "itchy elderly skin" for a long time. A systematic review of published cases found an average delay of 22.6 months from onset to diagnosis [3].

Which older adults should raise suspicion?

Pemphigoid has no single cause, but studies show it is more common in the following groups:

  • Older age: in the new study, the average age at diagnosis was 71.7 years [1]; in an earlier review of published cases it was 74.9 years [3].
  • Neurological illness, such as dementia, Parkinson disease or stroke. A meta-analysis of 14 studies found these conditions were clearly more common in people with pemphigoid [8], and a French case-control study linked major cognitive impairment, being bedridden and Parkinson disease with pemphigoid [9].
  • Certain medicines: the most discussed are DPP-4 inhibitors, a class of oral diabetes drugs (names usually end in "-gliptin"). An Israeli study found that people with diabetes taking these drugs were more likely to develop pemphigoid [10], and a French study linked long-term use of spironolactone (a diuretic) and some other drugs with pemphigoid [9]. These are associations: taking such a drug does not mean pemphigoid will occur, and it is not a reason to stop the medicine yourself.
  • Long-standing itch in older adults who depend on carers: people with limited mobility or difficulty communicating may not be able to say where they itch; families may notice only scratch marks, poor sleep or thickened skin.

Most itchy skin in older adults is still due to dryness, eczema or other common causes. The points above are clues that prompt doctors to think one step further, not a reason for alarm.

The new study: which clues point to pemphigoid?

The November 2026 issue of the Journal of the American Academy of Dermatology (JAAD) published a study from Shandong, China [1]. The team reviewed medical records from 2015 to 2025, identified 97 patients with confirmed blister-free pemphigoid, and compared them with 291 eczema patients matched for age and sex (a 1-to-3 ratio).

The table shows two things. First, the skin looks almost the same: red patches, bumps and severe itch were equally common. Second, the real differences lie in the overall picture: a long course, extensive skin involvement and neurological or metabolic illness make pemphigoid more likely. The main text does not specify which conditions counted as metabolic.

When these factors were analysed together, neurological illness had the highest odds ratio (roughly, a measure of relative likelihood), about 12.6; involvement of more than 30% of the body about 3.7; and metabolic illness about 2.6. In contrast, people who already had another autoimmune or inflammatory disease were less likely to have pemphigoid. The team turned these factors into a scoring chart (nomogram), which separated the two groups well in this sample (area under the curve 0.881) [1].

The study also compared patients treated with systemic corticosteroids alone. At 2 weeks, disease control was reached in 16.7% of blister-free pemphigoid patients versus 95.7% of eczema patients; at 4 weeks, 37.5% versus 100% [1]. The groups were small (24 and 23 patients), but the message is clear: when "eczema" responds unusually poorly to treatment that normally works, the diagnosis should be revisited.

Limitations: this was a retrospective study from one dermatology hospital system in Shandong; the letter does not describe validation of the scoring chart at other hospitals, and it is not a routine tool in Taiwan. Its value is in reminding doctors when to arrange a biopsy; it is not meant for families to use at home.

How is it diagnosed? Biopsy, immunofluorescence and blood tests

Pemphigoid cannot be confirmed by looking at the skin, especially the blister-free form. Three main tests are used:

  • Skin biopsy for routine pathology: a small piece of skin is taken under local anaesthesia. In the blister-free form, the findings are often non-specific and look like ordinary dermatitis [3][4], so the next test is usually needed as well.
  • Direct immunofluorescence (DIF): a second small skin sample is specially stained to look for antibody deposits along the skin's basement membrane. It is the most sensitive test: 88.3% in a large Dutch diagnostic study [7], and positive in 88.5% of the blister-free patients tested in the new study [1].
  • Blood antibody tests: BP180 and BP230 antibodies and indirect immunofluorescence. Blood tests are convenient, but in the new study only 63.5% of blister-free patients were positive for BP180 antibodies and 52.9% for BP230 antibodies [1], so a negative result does not rule pemphigoid out.

For what to expect from a skin biopsy and how to care for the wound, see our guide to skin biopsy and excision. These special tests are not available at every clinic and are usually arranged by a dermatologist.

The doctor will also rule out other conditions that mimic eczema, such as scabies (especially in care-home residents), fungal infection, drug eruptions, contact dermatitis or skin lymphoma [2]. See our articles on severe scabies, contact dermatitis and cutaneous T-cell lymphoma.

Treatment overview and care at home

Once diagnosed, treatment depends on how widespread the disease is, the person's general health and other illnesses. The 2022 European guideline names high-potency topical corticosteroids as the mainstay whenever possible, with oral corticosteroids as an alternative; if steroids are unsuitable or ineffective, dermatologists may consider other immune-modulating drugs or biologics [5].

Why start with strong creams? In a French randomised trial of 341 patients, among the 188 with extensive disease, one-year survival was 76% with whole-body potent topical corticosteroids versus 58% with oral corticosteroids, and severe complications were less frequent (29% versus 54%) [6]. Older adults often already have diabetes, osteoporosis or a higher infection risk, so long-term high-dose oral steroids carry a real burden.

For potency classes, amounts and side effects, see our topical corticosteroid guide. Pemphigoid often needs stronger creams in larger amounts than ordinary eczema. Apply them exactly as instructed, and do not cut down or stop on your own out of fear of steroids.

As for biologics, dupilumab was approved by the US FDA for adults with bullous pemphigoid in June 2025. For Taiwan, please refer to the indications approved by the Taiwan FDA; National Health Insurance does not currently reimburse it for pemphigoid. Whether it is suitable must be assessed by a dermatologist.

How families can help at home

  • Keep baths or showers short and lukewarm, and apply a gentle moisturiser afterwards.
  • Keep nails short and smooth; cotton gloves at night can reduce scratch damage and infection.
  • If blisters appear, do not pop them or apply home remedies. Keep the skin clean and return to the doctor promptly.
  • Prepare a complete list of medicines (including long-term prescriptions and supplements) for the dermatologist. Discuss any change to prescription drugs with the prescribing doctor.
  • For older adults taking oral steroids, watch blood sugar, blood pressure, infections and falls, and keep follow-up appointments.

When should you see a dermatologist?

Arrange a dermatology assessment if any of the following apply:

  • The rash was diagnosed as eczema but has not improved after several weeks of treatment used as directed, or keeps coming back.
  • The rash or scratch marks have spread over a large area of the body or have lasted for several months.
  • Blisters appear (especially tense ones that do not break easily), the skin weeps or erodes, or sores develop in the mouth.
  • The person has dementia, Parkinson disease, stroke or another neurological condition and has had long-standing widespread itch or poor sleep.
  • Widespread itch or rash started after a new medicine, such as a diabetes drug.
  • Broken skin becomes red, hot, painful or pus-filled, or there is fever: this may be an infection and needs prompt medical care.

For more on eczema itself, see our complete guide to atopic dermatitis.

Frequently asked questions

Is pemphigoid contagious?

No. It is an immune-system disorder, not a bacterial or viral infection, so living with or caring for the person carries no risk. However, scabies can also cause widespread itch in older adults and is contagious, so doctors sometimes check for scabies at the same time [2].

Without blisters now, will blisters definitely appear later?

Not necessarily. In a review of published cases, only 9.8% developed blisters during follow-up [3]; in another Dutch study of 69 patients the figure was 17% [4]. So there is no need to wait for blisters; testing should be arranged when pemphigoid is suspected.

If the BP180 blood test is negative, does that rule out pemphigoid?

No. In the new study, only about six in ten blister-free patients had positive BP180 antibodies [1]. Skin biopsy with direct immunofluorescence remains a key diagnostic test [7].

My parent takes a DPP-4 inhibitor for diabetes. Should it be stopped?

Do not stop it yourself. Studies link this drug class with pemphigoid [10], but whether to switch should be decided jointly by the dermatologist and the doctor managing the diabetes, based on the diagnosis and blood sugar control. Stopping on your own and losing glucose control also carries risk.

How well does treatment work, and for how long?

In the large French trial, most patients with extensive disease were controlled by 3 weeks of treatment [6], after which doctors taper the medicines gradually. Pemphigoid can relapse, however, so regular follow-up is needed; the length of treatment varies from person to person.

The eczema cream hasn't worked for months. Is it just not strong enough?

Possibly, or the amount or technique may be inadequate. But if it has been used correctly for some time without improvement, the diagnosis should be reconsidered [2]; it is also not advisable to switch to stronger creams or seek steroid injections or tablets on your own.

For healthcare professionals

For healthcare professionals

Clinical clues

  • Age: typically elderly; mean age at diagnosis of NBP in this study was 71.7 years (range 39–93) [1].
  • Pruritus: all NBP patients in this study had pruritus, severe in 68.0% [1]; some have itch and excoriations without primary lesions (22% in the Lamberts 2019 series) [4].
  • Course and extent: current flare 30.1 vs 4.9 months on average; BSA over 30% in 58.8% vs 22.0% [1].
  • Comorbidities: neurological OR 12.64, metabolic OR 2.59, pre-existing autoimmune/inflammatory disease OR 0.15 [1]. In meta-analysis, BP is associated with stroke, Parkinson disease, dementia, epilepsy and multiple sclerosis [8].
  • Drug history: DPP-4 inhibitors (Israeli case-control study, overall adjusted OR 3.2, 95% CI 1.9–5.4; vildagliptin 10.7, linagliptin 6.7) [10]; chronic spironolactone or aliphatic phenothiazine use is also associated [9]. Record when each drug was started.
  • Laboratory: peripheral eosinophilia in 46.5% (40/86) of NBP patients in this study [1]; supportive, but a normal count does not exclude NBP.

Differential diagnosis

  • Eczema / atopic dermatitis: for adults with presumed atopic dermatitis that does not respond to optimised treatment, the 2026 AAD Good Practice Statement recommends considering further work-up and lists biopsy with histopathology plus DIF for autoimmune blistering disease [2].
  • Scabies: acute onset, papules in web spaces, axillae, groin or genitals, nocturnal itch, itchy contacts, common in care-home residents; a single scraping has limited sensitivity, so sample several sites [2].
  • Drug eruption: sudden onset, flexural sparing, blisters or pustules, photosensitivity [2]. Drug-associated BP (e.g. with DPP-4 inhibitors) also exists [10]; biopsy with DIF separates the two.
  • Others: contact dermatitis (patch testing), cutaneous T-cell lymphoma (multiple biopsies may be needed), prurigo nodularis, fungal infection (KOH microscopy) [2].

Initial work-up and referral

In primary care: a full drug history (with start dates), BSA and itch assessment, CBC with differential, and scrapings for scabies or fungus where relevant. Diagnosis requires a skin biopsy (H&E) plus a separate specimen for DIF [2], with serum tests (IIF, BP180/BP230 ELISA) as supplements [7]. The 1,125-patient diagnostic study by Meijer et al recommends both DIF and IIF on salt-split skin; BP180 NC16A ELISA added no diagnostic value at initial diagnosis in multivariable analysis and is better suited to monitoring disease activity [7].

Consider dermatology referral when:

  • Elderly "eczema" has not improved after several weeks of adequate, correctly applied topical corticosteroids and emollients with confirmed adherence.
  • The course has lasted months, BSA exceeds 30%, or neurological disease is present [1].
  • Blisters, erosions or oral mucosal lesions develop.
  • New generalised itch or rash develops while on a DPP-4 inhibitor [10].
  • Before starting systemic corticosteroids, immunosuppressants or biologics [2].

What not to do

  • Prolonged or repeated oral or injected corticosteroids without a diagnosis. In this study, NBP had much lower disease control on systemic corticosteroid monotherapy (2 weeks 16.7% vs 95.7%; 4 weeks 37.5% vs 100%; P < .001) [1], and long-term systemic steroids carry a heavy complication burden in older adults [6].
  • Excluding the diagnosis on a negative BP180/BP230 ELISA: positivity in NBP was only 63.5% and 52.9%, with both positive in 37.6% [1], compared with a combined serological yield above 90% reported for classic BP [1].
  • Escalating refractory elderly "eczema" to systemic therapy before considering NBP and other differentials (our recommendation).
  • Telling patients to stop a DPP-4 inhibitor before diagnosis; after diagnosis, withdrawal should be coordinated with the diabetes prescriber and an alternative glucose plan arranged [10].

Treatment principles (primary-care level)

EADV 2022 S2k guideline: high-potency topical corticosteroids are the mainstay whenever possible, with oral corticosteroids as an alternative; when steroids are contraindicated or ineffective, dermatologists may use methotrexate, azathioprine, mycophenolate mofetil and similar agents; the role of doxycycline and dapsone is controversial; B-cell depletion or IVIG may be considered for refractory disease; omalizumab and dupilumab have shown promising results [5]. In the 2002 Joly RCT, topical clobetasol propionate outperformed oral prednisone in extensive BP (n = 188) for 1-year survival (76% vs 58%) and severe complications (29% vs 54%) [6].

The primary-care role: ensure topical therapy is used in adequate amounts and applied correctly (often by family members or carers), monitor skin care and infection, manage comorbidities such as glucose and bone health, and coordinate medication with dermatology. Dupilumab was approved by the US FDA for adult BP in June 2025; in Taiwan, refer to the Taiwan FDA-approved indications, and NHI does not reimburse it for BP. In this study only 7 NBP patients received dupilumab monotherapy, with complete remission at 6 months in 42.9% (vs 61.1% for eczema, not statistically significant) [1]; the data are limited.

Key study figures and limitations

  • Design: retrospective case-control study from one dermatology hospital system in Shandong, China: 97 NBP patients vs 291 age- and sex-matched eczema controls (2015–2025) [1].
  • Immunological tests: DIF positive 88.5% (77/87), IIF 56.1% (37/66), BP180 63.5% (54/85), BP230 52.9% (45/85), both 37.6% (32/85) [1]. If both ELISAs were run on the same 85 patients, at least one was positive in 67/85 (about 78.8%, our calculation), meaning roughly one in five was negative on both.
  • Multivariable model: flare duration OR 1.14 (unit not stated in the main text), BSA over 30% OR 3.70, neurological comorbidity 12.64, metabolic comorbidity 2.59, autoimmune/inflammatory comorbidity 0.15; 95% CIs are not given in the main text [1].
  • Nomogram: AUC 0.881, with a QR-code calculator [1]. The letter describes no internal or external validation, so the AUC may be optimistic; and because the case-control design fixes NBP at one quarter of the sample, its output probabilities cannot be read as pre-test probabilities for clinic patients (our interpretation).
  • Other limitations: the main text does not state whether all eczema controls had DIF, so unrecognised NBP may be among them; definitions of "metabolic comorbidity" and "disease control" and the steroid doses are in the supplement or not reported; treatment comparisons are very small (steroids 24 vs 23; dupilumab 7 vs 36). The table lists IgE positivity as 46/68 (67.4%), which does not match the fraction, so we do not use it.
  • Clinical interpretation: the findings agree with the existing literature (a review of published cases and a Dutch series of 69 patients: mean diagnostic delay of 22.6 and 29 months, mostly non-specific histopathology, reliance on immunofluorescence) [3][4], so the direction is plausible; but the nomogram needs external validation before use in Taiwanese clinics. It is best used as a prompt for when to arrange biopsy with DIF, not as a substitute for it.

For a fuller methodological appraisal, see our JAAD November 2026 literature review; for pathology, immunofluorescence and board-exam points on BP, see exam notes: bullous pemphigoid.
