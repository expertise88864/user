Atopic Dermatitis That Won't Improve: Could It Be Something Else? Testing for Adults With Stubborn Eczema

Adult eczema not improving despite proper treatment? It may be contact allergy, scabies, tinea or, rarely, lymphoma. What the 2026 AAD guideline advises.

Key points in 30 seconds

Key points
  • When adults do not improve despite guideline-based treatment taken as directed, the 2026 American Academy of Dermatology (AAD) guideline recommends considering further workup; this is a Good Practice Statement based on indirect evidence and expert consensus [1].
  • Before doubting the diagnosis, check that treatment is adequate and actually being used; fear of steroid side effects, which can lead to under-use, is one barrier to adherence [1].
  • Common look-alikes or add-on problems: allergic contact dermatitis (including allergy to skincare or to the treatment cream itself), scabies, fungal infection, drug eruptions, psoriasis and the uncommon but important cutaneous T-cell lymphoma [1][2].
  • Three key tests: patch testing for contact allergens, skin scraping for scabies mites or fungus, and skin biopsy for tissue diagnosis; more than one biopsy may be needed if lymphoma is suspected [1].
  • A study of 358 US prescription topical corticosteroids found that 77.7% contained at least one common contact allergen; ointments contained fewer [2].
  • Bring every product you apply or take to your appointment, and do not stop or switch treatments on your own.

Why "treatment-resistant atopic dermatitis" deserves a second look

Atopic dermatitis (AD) is a chronic, itchy, relapsing inflammatory skin disease. Many adults can control it with standard topical or systemic treatment, but some keep struggling even when the treatment is right and they use it as directed [1]. In 2026 the American Academy of Dermatology (AAD) published a guideline specifically on how to re-evaluate adults whose presumed AD does not respond to treatment [1]. For a general overview, see our complete guide to atopic dermatitis.

According to data cited in the guideline, more than 20% of adults prescribed a systemic medication for refractory AD may have been misdiagnosed or have another skin condition hidden alongside AD [1]. Worldwide, 2.0% of adults have AD, and 25% of them report that it began in adulthood [1]. Adult "eczema" deserves extra thought: rash distribution and appearance in adults often differ from children, with less involvement of the elbow and knee creases, coin-shaped or prurigo-like bumps, and less personal or family history of allergy. Other skin diseases, infections, autoimmune conditions and skin cancers also become more common with age [1].

Delay has a cost. Reviewing the literature on misdiagnosis, the guideline estimated that the median delay to the best management was 6 months (range 3 to 12 months) for contact dermatitis and 24 months (range 8 to 144 months) for cutaneous T-cell lymphoma; in the lymphoma cases, the median time spent on systemic therapy for presumed AD was 6 months (range 1 to 96 months) [1]. These figures come from a literature review whose study types are not described in the main text, and they do not represent any one person's risk, but they show why it matters to revisit the diagnosis when treatment fails.

What else could it be, or what might be added on top?

There are two kinds of possibility: the condition was never AD, or a second problem has been added on top of AD [1].

Allergic contact dermatitis: even the treatment can be the allergen

Allergic contact dermatitis is a delayed allergic reaction to something that touches the skin. People with AD have a weaker skin barrier and apply moisturisers, medicated creams, cleansers and skincare every day; some also handle chemicals at work. The two conditions therefore often coexist, with published estimates of overlap ranging from 7% to 91% [1]. Clues include rash concentrated on the head and neck or hands and feet, new rash in previously unaffected areas, and disease that becomes harder to control on the usual regimen [1].

Notably, the treatment itself can be the allergen: the active steroid can cause allergy, and so can inactive ingredients such as preservatives, emulsifiers and fragrance [2]. Irritant contact dermatitis can also resemble or coexist with AD [1]. See our guide to contact dermatitis.

Scabies: steroids can make it worse

Scabies is an infestation by mites that burrow into the skin. It is intensely itchy, worse at night, and favours the finger webs, armpits, groin and genitals; people living with the patient often itch too. It is more common in older adults and care-home residents, and the severe form is called crusted scabies [1]. The guideline notes that scabies is the alternative diagnosis most often found by skin scraping, and that treating it as eczema with topical steroids can make the infestation worse [1]. Treatment is covered in our guide to severe scabies.

Fungal infection: tinea incognito, better at first and then worse

Dermatophyte infections (such as tinea corporis) and candida often affect skin folds or the feet; the classic look is a ring-shaped red patch with a raised, scaly border [1]. Topical steroids or other immunomodulating treatments can disguise the infection so it no longer looks like typical ringworm, a picture called tinea incognito [1]. The typical story: the rash seems to improve on a steroid at first, then keeps spreading; lesions are asymmetric or ring-shaped, or appear outside the usual eczema sites [1]. Further reading: 7 myths about athlete's foot and nail fungus.

Cutaneous T-cell lymphoma: uncommon, but must not be missed

Cutaneous T-cell lymphoma (CTCL) is a lymphoma that starts in the skin. Its most discussed form, mycosis fungoides, can look very much like eczema in its early stages [1]. Clues include onset in adulthood with atypical features, persistence despite topical treatment, raised plaques, nodules or ulcers, widespread redness of the skin (erythroderma), and enlarged lymph nodes or other whole-body symptoms [1]. CTCL is relatively uncommon, and these clues do not mean lymphoma is present, but they warrant a dermatologist's assessment. See our guide to cutaneous T-cell lymphoma.

Other possibilities

  • Drug eruptions: sudden onset, sparing of skin creases, possible blisters or pustules, sensitivity to sunlight and, often, little itch [1]. Adults often take several medicines, so tell your doctor about all of them.
  • Psoriasis: usually less itchy, with less oozing and crusting [1].
  • Autoimmune blistering diseases such as bullous pemphigoid and dermatitis herpetiformis: blisters on the skin or mucous membranes [1].
  • Bacterial or viral infection: honey-coloured crusts or follicular pustules; or sudden clusters of small blisters with punched-out erosions, possibly with fever, as in eczema herpeticum [1]. The latter needs prompt medical attention.
  • Others: cutaneous lupus erythematosus, pityriasis rubra pilaris, prurigo nodularis, nutritional deficiencies (for example iron, zinc or biotin), and light-sensitive conditions concentrated on sun-exposed skin [1].

Two things to check before doubting the diagnosis

The guideline stresses two checks before looking for another disease: has treatment been optimised according to guidelines, and is it actually being used as prescribed [1]? Standard care is stepwise: moisturisers and trigger avoidance are the foundation; mild-to-moderate disease is treated with topical anti-inflammatory medicines, adding phototherapy or stronger steroids if needed; frequent flares can be prevented with scheduled proactive application; and moderate-to-severe disease may need biologics, JAK inhibitors or conventional oral immunomodulators [1]. Details of topical treatment are in our guide to topical therapy for AD.

Many factors affect adherence: ease of access, cost, complicated regimens, fear of side effects, the doctor-patient relationship, and the "treatment fatigue" that comes with a chronic relapsing disease [1]. Concern about skin thinning or absorption into the body is one such barrier and may lead to under-use or early stopping [1]. Then the problem is how the treatment is used, not the diagnosis; telling your doctor honestly how you apply it helps sort out under-treatment from a wrong diagnosis. For steroid strengths and safe use, see our guide to topical steroids.

What counts as "not responding"? The guideline acknowledges that there is no universal standard or timeline; response is usually judged after the period in which a given treatment would be expected to work [1]. Failure can show up as a larger affected area, higher severity scores, more itch, poor sleep, worse quality of life, or a change in what the rash looks like or where it appears [1].

Tests a dermatologist may suggest

The guideline does not prescribe a fixed set of tests; the choice depends on each person's presentation [1]. The three core tests are below.

Patch testing

Patch testing identifies contact allergens. The guideline calls it the gold standard for diagnosing contact allergy and says comprehensive patch testing should be considered for adult-onset dermatitis in an atypical distribution (such as the hands, face or eyelids), chronic relapsing dermatitis that does not respond to or worsens on standard AD treatment, and new or worsening facial dermatitis [1].

Small patches containing many allergens are applied to the back and read over 48 to 96 hours, which means several visits [1]. Your doctor may add extended series based on your home and work exposures. Ideally the test is done before starting systemic treatment; if you are already on treatment, it may still be done, sometimes with adjusted dosing or timing, which your doctor decides [1]. Positive reactions must be judged for relevance to your actual exposures, and testing is sometimes repeated [1].

Skin scraping

Scraping looks for scabies mites, eggs or faecal pellets, or for fungus. The doctor gently scrapes off some scale and examines it under a microscope with potassium hydroxide (KOH) or mineral oil, adding fungal or bacterial culture when needed [1]. It is quick.

The limitation is that a single scraping can miss the diagnosis. The guideline cites an estimated sensitivity of 43% to 90% (median 45%) for scabies and 59% to 91% for fungal infection [1]. Doctors may therefore sample several sites, and repeat testing or referral may be needed when scrapings are negative but suspicion remains high [1].

Skin biopsy

A biopsy distinguishes AD from other diseases in the tissue, such as cutaneous T-cell lymphoma, bullous pemphigoid, lupus or pityriasis rubra pilaris [1]. Usually a small cylinder of skin is removed under local anaesthetic (punch biopsy); beyond routine staining, special stains, immunohistochemistry, clonality studies or direct immunofluorescence may be added [1]. For the procedure and aftercare, see our guide to skin biopsy.

When lymphoma is suspected, one biopsy may not be enough. In the literature reviewed by the guideline, confirming the alternative diagnosis took 1 to 4 biopsies (median 2); if the first result is inconclusive, your doctor may biopsy again after some time or when the rash changes [1]. This does not mean things are getting worse; early lesions are simply hard to read under the microscope.

Other tests

Depending on the situation, other tests include bacterial or viral culture, viral PCR (faster when herpes infection is suspected), phototesting or photopatch testing when the rash favours sun-exposed skin, and blood tests when there are whole-body symptoms or concern about malignancy [1]. The guideline also warns that over-testing adds cost, burden and confusion, while under-testing can delay treatment, so decisions must be individualised [1].

What you can do

  • Bring everything: prescription creams (with the box or packaging), moisturisers, body wash and shampoo, cosmetics and skincare, and a list of materials you handle at work; list your oral medicines and supplements too.
  • Describe honestly how you actually use your treatment: how often, how much, and when you stopped.
  • Do not stop treatment abruptly or switch to over-the-counter products or home remedies on your own; if you are on injections or tablets, talk to your doctor before changing anything.
  • Tell your doctor if family members or housemates also itch, or if you live in a care home [1].
  • Plan for follow-up visits: patch testing needs several visits, and more than one biopsy may be needed [1].

When to see a dermatologist

  • No improvement, or worsening, after using treatment as directed for the expected period.
  • "Eczema" that first appeared in adulthood, or in unusual places such as the hands, face or eyelids [1].
  • Previously controlled disease whose appearance or location has changed, or that has spread to new areas [1].
  • A rash that improved on steroids at first but then spread or became ring-shaped [1].
  • Itch that is worse at night, or housemates who also itch [1].
  • Blisters, pustules, raised plaques or nodules, ulcers, widespread redness, enlarged lymph nodes or other whole-body symptoms [1].
  • You are about to start, or are already on, a biologic, JAK inhibitor or other systemic treatment that is not working as expected [1].

Fever with clusters of small blisters or punched-out erosions needs prompt medical care [1].

Frequently asked questions

My steroid cream seems to make the itch worse. Could I be allergic to it?

Possibly, but it is not the only explanation. The active steroid itself can cause allergy, and the cream's inactive ingredients are a common source too [2]. A study of 358 US prescription topical steroids found that 77.7% contained at least one common contact allergen, with an average of 1.83 per product; the most frequent were propylene glycol (56.4%) and cetyl stearyl alcohol (43.9%) [2]. Another possibility is an underlying fungal infection or scabies that the steroid is making worse [1]. Do not stop on your own; show the cream to a dermatologist, who may arrange patch testing or a scraping.

Do creams, ointments and gels differ in allergen content?

In the same US study, ointments contained an average of 0.78 allergens per product and only 47.2% contained at least one, whereas creams averaged 2.93 and all 131 creams contained at least one; foams averaged 3.10 [2]. These are US label data, products sold in Taiwan may differ, and containing an allergen does not mean you will react to it. The choice of base also depends on body site, season and how it feels to use, so discuss it with your doctor or pharmacist.

Can I have patch testing while on injections or tablets for AD?

The guideline says patch testing is ideally done before systemic treatment starts, but it can also be done during treatment if contact allergy is suspected, sometimes with adjusted dosing or timing [1]. Whether and how to adjust is your doctor's decision; do not stop treatment yourself.

My doctor wants a biopsy, maybe more than one. Does that mean it is serious?

Not necessarily. A biopsy is done to make the diagnosis more certain. In the misdiagnosis literature reviewed by the guideline, 1 to 4 biopsies (median 2) were needed to confirm the alternative diagnosis, because some diseases show only subtle changes early on [1]. An extra biopsy is usually about not missing something, not a sign that things are worse.

Wouldn't switching straight to a biologic or JAK inhibitor be faster than testing?

If the diagnosis is wrong, a stronger drug may not help. In the lymphoma cases reviewed by the guideline, the median time on systemic therapy for presumed AD was 6 months (range 1 to 96 months) [1]. That is why the guideline notes that patch testing is ideally done before systemic treatment, and why some expert groups suggest ruling out skin infection before escalating therapy [1]. This is not a criticism of these medicines; they work best when the diagnosis is right. See our guide to systemic treatment for AD.

For healthcare professionals

For healthcare professionals

What the guideline says: one Good Practice Statement

The AAD multidisciplinary workgroup used GRADE methodology. Its literature search (July 2024, updated April 2025) found no direct evidence on whether adults with treatment-unresponsive presumed AD should undergo further workup, so it issued a single Good Practice Statement (GPS) based on indirect evidence and consensus [1]. The guideline assigns no strength-of-recommendation or certainty-of-evidence grade to this statement, and the other testing advice in the text is likewise ungraded.

Primary-care checklist before escalating therapy

  1. Diagnostic clues: adult onset? Atypical distribution (head/neck, hands/feet, eyelids, photo-distributed, asymmetric)? Blisters, pustules, nodules, ulcers, erythroderma, lymphadenopathy or systemic symptoms [1]?
  2. Is treatment optimised: appropriate potency for site and severity, adequate duration, proactive therapy for recurrent disease [1]?
  3. Adherence: actual amount and frequency, steroid phobia, cost and access, treatment fatigue [1].
  4. Exposure review: every topical medicine (including vehicle and excipients), emollients, cleansers and personal-care products, occupational exposures; also consider systemic contact dermatitis (allergen absorbed by ingestion, intravenous or subcutaneous routes) [1]; for excipient data see [2].
  5. Scrapings: annular, asymmetric, intertriginous or foot lesions, or initial improvement then worsening on steroids → KOH; nocturnal itch, symptomatic contacts, finger-web or genital papules, care-home residence → scabies scraping; sample several sites in both [1].
  6. Signs of infection (crusting, oozing, pustules, grouped vesicles, punched-out erosions) → bacterial culture or viral PCR; AD skin is commonly colonised by Staphylococcus aureus, so a positive culture does not always mean infection [1].
  7. Drug history: timing of new medicines relative to the rash; sudden onset, no flexural involvement, little itch and photosensitivity suggest a drug eruption [1].
  8. Before starting systemic therapy, consider referral for patch-testing assessment [1].

For pharmacists and nurses: vehicles and excipients

Belair and colleagues searched US DailyMed in September–October 2025 and analysed 358 products of 15 commonly prescribed topical corticosteroids (5 low-, 7 mid- and 3 high-potency) in 8 vehicles against the American Contact Dermatitis Society (ACDS) Core Screening series [2]. They identified 19 ACDS allergens; 278 products (77.7%) contained at least one, with a mean of 1.83 per product, and only 55 products had inactive ingredients limited to mineral oil, coconut oil and/or petrolatum [2].

The most frequent allergens were propylene glycol (56.4%), cetyl stearyl alcohol (43.9%), sorbitan sesquioleate (29.6%), parabens (16.8%), lanolin alcohol (8.4%), chlorocresol (6.4%), sorbic acid (5.9%) and fragrance/benzyl alcohol (5.6%) [2].

  • Known contact allergy: check the label's ingredient list and avoid confirmed allergens; the authors mention safe-product lists such as ACDS CAMP, which do not include every steroid [2].
  • Products with the same active ingredient differ in excipients; for example, 83.5% of clobetasol products, not all, contained at least one allergen [2]. Re-checking the ingredient list after a brand or vehicle switch is therefore reasonable (our inference from these data).
  • The active steroid can itself be the allergen; corticosteroids were historically grouped by the Goossens-Coopman classes (A, B, C, D1, D2), but recent data show frequent cross-reactions between groups [2].
  • Limitations: only US prescription products were analysed, and data were collected during the 2025 US government shutdown, which may have affected DailyMed accuracy [2]; for Taiwanese products, check the local package insert. Some drug-level percentages in the paper's table do not match their numerators and denominators, so only overall and vehicle-level figures are used here.

Taiwan practice notes

  • Scabies: National Health Insurance (NHI) reimbursement of oral ivermectin requires microscopy-confirmed scabies with photographs on file; crusted scabies may be combined with topical treatment, but each prescription requires new typical lesions and positive microscopy; for residents of residential long-term care facilities, microscopy confirmation is not required but photographs are [3].
  • For AD in patients aged 12 years or older, NHI coverage of dupilumab, upadacitinib, abrocitinib and lebrikizumab requires prior authorisation and, in principle, documented failure of adequate phototherapy plus at least two conventional systemic agents (or medical reasons precluding them); patients previously approved for a psoriasis biologic must be free of psoriasis for at least two years, or have psoriasis excluded by biopsy with the diagnosis confirmed by a dermatologist [3]. This escalation pathway is itself a natural checkpoint for re-confirming the diagnosis.

When to refer

  • The clinical picture is unclear, initial tests are inconclusive, or a high-risk condition (such as CTCL or autoimmune blistering disease) is suspected [1].
  • Patch testing is needed: it requires standardised allergens, trained staff and several visits over 48 to 96 hours, and distinguishing irritant from allergic reactions takes experience [1].
  • A biopsy is needed where site and technique selection are critical, or dermatopathology expertise is required (CTCL changes can be subtle) [1].
  • Scrapings are negative despite high suspicion, infection is widespread or recurrent, or systemic treatment is being considered [1].

Interpretation and limits of the evidence

The direction of this GPS is uncontroversial, but it sets no time threshold for non-response and offers no testing algorithm or cost-effectiveness analysis. The delay figures come from the authors' review of the misdiagnosis literature (study types not described in the main text) and may be subject to publication bias; all tests have false negatives, their predictive values are unclear, and skin prick testing did not meet inclusion criteria [1]. The "more than 20%" figure is drawn from a study of patients with incomplete response to dupilumab [1][4] and should not be extrapolated to all adults with AD. Several authors disclose industry consulting or research funding; the guideline was funded internally by the AAD [1]. In practice, primary care can optimise treatment, check adherence, review exposures and perform scrapings, then refer on red flags.

For a full critical appraisal, see the JAAD November 2026 monthly review; board-exam notes are in Fitzpatrick Ch. 22 Atopic Dermatitis, Ch. 24 Allergic Contact Dermatitis, Ch. 119 Cutaneous Lymphoma and Ch. 178 Scabies.
