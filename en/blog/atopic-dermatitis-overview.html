Key points in 30 seconds

Key points
  • Atopic dermatitis results from a damaged skin barrier plus immune inflammation; it is chronic and waxes and wanes. About 7.88% of children worldwide are affected, and symptom prevalence in Taiwanese study groups ranges from 4.1% to 6.7% [1][2].
  • Diagnosis rests on examining the skin and taking a history; no single blood test confirms it. A positive allergy test does not mean that allergen causes the eczema, so avoid blind food elimination [1][15][19].
  • The foundation is a generous moisturizer at least twice a day, plus topical anti-inflammatory treatment during flares. Topical steroids used as directed are safe; under-using them out of fear makes the disease harder to control [1][15].
  • Once clear, applying treatment once or twice a week to flare-prone areas (proactive maintenance) markedly reduces flares [1][11].
  • For moderate-to-severe disease not controlled with topicals, options include phototherapy, oral immunosuppressants, biologics or oral JAK inhibitors; Taiwan's NHI covers some of these for patients who meet strict criteria and pass prior review [5][16][27].

What is atopic dermatitis?

Atopic dermatitis, also called atopic eczema, is a chronic inflammatory skin disease. It usually starts in childhood, but it can begin at any age; about a quarter of patients (16.5% to 37.2% across studies) first develop symptoms as adults. Genetically and biologically it behaves like a lifelong tendency, while clinically it relapses and remits: some people have only a little eczema in the elbow and knee creases, others have more than 90% of their skin involved, and in some it stays on the hands [1].

Why does it happen? Two core problems feed each other. The first is a damaged skin barrier: structural proteins of the outer layer (such as filaggrin) and lipids are deficient, so water escapes and irritants and allergens get in; even normal-looking skin has a weaker barrier. The second is type 2 immune inflammation: Th2 cells and related immune cells release IL-4, IL-13, IL-31 and other mediators that further damage the barrier and directly cause itch. Itch leads to scratching, scratching to more inflammation and more itch, an itch–scratch–inflammation cycle; overgrowth of Staphylococcus aureus on the skin adds to the inflammation [1][13].

How common is it, and who is at risk?

  • Children worldwide: the ISAAC programme, pooling 31 studies of more than 1.43 million children in 102 countries, found a prevalence of about 7.88%, making it one of the commonest childhood skin diseases [1].
  • Taiwan: symptom prevalence in Taiwanese study groups cited by the Taiwanese consensus ranges from 4.1% to 6.7% and has risen markedly over recent decades [1][2]. In national insurance data the 8-year claims prevalence was about 9.6% under age 20 and about 4% in adults; these are primary-diagnosis claims, so people with mild symptoms who never sought care are missed and the figures may be underestimates [23].
  • Adults: estimates vary widely: the AAD cites about 2.0% worldwide [10], one UpToDate topic estimates 2–5% [17], and another cites 10–14% in Nordic studies and about 7% in the US [13].
  • Family history is the strongest risk factor: about 70% of patients have one; one atopic parent raises a child's risk about two- to threefold, two atopic parents about three- to fivefold. Loss-of-function variants in the filaggrin gene raise the risk about three- to fourfold [13].
  • Environment: urban living, low sunlight or dry climates, a typical Western diet and repeated antibiotics before age 5 are associated with higher risk; Taiwanese studies link smoking, second-hand smoke and food allergy with adult-onset disease [1]. The Taiwanese pediatric guideline adds that in Taiwan's warm, humid climate fungi are a major aeroallergen risk factor, and that tobacco smoke exposure in pregnancy and early childhood, and high maternal work stress and long working hours in pregnancy, are associated with a higher risk in young children [3].

Symptoms: itch and dryness, in different places at different ages

Dry skin and itch are the cardinal features, and the itch is often intense [13]. Acute lesions are red bumps and tiny blisters that ooze and crust; with time the skin becomes dry, scaly and scratched, then thickened with deepened skin lines (lichenification) or cracks, and different stages can coexist [1][13].

  • Infants and toddlers (0–2 years): red, scaly, crusted patches on the cheeks, scalp and outer limbs, weeping when severe; it can be widespread but usually spares the diaper area [13].
  • Children and adolescents (2–16 years): less oozing; thickened plaques in the elbow and knee creases, wrists, ankles and neck [13].
  • Adults: flexures are still often involved, and the face, neck and hands are frequently affected [13]; the Taiwanese consensus notes that adult disease is mostly chronic and thickened, more often on the head, neck, hands and feet, and may be coin-shaped (nummular), nodular (prurigo-like) or seborrheic-like [1].
  • Hand eczema is common in adults, typically on the inner wrists and backs of the hands, and is especially common in people doing "wet work"; about a third also have foot eczema [13]. Reapply moisturizer after handwashing [15]. If substances at work may be involved, see contact dermatitis.

About 60% start in the first year of life and nearly 85% by age 5 [13]. Common minor signs include an extra fold under the eyes, darkening around the eyes, pale facial patches (pityriasis alba), rough bumps on the upper arms (keratosis pilaris), prominent palm creases and cracked lips [1][13].

In darker skin, inflammation may look brown or violet rather than red, and temporary darkening or lightening after inflammation is common. The Taiwanese consensus advises telling patients that atopic dermatitis itself can make the skin temporarily lighter or darker; this is usually not caused by the cream [1][13].

How is it diagnosed? Do I need allergy blood tests?

There is no definitive laboratory or pathology marker; the diagnosis rests on history and the pattern and distribution of the rash. The Taiwanese consensus uses the internationally accepted Hanifin–Rajka criteria: at least three major features (itch, typical pattern and distribution, chronic or relapsing course, personal or family atopy) plus at least three minor features [1].

Allergy tests must be interpreted with care. Up to 80% of patients have raised IgE, yet a normal IgE does not rule out atopic dermatitis [1][13]. About half of children with AD test positive to one or more foods (especially milk, egg, wheat and peanut) on skin-prick or blood tests, but for most this sensitization is clinically irrelevant to the course of the eczema [15]. "Positive" does not mean "eating it triggers a flare", let alone "this is the cause"; long-term avoidance based on test results can lead to poor nutrition or more severe reactions later [19].

When does testing help? Hives, lip or eyelid swelling, vomiting or wheeze within minutes to 2 hours of eating a food suggest true food allergy and need a medical assessment [19][21]; with breathing difficulty or dizziness, call 119 immediately [20]. For new-onset adult eczema, eczema in unusual places (hands, face, eyelids) or eczema that does not respond to proper treatment, the doctor may arrange patch testing (for contact allergy), skin scrapings (for scabies or fungus) or a biopsy (to exclude skin lymphoma and other conditions); see Work-up of refractory atopic dermatitis [10][17].

Treatment goals, severity and the treatment ladder

Current treatment does not cure atopic dermatitis, but it can control it well, and many people need long-term maintenance [15]. The Taiwanese consensus sets the goal as less itch and sustained control so that patients function normally at home, work and school [1]. The Japanese guideline is more specific: no or mild symptoms, no disruption of daily life and little need for medication [12].

How is severity judged? Doctors look at extent and intensity (often with scores such as EASI or SCORAD) and ask about itch, sleep and daily life. The practical definition cited by the Taiwanese consensus is moderate-to-severe disease when lesions cover at least 10% of body surface area, or when moderate-to-severe lesions affect special sites or quality of life is clearly affected regardless of area [1]. Patients can self-assess with POEM: count the days in the past week with itch, sleep disturbance, bleeding, weeping, cracking, flaking and dryness; the total is 0–28 and takes under 2 minutes [1][15].

The Taiwanese three-line approach, plus newer drugs

The 2020 Taiwanese Dermatological Association consensus divides treatment into three lines, always on top of basic care (moisturizers, education, avoiding irritants and allergens), followed by maintenance once controlled [1][2]:

  1. First line: moisturizers, topical corticosteroids and therapeutic education; antihistamines are listed as an itch adjunct (newer evidence shows little benefit; see the FAQ) [1][2][22].
  2. Second line: topical calcineurin inhibitors (tacrolimus, pimecrolimus), a short course of systemic corticosteroids, phototherapy and antibiotics when infected [1][2].
  3. Third line: systemic immunomodulators (ciclosporin, azathioprine, methotrexate, mycophenolate, dupilumab), antiseptics (such as dilute bleach baths) and complementary medicine; the consensus algorithm also lists potent topical steroids and psychological approaches at step 3 [1][2].

Since 2020, international guidelines have added more options: non-steroid topicals (such as crisaborole), more biologics (tralokinumab, lebrikizumab, nemolizumab) and oral JAK inhibitors (upadacitinib, abrocitinib, baricitinib) [5][6][16]. Newer international guidance generally places biologics ahead of conventional oral immunosuppressants [5][16]; Taiwan's NHI, however, still requires patients aged 12 or over to have failed phototherapy and conventional systemic therapy before biologics or JAK inhibitors can be reimbursed [27].

Topical steroids: how to use them safely and well

Topical corticosteroids are the first-line anti-inflammatory treatment for flares, with well-established efficacy in adults and children; used appropriately under medical supervision they are safe and effective, and should be continued until the flare is controlled [1]. The American Academy of Dermatology (AAD) adult and pediatric guidelines both give them a strong recommendation [4][8].

  • Match the strength to the site: thin skin on the face (especially eyelids), neck, folds and genitals should not get potent steroids; use a weak steroid or a non-steroid cream there. Medium strength suits the body and limbs; potent steroids are for severe flares on thick skin, for short periods [1][4][8].
  • How long: the Taiwanese consensus gives 1–2 weeks for an acute flare as an example, tapering once itch clearly settles [1]; UpToDate suggests 2–4 weeks, limiting potent steroids to 2 weeks twice daily (or 4 weeks once daily) on the body and 3–4 days on the face, neck and folds, and only when a doctor specifically directs it [15]. Do not stop abruptly [12].
  • How often and how much: the Taiwanese consensus generally advises twice daily, though once daily may suffice for some products [1]; the US JTF guideline suggests once daily for mid-to-high potency products [11]. The Japanese guideline's fingertip unit, a line of cream from the tube to the first finger joint, is about 0.5 g and covers roughly two adult palms [12]. About 30 g covers an adult's whole body once [15]; the Taiwanese consensus cites average monthly amounts of 15 g for infants and 30 g for children, and up to 90 g for adolescents and adults [1].
  • Formulation: creams for moist, inflamed skin, ointments for dry, thickened skin and solutions for hairy areas such as the scalp [1]. Steroids can be applied to scratched, weeping or cracked skin; infection is not a contraindication, but it must be treated as well [1].

Worried about steroid side effects? The facts

Fear of steroids is very common: its prevalence ranges from 21% to 83.7% across countries, Taiwan ranks in the top three in international surveys, and it is most marked in parents of infants under 1 with moderate to very severe disease who have not attended education sessions. Under-treatment out of fear makes the disease harder to control [1][2].

  • Thinning: skin atrophy is the main local concern; the risk comes from potent products, occlusion, thin or fold skin, older age and long continuous use. Visible vessels, stretch marks, steroid acne or rosacea can also occur [1][4]. Choosing the right strength for the site and following the course keeps the risk low.
  • Darkening: inflammation itself often leaves temporary pigment changes, and the Taiwanese consensus notes that atopic dermatitis can temporarily lighten or darken the skin [1]. Such pigment changes are usually temporary.
  • Whole-body effects: short-term use of weak-to-medium steroids does not suppress the adrenal glands; in children fewer than 3% developed mild adrenal suppression. The real concern is long-term use of potent steroids over large areas; a very potent steroid such as clobetasol propionate can suppress the adrenal glands even after a single application [1][4].
  • Eyes: children are more prone to steroid-induced glaucoma and cataract, so long-term use on the eyelids and around the eyes should be avoided [1].
  • Infection: topical steroids do not increase skin infection, and eczema herpeticum is linked with untreated atopic dermatitis [1]. The AAD rates the evidence on "topical steroid addiction or withdrawal" as low to very low [4]. More in our guide to topical steroids.

After clearing: proactive maintenance to prevent flares

Skin that looks clear still has low-grade inflammation and a weak barrier. For flare-prone areas, continuing a topical steroid or tacrolimus ointment once or twice a week after clearing, together with daily moisturizer, prevents relapses better than moisturizer alone [1][12]. In trials summarized by the US allergy Joint Task Force (JTF), 38% of patients on proactive maintenance flared compared with 69% of those who treated only when it flared [11].

What to do in a flare: a flare is a worsening that needs more treatment. Restart daily anti-inflammatory treatment (at the strength and frequency your doctor gave) and moisturize more often; after about 2–4 weeks of continuous treatment, taper back to the maintenance schedule your doctor set (once or twice a week in the Taiwanese consensus [1]; 2–3 days a week in UpToDate [15]). Agree a personal action plan with your doctor in advance [11]; if it does not settle or flares are frequent, see your doctor (see "When to seek care") [15].

Non-steroid creams and ointments

  • Topical calcineurin inhibitors (tacrolimus ointment, pimecrolimus cream) do not thin the skin and suit the face, eyelids, neck, folds and genitals, or areas needing long-term treatment [1][15]. Burning or stinging in the first days is common and usually fades; calming an acute flare with a steroid first reduces this [1]. The US label still carries a boxed warning based on rare lymphoma reports; the AAD adult guideline considers the absolute lymphoma risk very low, and the AAD pediatric guideline, the JTF and the Taiwanese consensus find the data do not support a link; Canada removed the warning in 2021 [1][4][8][11]. Tips: if it burns at first, refrigerate the tube for 15–20 minutes [15]; do not cover it with cling film or other occlusion unless a doctor says so [1]; use sun protection during treatment [2].
  • Crisaborole ointment (a PDE4 inhibitor) is for mild-to-moderate disease; its Taiwanese licence covers adults and children aged 3 months and older [28]. Application-site stinging is fairly common [15]. The NHI reimbursement rules contain no specific rule for it [27].
  • Abroad there are also topical JAK inhibitors (ruxolitinib cream, delgocitinib ointment), tapinarof cream, roflumilast cream and difamilast ointment [4][6][12]; in the Taiwan FDA licence data as of 18 November 2026, none was found to be approved for atopic dermatitis in Taiwan [28].

When are injections or tablets needed?

Most patients have mild disease and are managed with moisturizers and topical treatment [1]; among 377,423 patients aged 2 or over in Taiwan's 2015 insurance data, prescription records classified 84.5% as mild, 11.9% as moderate and 3.6% as severe [36]. Systemic treatment is for severe or refractory disease that seriously affects quality of life and social activities [1], for example moderate-to-severe disease despite optimal topicals, frequent flares needing continuous potent topicals, or intractable itch regardless of the extent of the rash [16].

Before starting, doctors usually check that the diagnosis is right, that topical treatment has been used correctly and sufficiently, and whether contact allergy or skin infection is also present [10][16].

  • Phototherapy: narrowband UVB and UVA1 are the most used [1]; adults and adolescents usually attend 2–3 clinic sessions a week for 10–14 weeks, an option for those who prefer to avoid or cannot take systemic drugs, though it means frequent visits [5][16]; for children, UpToDate considers it from about age 6 if they can cooperate [18], whereas the Taiwanese pediatric guideline does not recommend it under age 12 [3]. Taiwanese insurance data following 1,241 adults aged 20 or over treated with UVB (median 11 years), compared with 4,964 patients never treated with phototherapy: no increase in skin cancer was observed, though cases were few and only adults were studied [32].
  • Conventional oral immunosuppressants: ciclosporin, methotrexate, azathioprine and mycophenolate are cheaper but need regular blood tests for kidney, liver and blood counts, and ciclosporin is not suitable long term [1][5].
  • Biologics (injections under the skin): antibodies against type 2 inflammation, given every 2–4 weeks. In Taiwan dupilumab is licensed for moderate-to-severe disease from 6 months of age [28]; conjunctivitis and injection-site reactions are the main side effects, and routine blood tests are not needed [5][18]. Retrospective experience in 111 adults at three Taiwanese hospitals showed that some patients with an inadequate response at 4 months kept improving up to 12 months [26].
  • Oral JAK inhibitors (daily tablets) work quickly on itch and rash but can increase infections such as shingles and carry warnings about clots, cardiovascular events and cancer; blood tests and screening are needed before and during treatment, with extra caution in older people or those with a history of cardiovascular disease, cancer or clots [5][16].
  • Oral steroids work fast, but rebound flares after stopping are common; they are not for long-term control and are used only briefly for severe acute flares or as a bridge to other treatment [1][5].

The choice between injections and tablets depends on age, severity, other illnesses, pregnancy plans and personal preference, and is made with your doctor; see our overview of biologics, and for NHI criteria the clinician section below and our guide to NHI dermatology drugs.

Daily care: bathing, moisturizing, itch and triggers

  • Moisturizing is treatment: apply a moisturizer at least twice a day, or whenever the skin feels dry, and use plenty; apply it as soon as possible after bathing while the skin is still damp [1]. Regular moisturizing reduces flares, delays relapse and lowers steroid needs; there is no credible evidence that any one product is better than others [1].
  • Choosing one: "fragrance-free" is better than "unscented"; botanical additives such as tea tree oil can sensitize [15]. In winter use richer, oilier products [1]; in hot, humid conditions heavy ointments or thick creams can cause folliculitis, so lighter creams or lotions may suit better [15]. UpToDate prefers thicker creams or ointments (such as petroleum jelly), which may protect better, but different formulations appear similarly effective, so personal preference is the main factor [15]; there is no need for expensive or particular brands, as a plain fragrance-free over-the-counter moisturizer is fine [4][11]. There is no set amount for moisturizers, but use plenty; as a guide, UpToDate estimates that about 30 g of a topical preparation covers an adult's whole body once; reapply after handwashing too [15].
  • Bathing: a 5–10 minute lukewarm soak is acceptable; the Japanese guideline considers 38–40°C best for barrier recovery and advises against 42°C or hotter [1][12]. Use a small amount of a neutral or low-pH, hypoallergenic, fragrance-free non-soap cleanser; adding oils, emollients or other additives to bath water is not advised as treatment [1].
  • Itch and scratching: topical anti-inflammatory treatment (steroids, calcineurin inhibitors and others) also controls itch effectively; a tepid bath followed by moisturizer relieves itch, and some people find a refrigerated moisturizer works better; for chronic itch, habit-reversal training, relaxation training and cognitive behavioural therapy have been reported as beneficial [15]. For severe flares your doctor may show you wet wraps after applying treatment (a damp cotton layer under a dry one) [8][15].
  • Common triggers: sweating and overheating, dry air or winter, stress, rough wool or synthetic fabrics, fragrances, soaps and cleaning products, and frequent washing without moisturizing [15][20]. House-dust-mite measures have very limited evidence of benefit for the eczema itself [15][19].
  • Supplements and remedies: there is insufficient evidence for probiotics, fish oil or vitamins as treatment; about 30% of Taiwanese patients have used traditional Chinese medicine, some herbs can be toxic to the liver or heart, and "natural" remedies may be adulterated with steroids. Tell your doctor about any complementary treatment [1][2].

Extra care for severe flares (under professional guidance)

  • Wet wraps: apply moisturizer or the prescribed medicine (a low-to-medium potency steroid is preferred), then a layer of damp cotton clothing covered by a dry layer; keep the wet layer from drying out, leaving it overnight or changing it every few hours, for as long as your doctor advises [15][18]. An example for children: once or twice a day for 3–5 days and up to 14 days (UpToDate) [18]; the Taiwanese pediatric guideline gives 3–7 days, up to 14 days if severe [3]. Wet wraps increase steroid absorption and can cause chills or folliculitis, and layered damp clothing carries risks of strangulation and chilling in infants [8][18].
  • Dilute bleach baths are not routine; a doctor may suggest them for moderate-to-severe disease with recurrent bacterial infection. The dilution depends on the product and your doctor's instructions, the skin is rinsed afterwards, and undiluted bleach can burn the skin [1][8][11]. The benefit may come mainly from the bath itself [1].

Warning signs of skin infection

Eczematous skin is prone to infection with Staphylococcus aureus, herpes simplex virus, molluscum contagiosum virus and others [1]. Yellow crusts or pus usually mean bacterial infection and need assessment for antibiotics [15][20]. A condition needing urgent care is eczema herpeticum (widespread herpes simplex infection, in fewer than 3% of patients): many uniform, scattered small blisters or pustules appear quickly and turn into "punched-out" erosions and crusts, possibly with fever [10][13][15]; eczema that worsens after contact with someone with a cold sore also needs a doctor [20]; eczema herpeticum needs prompt antiviral treatment [15]. Hand, foot and mouth disease from coxsackievirus A6, in children and less often young adults with AD, tends to appear widely on eczematous skin ("eczema coxsackium"); it is self-limited and needs no specific treatment, but it can look like eczema herpeticum, so see a doctor if unsure; widespread molluscum contagiosum can also occur [13][15].

Food and food allergy: should I avoid foods?

For most people, food does not cause atopic dermatitis; food allergy usually shows up as hives or swelling rather than worsening eczema [18][21]. Occasionally, especially in young children with more severe eczema, food can aggravate it [21].

  • Blind elimination diets are not advised: in meta-analysis, elimination diets gave only a small, clinically unimportant improvement, and both the US JTF guideline and UpToDate advise against them for most patients [11][19].
  • Elimination has risks: poor nutrition and lower quality of life; in an allergy clinic review of 298 patients, 19% of those with suspected food-triggered eczema and no previous immediate reactions developed acute reactions to the eliminated food, 30% of them anaphylaxis [19].
  • If you really suspect a food: note what was eaten and what happened when, and let the doctor judge, with a supervised oral food challenge if needed; foods that are already tolerated should not be stopped just because a test is positive [19].

Introducing solids to babies: early introduction of common allergenic foods does not prevent atopic dermatitis itself but may lower the risk of food allergy [9]. In the LEAP peanut trial in high-risk infants (which the JTF treats as indirect evidence), peanut allergy was about five times as common with avoidance as with early introduction, and the JTF suggests that active eczema treatment with early introduction of allergenic foods is most appropriate [11]. The Japanese guideline considers that maternal food avoidance during pregnancy or breastfeeding may not help prevent childhood AD and is instead harmful [12]. Discuss timing and method with your pediatrician.

Can it be prevented? In babies at risk of atopic dermatitis, regular moisturizing before age 2 may reduce the risk (low-certainty evidence), but no protective effect is seen in babies in general; the AAD conditionally recommends against probiotics or vitamin D supplements for prevention (evidence suggests no benefit, low certainty) [9].

When a child has atopic dermatitis

  • Will it go away? Most children with early onset are clear by late childhood, but in some it continues into adolescence and adulthood; later onset, greater severity and longer duration make persistence more likely [13].
  • What the Taiwanese pediatric guideline (2022) says: the Taiwan Academy of Pediatric Allergy, Asthma and Immunology (TAPAAI), as named in the guideline, diagnoses childhood atopic dermatitis with three criteria: itch, symmetrical eczema in an age-specific pattern, and a chronic or relapsing course (more than 2 months under age 1, more than 6 months in older children and adolescents); severity is graded by the extent of severe eruptions as mild (only mild eruptions), moderate (under 10% of body surface area), severe (10–29%) or very severe (30% or more) [3]. For care it advises applying moisturizer immediately after a 10- to 15-minute lukewarm bath or shower, starting moisturizers in high-risk babies with a parent or sibling with allergic disease as soon as dry skin appears, and does not support maternal dietary restriction in pregnancy or breastfeeding to prevent allergy [3].
  • Medicines: children have a larger skin area relative to body weight, so milder steroids are preferred and long-term use on and around the eyelids is avoided [1]; per the Taiwanese consensus, tacrolimus 0.03% and pimecrolimus can be used from age 2 (the Taiwanese licence for pimecrolimus states 3 months and older) [1][28]. Parental steroid fear is common, and education sessions help reduce anxiety and improve adherence [1].
  • Sleep: itch disturbs children's sleep; sedating antihistamines may impair sleep quality and are not advised long term [1]. A small Taiwanese randomized trial found melatonin shortened time to fall asleep and reduced severity, but more studies are needed [1][15].
  • Wet wraps can be used with topical treatment during flares, but layered damp clothing carries risks of strangulation and chilling in infants, so do this only under professional guidance [8].
  • Systemic treatment: systemic steroids for more than 2 weeks clearly increase infection, growth delay and obesity in children, so great caution is needed [1]; the AAD pediatric guideline advises against systemic steroids in children except as a short bridge for severe acute flares [8]. Dupilumab is licensed in Taiwan from 6 months, and NHI covers eligible children from age 6 [27][28]. For children on dupilumab, check the timing of routine vaccines, especially live vaccines, with the doctor [18]. For growth data see Atopic dermatitis and children's height.
  • School and wellbeing: children with AD more often have anxiety and attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) [9]; poor sleep and severe disease can make these worse, and a child psychiatrist can join the care when needed [1].

Pregnancy and breastfeeding

Atopic dermatitis may worsen, stay the same or improve during pregnancy, and most pregnant women can be managed with non-drug measures and topical steroids [15]. Good control matters in pregnancy and breastfeeding too; the Japanese guideline notes that before week 4 of pregnancy (about 2 weeks after fertilization) drugs do not pass directly to the embryo, so treatment need not be stopped for that reason, and every pregnancy carries a 2–3% background rate of malformations [12]. This does not apply to drugs such as methotrexate and mycophenolate, which must be stopped before conception, nor is it a reason not to tell your doctor; if you are pregnant or planning pregnancy, review all medicines with your doctor promptly [1][2].

  • Topical steroids are generally safe in pregnancy; very potent ones may be linked with low birth weight and are best avoided except as rescue for localized chronic or thickened lesions, and the Taiwanese consensus advises avoiding fluticasone propionate in pregnancy [1].
  • Breastfeeding: apply topical medicines after feeding, clean the nipple before the next feed and avoid applying them to the breast just before nursing [1][12].
  • Topical calcineurin inhibitors: data are limited. Taiwanese labels: tacrolimus only if clearly needed in pregnancy and not recommended during breastfeeding; pimecrolimus with caution in pregnancy and not on the breast during breastfeeding. Given minimal absorption and a favourable benefit–risk ratio, the Taiwanese consensus still supports off-label use in pregnancy and, as with steroids, applying them after feeds and cleaning the nipples before feeding [1][2].
  • Antihistamines: if needed, loratadine has the most pregnancy experience [1]; during breastfeeding second-generation drugs are preferred, as first-generation ones can make infants irritable or drowsy [12].
  • Phototherapy and tablets: narrowband UVB and UVA1 are acceptable but PUVA should be avoided, with folic acid when planning pregnancy and in the first trimester; oral steroids only briefly; after assessment, ciclosporin can be the first-line systemic drug for long-term control in pregnancy and breastfeeding; methotrexate and mycophenolate are absolutely contraindicated in pregnancy and breastfeeding and must be stopped 6 months before conception; the exact interval follows each label and your doctor [1][2].
  • Biologics and JAK inhibitors: the 2020 Taiwanese consensus advised against dupilumab in pregnancy and breastfeeding until more data are available [1][2], and the NHI rules for patients aged 12 and over exclude pregnant or breastfeeding women [27]. A pregnancy test is needed before an oral JAK inhibitor, and pregnancy should be avoided during treatment [5][11]. Discuss pregnancy plans with your doctor in advance.
  • Taiwanese pregnancy data: a study of Taiwan's insurance and maternal–child databases (2003–2019; 15,495 mothers with AD compared with 77,475 similar mothers) found slightly higher risks of threatened abortion, pre-eclampsia, premature rupture of membranes, threatened preterm labour, fetal growth restriction and bleeding before or after delivery in mothers with AD (adjusted hazard ratios 1.09–1.71), while gestational diabetes, gestational hypertension, preterm labour and fetal death in the womb did not differ significantly; their children had a hazard ratio of about 2.29 for a later diagnosis of AD, and slightly higher risks of allergic rhinitis, asthma, alopecia areata and ADHD. This is a database association study that cannot show the eczema caused these outcomes; it had no severity data and reports only relative risks, not absolute rates [30].

Conditions that often come with it

  • Asthma, allergic rhinitis and food allergy: in a study following more than 27,000 children diagnosed before age 1 for 8 years, about 19% developed asthma (controls 9%), 28% allergic rhinitis (13%) and 16% food allergy (2%) [14]. Still, most children with AD do not go on to develop other allergic conditions [19]. In Taiwan's insurance data, 49.8% of patients with AD also had allergic rhinitis and/or asthma, and 61.2% of children and adolescents under 20 [23].
  • Eyes: atopic keratoconjunctivitis mostly affects adults with AD, with itch, burning, tearing and discharge, and can lead to keratoconus or infectious keratitis [14]; eczema on the eyes needs a doctor [20], and severe, persistent or refractory conjunctivitis warrants ophthalmology referral [5][16].
  • Sleep and mood: chronic itch and poor sleep affect mood. In a Taiwanese National Health Insurance follow-up study (8,208 patients aged 12 or over without prior psychiatric illness, enrolled 1998–2008, compared with people of the same age and sex and followed to 2011), the hazard ratio for a later diagnosis of major depression was about 6.56 and for anxiety disorders about 3.57; in absolute terms, major depression occurred in 1.42 versus 0.20 per 1,000 person-years. This is an association that cannot show the eczema caused it, and the authors note wide confidence intervals [29]; the AAD adult guideline also confirms associations with depression and anxiety and notes that treating the skin may ease depressive symptoms in some people [7]. A Taiwanese study of 200 adults also found that abnormal anxiety and depression screening scores became more common with greater severity; these are screening results, not diagnoses, and causation cannot be shown [25].
  • Skin infections: bacterial, viral and fungal infections are more common [7][9].
  • Others: alopecia areata, urticaria, osteoporosis and fractures in adults, obesity and hypertension have been associated with AD; these are associations, not certainties, and the international guidelines do not call for screening for each one [7][9]. In a Taiwanese insurance study (1997–2013; 36,855 patients and 147,420 controls), the 16-year cumulative incidence of fractures was 8.043% vs 7.366%; after adjustment for other factors, however, the fracture risk of patients overall did not differ clearly, and only moderate-to-severe disease was associated with a higher risk (hazard ratio 1.31); this is an association study [31]. Taiwanese insurance data show a slightly higher rate of venous thromboembolism in adults with AD (1.05 vs 0.82 per 1,000 person-years), though the authors note the absolute difference is small [24].

When to seek care promptly

  • Seek care promptly: sudden crops of small blisters or punched-out erosions on eczema, which may be eczema herpeticum [13][15].
  • Call 119 (in Taiwan) immediately for hives; swelling of the face, eyelids, ears, lips, hands, feet or tongue; runny nose; flushing or itching all over without hives; difficulty breathing, wheeze, coughing or voice change; nausea, vomiting or diarrhoea; dizziness or fainting; or, in babies and young children, looking sick, seeming sleepier than usual or not acting normally: possible anaphylaxis (for example after a food, but not only then) [20].
  • See a doctor soon: fever of 38°C or higher or chills; pus or yellow crusts; eczema getting worse or covering most of the body; involvement of the eyes, lips or inside the mouth; worsening after contact with someone with a cold sore [20].
  • Tell your doctor at your visit: itch keeping you awake, or low mood or anxiety [20].
  • No improvement after 4–8 weeks of proper treatment, persistent problems at special sites, or 4 or more flares a year despite appropriate maintenance therapy: see a dermatologist [15].

Frequently asked questions

Will atopic dermatitis go away?

It is a chronic, relapsing condition; current treatment cannot cure it, but it can control it well [1][15]. Many children who start young improve markedly as they grow, while in others it persists into adulthood or returns in adult life [13][17]. A realistic goal is little itch or rash, good sleep and normal school or work, maintained with as little medicine as possible [1][12].

Will steroid creams darken or thin my skin?

Darkening is mostly a pigment change caused by the eczema itself and is usually temporary; thinning is linked mainly with potent products, thin-skinned sites, occlusion and long continuous use. Matching strength to site and tapering as directed gives both benefit and safety; one common problem is using too little out of worry [1][4].

Should I avoid foods? Are allergy blood tests useful?

Most people do not need to avoid foods. A positive test does not mean the food worsens the eczema, and long-term avoidance based on tests alone carries risks; assessment is needed only when a food quickly causes hives, swelling or wheeze (see the food section above) [19][21]; with breathing difficulty, widespread hives or dizziness, treat it as anaphylaxis and call 119 immediately [20].

Injections (biologics) or tablets (JAK inhibitors): which is better?

There is no one-size-fits-all answer. High-dose JAK inhibitors improve the rash more within 16 weeks and relieve itch quickly, but carry concerns about infection and clots and need regular blood tests; biologics need no routine blood tests and have more long-term safety data, but conjunctivitis can occur [5][16]. Age, other illnesses, pregnancy plans, attitudes to injections and NHI criteria all shape the choice [16][27].

How does NHI cover biologics and JAK inhibitors?

Under the rules updated 22 September 2026, NHI covers dupilumab (from age 6) and upadacitinib, abrocitinib and lebrikizumab (from age 12) for patients with lesions for at least 6 months, severity thresholds such as EASI ≥16; from age 12, failure of phototherapy plus at least two of methotrexate, azathioprine and ciclosporin used one after another, not together (mycophenolate does not count), or medical reasons precluding them, and at ages 6–11, failure of topicals plus one of these three; all need prior approval and reapplication every 6 months [27]. In practice an eligible specialist submits records, treatment history and photos for prior review; approval covers 6-month courses, with renewal requested 1 month before expiry; at least a 50% EASI improvement is required at week 16 (dupilumab, abrocitinib, lebrikizumab) and at 6 months (all) to continue; restarting after more than 3 months off counts as a new application [27]. Full criteria are in the clinician table.

Do antihistamine tablets help?

They do little for the eczema itself. A 2026 meta-analysis of 47 randomized trials with 6,230 patients found that second-generation antihistamines reduce severity and itch by a detectable but clinically unimportant amount and may not improve sleep or prevent flares, while first-generation (sedating) drugs increase the risk of cognitive impairment [22]. If you also have hives or allergic rhinitis, antihistamines still treat those; whether to use a sedating one briefly for itch-disturbed sleep is a question for your doctor [1][22].

Epidemiology and pathogenesis: additional points

  • Filaggrin loss-of-function variants have been reported in up to 50% of European and up to 27% of Asian-descent patients and in under 1% of African patients (higher in African Americans: 15.3% in a US pediatric cohort); carriers have earlier, more persistent disease and more asthma, food allergy and hand and foot eczema [13].
  • By IgE sensitization, disease is extrinsic (high total and specific IgE) or intrinsic (normal total IgE, no specific IgE); serum IL-31 correlates with severity and itch [1]. Across 95 studies, 70% of patients carried S. aureus on lesional skin and 39% on nonlesional skin [13].
  • Persistence: compared with onset before age 2, hazard ratios for persistent disease are 2.65 for onset at 2–5 years, 4.22 at 6–11 and 2.04 at 12–17; severity, duration and female sex also predict persistence, whereas allergen sensitization does not [13].
  • Taiwanese epidemiology: in the insurance sample (2000–2007; 66,446 patients among 997,729 people), the 8-year claims prevalence was 6.7% (females 7.5%, males 5.9%), highest under age 1 (22.4%); prevalence was higher in boys before age 8 and in girls afterwards; visits peaked from late spring to mid-summer; only 120 (0.18%) were ever hospitalized, with asthma (18.3%), cellulitis (17.5%), pneumonia (10%) and eczema herpeticum (3.3%) the commonest associated diagnoses. Cases were defined by primary diagnosis codes, and the data contain no severity or duration [23]. The pediatric guideline lists Taiwanese childhood prevalence of 1.4% (1974), 5.2% (1994) and 11.3% (2019) from different studies and populations, not one series [3]; another Taiwanese paper cites 1.28–6.7% from two cohort studies in 2010 [25]. The journal version of the Taiwanese consensus notes that adult-onset disease more often involves the face, hands and feet and less often the flexures [2].
  • Childhood prevalence in Taiwan depends on the definition: when dermatologists examined schoolchildren aged 6–11 in person, diagnosing AD by itch, a chronic or relapsing course of at least 6 months and typical morphology and distribution, point prevalence was 1.7% in Kaohsiung County in 2004 (4,067 children; 52% response) [34] and 4.33% in Magong, Penghu in 2005 (95% CI 3.63–5.03%; 3,273 children; boys to girls 1.49:1) [35]; both used purposive sampling at a single time point, and the papers contain minor internal inconsistencies (Kaohsiung CI 1.3–2.1% vs 1.1–2.1%; Penghu 4.33% vs 4.32%). At the other extreme, in a national birth cohort of 1,174,941 singletons born in 2006–2012, 26.6% (95% CI 26.5–26.7) had claims for ICD-9-CM 691 before age 5 (≥3 outpatient visits or 1 admission) [33]. These definitions, ages and methods differ, so the figures cannot be compared directly or read as a trend.
  • Parental disease and AD before age 5 in a Taiwanese birth cohort: in the same birth-cohort study (birth registry linked to insurance data; parental disease defined by diagnosis codes within 3 years before delivery; conditional logistic regression), adjusted ORs for AD before age 5, compared with neither parent affected, were 1.474 with maternal AD only, 1.328 with paternal AD only and 2.071 with both; for parental asthma 1.286, 1.211 and 1.494; and 1.243 for maternal psoriasis only, 1.212 for maternal rheumatoid arthritis only and 1.177 for maternal lupus. The intervals in the table are Bonferroni-corrected 99.69% CIs (e.g. 1.443–1.505 for maternal AD). Limitations: code-based definitions with possible misclassification, no way to trace parents' childhood AD, and no smoking or socioeconomic data; these are observational associations [33].
  • In adults with refractory disease prescribed systemic therapy, more than 20% may be misdiagnosed or have a coexisting dermatosis (contact dermatitis, drug eruption, tinea, cutaneous T-cell lymphoma, psoriasis, scabies) [10].

Topical therapy: details and where guidelines differ

  • Topical corticosteroids: supported by the AAD for adults (strong, high certainty) and children (strong, moderate) [4][8]. Maintenance: AAD adults, medium-potency twice weekly (strong, high); children, low-to-medium potency up to three times weekly (strong, moderate); the JTF strongly recommends proactive therapy to flare-prone sites (moderate certainty); UpToDate uses once daily on 2–3 days a week [4][8][11][15]. Once versus twice daily: the JTF conditionally suggests once daily for mid-to-high potency agents (moderate certainty), while the AAD adult guideline notes most trials used twice daily but once daily may suffice, particularly for potent agents [4][11].
  • Topical calcineurin inhibitors: tacrolimus 0.1% outperforms mild steroids, pimecrolimus 1% and tacrolimus 0.03% [1]; strong recommendation from the AAD in adults (high certainty) and in children, including proactive intermittent maintenance (moderate) [4][8]. Cancer risk: the AAD adult guideline notes a possibly increased relative risk of lymphoma with low absolute risk; the JTF, pooling 121 studies with over 3.4 million participants, finds regular use not associated with more cancer (summarized in the patient section) [4][8][11]. The Taiwanese consensus advises against combining them with phototherapy [1].
  • Bleach baths: the JTF conditionally suggests them for moderate-to-severe disease and against them in mild disease (low certainty); the AAD pediatric guideline conditionally recommends them (very low); the Taiwanese consensus sees a role as maintenance in moderate-to-severe disease with frequent bacterial infection, with hypochlorous acid as an alternative [1][8][11]. The AAD adult topical guideline, by contrast, conditionally recommends against topical antiseptics (very low certainty) and mentions bleach baths only for moderate-to-severe disease with signs of secondary bacterial infection [4]. Target about 0.004–0.005% depending on bleach strength: the JTF example is half a cup (120 mL) of 5.25% household bleach in a full 40-gallon tub, a 10-minute soak, rinse off, 2–3 times a week; UpToDate's 0.005% assumes half a cup of 6% bleach in a full tub; Taiwanese products vary in strength, so dilution must follow the label [11][15]. Meta-analysis suggests the benefit may come mainly from the bath itself [1].
  • Antibiotics: topical antimicrobials or antibiotics are not advised without infection [1][4][8]; for oral antibiotics AAD 2024 found insufficient evidence and limits them to clinically evident infection [5]. For bacterial infection in children, flucloxacillin/dicloxacillin is first line and cephalexin is commonly used; Taiwanese data show high rates of MRSA and multidrug-resistant S. aureus in children with AD, for which trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, rifampin, fusidic acid and mupirocin are suitable for S. aureus decolonization [1][2]. Routine skin swabs are not recommended [15].

Systemic therapy: biologics and JAK inhibitors

The AAD 2024 guideline on phototherapy and systemic therapy is the main international reference for adults: strong recommendations for dupilumab, tralokinumab, upadacitinib, abrocitinib and baricitinib (all moderate certainty); conditional recommendations for phototherapy, methotrexate, azathioprine, ciclosporin and mycophenolate (low to very low certainty); and a conditional recommendation against systemic corticosteroids (low certainty) [5]. The newer 2025 focused update adds strong recommendations for lebrikizumab and for nemolizumab with concomitant topical therapy (high certainty), without changing the other 2024 recommendations [6]. All workgroup members favoured dupilumab as first-line systemic agent, and JAK inhibitors, approved in the US after failure of or unsuitability for other systemics, are not generally considered first line [5].

  • Choosing: UpToDate starts with a biologic in most situations, preferring dupilumab, with tralokinumab or lebrikizumab as alternatives after an unsatisfactory dupilumab response and nemolizumab for severe pruritus [16]. JAK inhibitors suit selected patients over 12 without contraindications: seasonal flares, biologic failure or intolerable biologic adverse effects (ocular surface disease, arthritis), intolerable itch, or comorbidities such as alopecia areata or vitiligo for which JAK inhibitors are also indicated; avoid in those over 65 or with cardiovascular risk factors, previous cancer, major cardiovascular events or thrombosis [16].
  • JAK inhibitor safety: based on data from other JAK inhibitors in other populations, the US FDA applied class warnings for serious cardiovascular events, cancer, thrombosis and death; vaccinate against zoster (non-live recombinant vaccine) and give any needed live vaccines before starting, and start at the lower dose, particularly in older adults [5]. In observational data, hazard ratios versus dupilumab were 2.75 (95% CI 1.19–6.38) for pulmonary embolism and 2.54 (1.14–5.64) for deep vein thrombosis; another observational study of 938 patients found more skin infections, acne, cytopenias and hyperlipidaemia but no increase in death, malignancy or cardiovascular events [16].
  • Vaccines: the US dupilumab label advises avoiding live attenuated vaccines (rotavirus, MMR, varicella, live intranasal influenza); a 2024 expert Delphi consensus, based on limited data, considered vaccination including live vaccines safe, and small studies found no serious events, so decide case by case [18]. Complete needed vaccines, including the non-live zoster vaccine, before starting a JAK inhibitor [5][11]. The AAD topical, 2025 update and pediatric guidelines make no vaccine recommendations [4][6][8].
  • Dupilumab points: in late-onset adult disease, exclude early mycosis fungoides before starting [16]; conjunctivitis is usually managed with artificial tears, with ophthalmology referral if severe, persistent or refractory [5]. After 6–12 months of stable remission the interval can be extended; in a multicentre study 83% of 401 adults switched to every 3–4 weeks stayed on the longer interval [16]. See Dupilumab: long-term maintenance and longer intervals.
  • Taiwanese real-world data: among 111 adults at three hospitals (August 2018 to February 2021, at least 16 weeks of observation; called monotherapy in the Discussion, but the methods do not report topical use and the limitations note non-uniform concomitant therapy), EASI-75 was 44.1% and EASI-50 86.5% at month 4; and at 12 months 29/47 (61.7%) reached EASI-75 and 46/47 (97.9%) EASI-50. EASI-75 at month 4 was lower than in the CHRONOS trial (64%; dupilumab plus topical corticosteroids, week 16) [26]. In exploratory analyses, early onset and younger age were associated with better response, and previous conventional systemic drugs and nummular, prurigo and lichenoid phenotypes with poorer response; eosinophils and total IgE did not predict response [26]. Adverse events were mainly allergic conjunctivitis (27 patients) and head and neck redness (19, 17.1%), and the Results report 2 stopping because of severe conjunctivitis and corneal ulcer while the Discussion reports 3 discontinuations for adverse events in total, an internal inconsistency [26]. Limitations: retrospective with possible selection bias, only 47 patients at 12 months, and 59.5% had their interval lengthened after 4–6 months; the authors' ROC-derived cut-off for the number of doses is exploratory and should not be used as dosing advice [26].
  • Severity distribution and burden of severe disease in Taiwanese claims: using 2014–2017 insurance data, Tang et al included 377,423 patients aged 2 or over with at least one AD claim (691.8) in 2015 and, by a prescription algorithm, classified 84.5% as mild, 11.9% as moderate and 3.6% as severe. With an unvalidated algorithm based on local expert advice (moderate: systemic immunosuppressants for 1–89 days and/or ≥6 phototherapy sessions; severe: systemic immunosuppressants for ≥90 days), they defined 897 patients as 'eligible for targeted therapy', 1.5% of moderate-to-severe and 0.24% of all patients; this is not the NHI 13.17 criteria, and no targeted drugs were available and no reimbursement rules had been published in Taiwan during the study. Within 1 year, 91.6% of these 897 used systemic anti-inflammatory drugs (azathioprine 59.1%, systemic corticosteroids 51.4%, methotrexate 43.1%) and 17.1% had phototherapy; mean AD-related direct medical costs were US$506. The study was funded by Eli Lilly, and some authors are company employees [36].

Conventional immunosuppressants, phototherapy and systemic steroids

  • Ciclosporin: Taiwanese consensus 2.5–5 mg/kg/day, complete response expected in about 2 weeks, lowest effective dose for a short period, with EADV advising no more than 2 years of continuous use; AAD 2024 suggests starting at 3–5 mg/kg/day, generally limited to 1 year and preferably less (conditional, low certainty) [1][5]. Monitor blood pressure and creatinine every 2 weeks for 3 months then monthly, plus blood count and liver tests [16]. Do not combine with phototherapy [1].
  • Methotrexate: Taiwanese consensus 5–25 mg weekly, onset in 8–12 weeks, with folic acid; children 10–15 mg/m² weekly [1]. AAD 2024: conditional (low certainty), 10–25 mg weekly [5]. Monitor blood count and liver tests [16].
  • Azathioprine: Taiwanese consensus 2–3 mg/kg/day, with haematological abnormalities more frequent [1]; Taiwanese pediatric guideline 1–2.5 mg/kg/day [3]; the AAD 2024 dosing table gives 2.5–5 mg/kg/day with TPMT genotype or enzyme activity checked first, and conditionally recommends TPMT-guided dosing (low certainty) [5]; NHI also allows lower doses in NUDT15 or TPMT poor metabolizers [27].
  • Mycophenolate mofetil: adults 1–3 g/day, children 20–50 mg/kg/day, onset in 8–12 weeks, inconsistent efficacy [1]; AAD 2024 conditional for refractory disease (very low certainty), up to 3,000 mg daily in two doses [5].
  • Where guidelines differ: in the setting of failure after biologics, the JTF conditionally suggests against azathioprine, methotrexate and mycophenolate and conditionally for switching to ciclosporin [11]; AAD 2024 does not regard any of the four as first line because of lower-certainty evidence, the need for stringent monitoring and lack of US approval for AD [5]. Taiwan's NHI still requires methotrexate, azathioprine and ciclosporin first (at least two of these three; mycophenolate is not included) [27].
  • Phototherapy: medium-dose UVA1 (50 J/cm²) and narrowband UVB have similar efficacy, the former mainly for acute flares and the latter for chronic disease; PUVA carries a higher long-term cancer risk [1]. AAD 2024: conditional (low certainty), with insufficient evidence on PUVA [5]. In children, it is considered from about age 6 in those who can cooperate [18]. For NHI biologic applications, phototherapy must be at least twice weekly for 12 weeks with dose records [27].
  • Taiwanese phototherapy safety data: Ko et al used insurance, cancer registry and death data to compare 1,241 patients with AD aged 20 or over who started UVB (procedure code 51019B) in 2001–2010 with 4,964 patients with AD never treated with phototherapy, matched 4:1 by age, sex, CCI, facility level and year of diagnosis, and followed to 2018 (median 11.0 years); the UVB group had a mean of 23.6 sessions and 134 (10.8%) had more than 50. Skin cancer occurred in 6 vs 25 patients (0.44 vs 0.45 per 1,000 person-years), adjusted HR 0.91 (95% CI 0.35–2.35); nonmelanoma skin cancer 0.80 (0.29–2.26); melanoma 0.80 (0.08–7.64); HR per additional session 0.99 (0.96–1.02). Limitations: few events and wide confidence intervals; claims codes cannot separate narrowband from broadband UVB and carry no dose per session; no smoking or sunscreen data; adults only, mostly Asian patients with Fitzpatrick types II–V, so the findings cannot be extended to children; the adjustment variables listed in the methods and in the table footnotes do not fully match [32].
  • Systemic corticosteroids: the Taiwanese consensus caps the dose at 0.5 mg/kg/day, usually within 1 week and at most 1–2 weeks, with short courses (3–7 days) as a bridge to other systemic therapy [1]; UpToDate's occasional short course for adults and adolescents starts higher (prednisone 40–60 mg/day, then tapered) [15]. AAD 2024 conditionally recommends against them in adults, the AAD pediatric guideline advises against them as a good practice statement, and the JTF conditionally suggests against them [5][8][11].

Special populations: age and pregnancy

  • Age thresholds vary by country and document: baricitinib is 12+ in the AAD pediatric guideline (off-label in the US), 18+ in the JTF and 2+ on the Taiwanese licence; nemolizumab 12+ (AAD) versus 13+ (Japan); pimecrolimus 2+ in the Taiwanese consensus, 3 months+ on the Taiwanese licence and 2+ for NHI [1][8][11][12][27][28]. Follow the Taiwanese label and NHI rules when prescribing.
  • Pediatric systemic data in the 2020 consensus were limited: dupilumab was approved for adults in Taiwan in May 2018 [2], and the US extended it to age 12 and over in March 2019 [1][2]; the Taiwanese licence now covers 6 months+, and the AAD pediatric guideline gives a strong recommendation from 6 months, with less data from 6 months to 2 years [1][8][28]. Pediatric dupilumab is weight-based: 6 months to 5 years, 5 to under 15 kg 200 mg every 4 weeks and 15 to under 30 kg 300 mg every 4 weeks (no loading dose); age 6+, 15 to under 30 kg 600 mg then 300 mg every 4 weeks, 30 to under 60 kg 400 mg then 200 mg every 2 weeks, 60 kg or more as adults [12][18][27].
  • Pregnancy: neither the AAD topical nor pediatric guideline makes pregnancy recommendations, and the topical guideline lists pregnancy and lactation as a research gap [4][8]; Taiwanese consensus, NHI and JAK inhibitor points are in the pregnancy section above.
  • Taiwanese pregnancy and offspring data: Weng et al ran a retrospective, propensity-score-matched cohort using the national insurance database and the Taiwan Maternal and Child Health Database, defining maternal AD as at least 3 diagnoses by dermatologists or rheumatologists within 1 year. Pregnancy outcomes (2003–2019; 15,495 mothers with AD, 77,475 controls), adjusted HRs: threatened abortion 1.14 (95% CI 1.10–1.18), pre-eclampsia/eclampsia 1.13 (1.01–1.27), premature rupture of membranes 1.16 (1.07–1.26), threatened preterm labour 1.16 (1.11–1.20), fetal growth restriction 1.14 (1.04–1.26), antepartum haemorrhage 1.12 (1.00–1.25), postpartum haemorrhage 1.12 (1.02–1.24), anaemia 1.09 (1.04–1.14), surgical complications 1.27 (1.18–1.37), infections 1.10 (1.04–1.15), pulmonary events 1.12 (1.06–1.20) and renal events 1.71 (1.08–2.70); gestational diabetes 1.06 (0.98–1.15), gestational hypertension 1.09 (0.92–1.27), preterm labour 1.12 (1.00–1.27, P = .059) and intrauterine fetal death 1.16 (0.92–1.47) were not significant. Offspring (born 2003–2015; 19,173 vs 95,865): AD 2.29 (2.14–2.45), allergic rhinitis 1.10 (1.07–1.12), asthma 1.05 (1.02–1.08), alopecia areata 1.53 (1.02–2.30) and ADHD 1.15 (1.08–1.22); autism, vitiligo, lupus and others were not significant. Limitations: retrospective design, possible diagnostic misclassification and residual confounding, no data on severity, duration or absolute rates; the pregnancy-outcome models were adjusted for corticosteroid and anti-metabolite use, whereas the offspring models did not include medication use [30].

Work-up of refractory disease (AAD 2026)

The AAD 2026 guideline makes a single good practice statement: in adults with suspected AD unresponsive to management, consider further work-up; it rests on indirect evidence and expert consensus, with no direct studies [10]. First confirm optimized, guideline-based management and adherence [10].

  • Patch testing: comprehensive (expanded) testing for adult-onset dermatitis with atypical distribution (hands, face, eyelids), chronic relapsing dermatitis unresponsive to or worsening on standard therapy, or new or worsening facial dermatitis, ideally before systemic therapy; reading needs several visits over 48–96 hours [10]. The UpToDate author advises patch testing all severe recalcitrant eczema before immunosuppression to avoid false negatives [17].
  • Biopsy: for plaques, tumours, or erythroderma with lymphadenopathy or systemic symptoms; suspected CTCL may need repeated biopsies and reassessment over time; in the literature on misdiagnosed refractory adult "AD", 1–4 biopsies (median 2) were needed to confirm an alternative diagnosis [10]. Median delay to appropriate management was 6 months for contact dermatitis and 24 months for CTCL [10].
  • Other tests: skin scrapings for scabies (sensitivity 43–90%) and tinea (59–91%); bacterial culture or viral PCR if infection is suspected; phototesting; blood count, chemistry, autoimmune serology, flow cytometry or clonality studies only in specific situations [10]. Refer when the picture is unclear, initial tests are inconclusive or a high-risk condition is suspected [10]. Full discussion in Work-up of refractory atopic dermatitis; for adult differentials see also contact dermatitis and prurigo nodularis.

Comorbidities: awareness and counselling

The AAD adult (2022) and pediatric (2026) comorbidity guidelines state associations only and explicitly make no screening or management recommendations; their aim is clinician awareness [7][9].

  • Adults (AAD 2022, 32 statements): clearly associated with asthma, allergic rhinitis, food allergy (high certainty; about 11% of adults with AD), alopecia areata, urticaria, depression and anxiety, osteoporosis (high) and fractures, and skin infection; probably associated, mostly with small effects, with hypertension, coronary artery disease, heart failure, thromboembolism, obesity and dyslipidaemia; associations with myocardial infarction, stroke and others are uncertain or low certainty [7]. The guideline suggests systemic corticosteroids may contribute to the bone findings, and treating AD may relieve depression for some [7].
  • Children (AAD 2026, 29 statements): associated with allergic rhinitis (aOR 3.59), eosinophilic esophagitis (in the reverse direction, about 21.8% of children with eosinophilic esophagitis have AD, pooled from 6 studies), food allergy (23.6%), asthma (21.4%), ADHD (aOR 1.43) and skin infection; probably associated with anxiety (aOR 1.33) and obesity (aOR 1.35); uncertain for depression and autism; probably not associated with suicide or substance use, though adolescents with AD may be more likely to use substances [9].
  • In practice: UpToDate advises vigilance for depression in severe disease with psychiatric referral as needed; large UK data show modest increases in myocardial infarction, heart failure, atrial fibrillation and cardiovascular death in severe adult AD, though these are observational data with possible residual confounding and other studies disagree; the authors consider screening for cardiovascular risk factors possibly appropriate [14]. CTCL risk rises with severity (HR 1.73 mild, 5.60 moderate, 18.09 severe), partly because early lymphoma may be misdiagnosed as severe AD [13].
  • Taiwanese data: in a national insurance cohort of adults aged 20 or over newly diagnosed in 2003–2017 (3 or more outpatient diagnoses within 1 year or a discharge diagnosis by dermatologists or rheumatologists; 142,429 with AD and 142,429 controls), VTE occurred in 1,066 (0.7%) vs 829 (0.6%), 1.05 vs 0.82 per 1,000 person-years, HR 1.28 (95% CI 1.17–1.40); there was no difference between severe and mild AD (HR 1.04, 0.92–1.18), and the association was significant only at age 45 or older. The authors stress that the absolute difference is small and suggest assessment when VTE symptoms occur; severity was defined by systemic treatment use and is unvalidated, and dupilumab and JAK inhibitors were not yet reimbursed during the study period, so they could not be assessed [24]. In a Taiwanese cross-sectional study of 200 adults recruited by severity stratum (so the proportions are not population figures), abnormal anxiety scores (HADS-A ≥11) were found in 43.1%, 33.33% and 18.6% with severe, moderate and mild AD and abnormal depression scores (HADS-D ≥11) in 19.0%, 9.72% and 5.7%; HADS total, anxiety and depression scores correlated positively with SCORAD (r = 0.395, 0.348 and 0.349; P < .001); the authors advise early detection and joint care with psychiatry in moderate-to-severe disease, but causation cannot be shown and HADS is a screening tool [25]. In the insurance data, ORs for allergic rhinitis and asthma among patients with AD were 2.28 (2.25–2.32) and 2.38 (2.33–2.42), and 9.04 (8.81–9.27) for asthma in those with both AD and allergic rhinitis [23].
  • Taiwanese cohort data on depression and anxiety: Cheng et al enrolled 8,208 patients with AD aged 12 or over without prior psychiatric illness from the national insurance database in 1998–2008 (ICD-9-CM 691 or 691.8 by dermatologists, pediatricians or rheumatologists, every diagnosis made at least twice) and age- and sex-matched 1:1 controls, followed to 2011, with outcomes diagnosed by psychiatrists. After adjustment for demographics, asthma, allergic rhinitis and allergic conjunctivitis, HRs were 6.56 (95% CI 3.64–11.84) for major depression, 5.44 (3.99–7.44) for any depressive disorder and 3.57 (2.55–4.98) for anxiety disorders; at 12–17 years, 4.26 (1.39–13.13) for major depression and 5.40 (2.02–14.39) for anxiety, and in adults 7.56 (3.75–15.23) and 3.36 (2.38–4.80). Incidence per 1,000 person-years: major depression 1.42 vs 0.20, any depressive disorder 4.32 vs 0.74, anxiety 2.83 vs 0.70. Limitations: no data on severity, lifestyle or environment; only people who sought care were counted, so incidence may be underestimated; the authors acknowledge wide confidence intervals and limited precision [29].
  • Taiwanese fracture data: Lin et al ran a matched cohort in the LHID 2000/2010 insurance samples (1997–2013; AD defined as more than 3 outpatient primary diagnoses of 691.8 (the limitations section says at least 3) or an admission for AD by dermatologists, immunologists or rheumatologists; 36,855 with AD and 147,420 controls matched 1:4 by age, sex, index date and propensity score; mean age 22.6 years, mean follow-up 6.8 years). Fractures occurred in 4.12% vs 3.78%, with 16-year cumulative incidence 8.043% vs 7.366%; vertebral fractures 0.65% vs 0.52%. In the multivariable model, AD overall had an aHR of 1.03 (95% CI 0.97–1.09, not significant) and moderate-to-severe AD (admission for AD, or ≥30 days a year of phototherapy, systemic corticosteroids ≥10 mg/d or DMARDs) 1.31 (1.08–1.59); in the same model topical corticosteroids 1.21 (1.05–1.39) and systemic corticosteroids 1.62 (1.38–1.91) were associated with higher risk, and DMARDs 0.71 (0.53–0.90) and phototherapy 0.73 (0.56–0.95) with lower risk. The abstract, discussion and conclusion call the 1.31 group 'severe AD' whereas the results and Table 3 say moderate-to-severe, and drug exposure is defined in the methods as use for more than 30 days a year but described in the discussion as systemic steroids above 10 mg/d for longer than 3 months, an internal inconsistency. The authors stress that only an association can be shown; the database lacks BMI, physical activity, diet, smoking and alcohol, and topical steroid potency and treated area could not be estimated [31].

When to refer

  • Persistent disease after 4–8 weeks of appropriate topical therapy, special sites not improving on low-to-mid potency steroids, 4 or more flares a year despite appropriate maintenance, or inability to adhere: refer to dermatology [15].
  • Suspected eczema herpeticum: prompt assessment and early oral or parenteral antivirals [15]. Persistent eye symptoms or vision change: ophthalmology [5][14]. Marked depression or anxiety: psychiatry [14].
  • Refractory disease in infants and young children: pediatric dermatology or pediatric allergy [18]. Moderate-to-severe disease uncontrolled despite standard care with aeroallergen (e.g. house dust mite) sensitization: allergy referral for immunotherapy (JTF conditional, moderate certainty; SCORAD-50 in 40% vs 26%, with more adverse events) [11][19].
