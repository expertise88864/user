Key points in 30 seconds

Key points
  • Acne is a chronic inflammation of the hair follicle and its oil gland: more oil, plugged pores, overgrowth of acne bacteria and inflammation act together. Vigorous scrubbing and squeezing can worsen inflammation and scarring [1][3][4].
  • The 2024 Taiwanese consensus grades acne from 0 to 4. Milder acne is treated mainly with topical retinoids and benzoyl peroxide (BPO); more severe acne adds oral antibiotics or hormonal tablets; oral isotretinoin is an option for severe, scarring, distressing or repeatedly relapsing acne [1].
  • Antibiotics are never used alone: topical antibiotics go with BPO or a topical retinoid, and oral antibiotics are generally limited to 3–4 months to limit resistance [1][2].
  • Oral isotretinoin works well but causes serious birth defects: strict contraception is needed from 1 month before, throughout, and for 1 month after treatment. On depression and inflammatory bowel disease, larger observational studies mostly show no clear increase, but the evidence is of low certainty and case reports exist; a subgroup treated for 1 year or more showed a higher bowel-disease risk, and causation has not been established. Watch for mood or bowel symptoms during treatment and tell your doctor [1][2][6].
  • Most topical treatments take 8–12 weeks to show results, and maintenance is needed afterwards; the earlier inflammation is controlled, the less scarring is likely [4][7][10].
  • Evidence on diet is limited and inconsistent: milk has only observational (association) data, randomised trials of low-glycaemic diets conflict, and an effect of chocolate has not been proven; strict self-imposed diets are not needed [2][3][4].

What is acne, and how common is it?

Acne vulgaris (pimples, spots) is a chronic inflammatory disease of the pilosebaceous unit, the hair follicle and its oil gland. It favours the face, neck, chest, upper back and upper arms [2][3]. It can start in primary school and often continues into the 20s and 30s [1][7].

Four mechanisms drive acne [1][3]: (1) more sebum, as androgens from puberty onward stimulate the oil glands; (2) plugged follicles, as skin cells build up at the follicle opening to form an invisible microcomedo, the starting point of every acne lesion; (3) growth of Cutibacterium acnes (formerly Propionibacterium acnes), a normal skin bacterium that multiplies in the oily, low-oxygen, plugged follicle; and (4) inflammation, which makes the area red and swollen. If the follicle wall ruptures, its contents spill into the dermis and form a deep, painful nodule that is more likely to scar. The order in which these steps occur is still unclear [3].

How common is it?

Taiwanese data: a National Health Insurance database study (2000–2013; a patient had to be diagnosed by a board-certified dermatologist at least three times; 194,529 patients) found that acne prevalence rose from 2.11% in 2000 to 2.88% in 2013. Women outnumbered men at every age, the peak was 12–25 years, and acne in children under 12 rose about fourfold over the 13 years [11]. Only treated, repeatedly diagnosed acne is counted, so mild or untreated acne is missed and the figure cannot be compared directly with surveys from other countries [11].

Data from other countries (not Taiwanese): the Taiwanese consensus cites that about 85% of adolescents have had acne [1], and UpToDate gives adolescent prevalence from 35% to over 90% [3]. In a Dutch study of 4,561 13-year-olds, 54% had visible acne (girls 62%, boys 45%), and moderate-to-severe acne affected 14% of girls and 9% of boys [18]. Adults are often affected: in a study of 2,895 women, about half of women in their 20s, a quarter in their 30s and over 10% in their 40s still had clinically significant acne [7].

Acne's impact is often underestimated. Guidelines note that its effect on quality of life is comparable to chronic diseases such as asthma, psoriasis and arthritis, and that it is associated with anxiety, depression, low self-esteem, stigma and bullying [1][2]. A systematic review of European and North American studies found the burden greatest in women, adults and people with more severe acne [17]. Even acne that looks mild to a doctor deserves treatment if it is causing distress [5][7].

Why does acne happen, and what makes it worse?

Acne results from several factors acting together, and most people have no single cause. The factors below are those named by guidelines and UpToDate; many are associations, not proven causes [1][3][7].

  • Age, family history and oily skin: the Taiwanese consensus lists family history, entering puberty, overweight or obesity and oily skin as risk factors [1]; people with an affected first-degree relative have more than three times the risk [3]. Studies on body weight are inconsistent [3].
  • Hormones: androgens stimulate the oil glands, yet most people with acne have normal hormone levels [3][7]. Women who also have irregular periods, excess hair growth, androgenic hair loss or a sudden severe flare should be assessed for androgen excess such as polycystic ovary syndrome [2][7]. Up to 83% of women notice premenstrual flares [7].
  • Skin care, make-up and hair products: products containing comedogenic ingredients can trigger comedones (acne cosmetica), and oily hair products can cause forehead acne (pomade acne) [3][7]. UpToDate notes that in the US "noncomedogenic" labelling is not regulated and may not always be accurate [4].
  • Friction and pressure: prolonged rubbing or pressure from turtlenecks, bra straps, shoulder pads, helmets or casts can trigger acne mechanica [3].
  • Squeezing, picking and scrubbing: vigorous scrubbing with soaps, exfoliants or astringents can rupture comedones and worsen inflammation, and squeezing raises the risk of scarring [3][7].
  • Medicines: corticosteroids, lithium, phenytoin, some targeted cancer drugs (EGFR inhibitors), JAK inhibitors, progestin-only contraceptives, androgens or testosterone, and vitamins B2, B6 and B12 can cause drug-induced acne, typically a uniform eruption of inflamed papules rather than the mix of lesion stages seen in ordinary acne [3][8]. If you suspect a medicine, talk to the prescriber rather than stopping it yourself.
  • Stress: some studies link perceived stress, for example around exams, to more severe acne, although sebum output did not track stress [3].
  • Smoking: some studies link smoking to adult female acne, others do not, and causation is uncertain [7].
  • Diet: evidence is limited; see the diet section below.

Types of acne lesions and special forms

Acne lesions are non-inflamed or inflamed [1][3]. Comedones are non-inflamed: a closed comedo (whitehead) is a small skin-coloured or white bump; an open comedo (blackhead) has a dilated opening with a dark centre, coloured by packed skin cells, oxidised oils and melanin rather than dirt [3]. Inflamed lesions include red, firm papules; pustules with a white-yellow head; and larger, deeper, often painful nodules. Comedones and ordinary papules and pustules are usually under 5 mm [3]. What many people call "cystic acne" is mostly nodular; true cysts are rare [3].

Lesions at different stages usually coexist, and the presence of comedones is a key clue that the problem is acne [3]. The Taiwanese consensus cites that up to 61% of patients also have acne on the trunk [1]. Acne is diagnosed by examination; most people need no blood tests or biopsy [2][3].

Special forms

  • Neonatal and infantile acne: neonatal acne appears in the first weeks of life without comedones and clears within a month or two [8]. Infantile acne starts later (sources differ: 3–6 months or 6–16 months), mostly on the cheeks, and usually clears by about age 1, at most by 2; up to half of affected babies are left with small pitted scars, so a doctor's assessment is worthwhile [3][8].
  • Children aged 1–7: androgen levels are very low at this age, so acne is unusual and calls for assessment for a hormonal disorder [3][4][8].
  • Pre-teens (7–12 years): often the first sign of puberty, mainly comedones on the forehead, nose and chin (T-zone) [8].
  • Adult female acne: acne at 25 or older. Most often it is adolescent acne that never settled; true late onset is less common [7]. It often involves the lower face and jaw with premenstrual flares, yet only about 11% have lesions limited to the jaw, about half have truncal acne, and severity is usually mild to moderate [3][7].
  • Drug-, cosmetic- or hair-product-induced acne: see the previous section [3][7].
  • Acne excoriée: mild lesions are repeatedly picked, leaving erosions and scars out of proportion to the acne; it can be linked to psychological distress and may need psychotherapy or medication [3][7].
  • Acne conglobata: a severe nodular form, mostly in young men, on the back, chest and buttocks, with draining lesions, sinus tracts and severe scarring [3].
  • Acne fulminans is a red flag: sudden crops of large, painful nodules that ulcerate and crust, sometimes with fever, malaise, or bone and joint pain. It is rare, mainly affects adolescent males with existing acne, and can be triggered by starting isotretinoin. Seek care promptly [3][5].

Conditions that can look like acne

Rosacea usually has no comedones but has visible small blood vessels (see our rosacea guide); perioral dermatitis forms small red papules around the mouth, usually sparing a rim next to the lip (see perioral dermatitis); Pityrosporum folliculitis ("fungal acne") gives uniform small papules and pustules on the chest and back; sudden pustules around the nose after long antibiotic courses suggest Gram-negative folliculitis; red papules and pustules in the beard area after shaving may be pseudofolliculitis barbae, caused by short curled hairs growing back into the skin; and recurrent boils in the armpits or groin may be hidradenitis suppurativa [1][2][3]. These need different treatment, so if acne treatment is not working, ask your doctor to re-check the diagnosis [4].

How is severity graded?

There is no universal acne grading system. The 2024 Taiwanese consensus uses the investigator's global assessment (IGA), grading acne from 0 to 4, and links treatment choices to the grade [1][2]:

  • Grade 0: no inflamed or non-inflamed lesions [1].
  • Grade 1: nearly normal skin with very few comedones or a few small inflamed lesions [1].
  • Grade 2: some comedones plus a few papules or pustules, no nodules [1].
  • Grade 3: many comedones, possibly some inflamed lesions, and no more than one small nodule [1].
  • Grade 4: many comedones and inflamed lesions plus some nodules [1].

Note that two tables in the consensus give the same numeric grades different labels (the grading table calls grades 1–4 mild, moderate, severe and very severe; the treatment table calls them almost clear, mild, moderate and severe), so this article refers to the numeric grade throughout [1].

Grading is not only about counts. If acne is unusually extensive, already scarring, keeps relapsing, or clearly affects daily life or mood, the doctor can move it up a grade and treat more actively [1][3]. The AAD guideline likewise says patients with psychosocial burden or scarring should be considered as having severe acne [2]. The Taiwanese consensus notes that a lesion takes about three months from comedo to resolution or scar, a window worth using to prevent scarring [1].

Treatment principles and the treatment ladder

The aims are less oil, fewer comedones, less inflammation and no scars [1]. The Taiwanese consensus favours combining two or more topical drugs with different mechanisms, which works better and reduces resistance, and choosing treatment by the extent, site and severity of acne together with the patient's budget, preferences and risk tolerance, in shared decision-making [1].

Expectations matter: most treatments need 2–3 months of steady use before their effect can be judged, and early results mean fewer lesions, not complete clearance. Most acne treatments suppress rather than cure, so a topical retinoid is usually continued as maintenance to prevent relapse [1][4].

The Taiwanese ladder [1]: grade 1 starts with a topical retinoid alone, with BPO or azelaic acid alone as alternatives; grade 2 uses a topical retinoid, BPO or both (separately or as a fixed combination), then a topical antibiotic plus BPO (with or without a retinoid); grade 3 starts with a topical retinoid plus BPO, then an oral antibiotic, combined pill or spironolactone with topicals, or oral isotretinoin; grade 4 starts with an oral antibiotic, combined pill or spironolactone with topicals, then oral isotretinoin.

The consensus allows two adjustments. Isotretinoin may be used earlier, after a full discussion of cost and risks, when acne causes severe scarring, affects daily life or mood, relapses after stopping, comes with heavy oiliness, or the patient wants active treatment. Intralesional corticosteroid injection can be added for widespread papules or nodules, rapid relief of pain and swelling, or scar risk [1]. Extensive nodulocystic acne and acne conglobata fall outside the consensus and need individual management [1].

Not working? Check three things first

Topical treatment usually brings improvement within 12 weeks, though not necessarily complete clearance [4]. Before concluding that a treatment has failed, UpToDate advises checking three things [4]:

  • Regular use for long enough: treatment often fails because it was not used consistently for long enough or was stopped early because of irritation; many acne medicines need 8–12 weeks of steady use to judge [4][7]. At follow-up, describe exactly how you use each product every day; this reveals more than simply saying whether you used it daily [4].
  • The right diagnosis: Pityrosporum folliculitis or Demodex folliculitis can be mistaken for acne and will not respond to acne treatment [4].
  • An underlying condition: for example androgen excess, which can make acne resistant to treatment [4].

Once these are checked, a different or more active treatment may be needed [4].

After improvement: maintenance

Topical acne treatments suppress rather than cure, so maintenance is usually needed; both the Taiwanese consensus and UpToDate use a topical retinoid for maintenance because it prevents microcomedones and relapse [1][4]. UpToDate's author switches to a topical retinoid alone once improvement has been stable and satisfactory for at least 3–6 months on combined topicals, adds a BPO-containing product if the retinoid alone is not enough, and avoids long-term antibiotics because of resistance [4].

Topical treatments: choosing and using them

Topical retinoids

Topical retinoids clear comedones, reduce inflammation and improve post-acne pigmentation, and are internationally accepted first-line drugs [1][2]. Common agents are tretinoin, adapalene, tazarotene and trifarotene, and a topical isotretinoin gel is also available in Taiwan; efficacy does not differ significantly between them, and adapalene is less irritating [1][21]. Adapalene is sold over the counter in the US, but every adapalene product we found in Taiwan is prescription-only, whereas some tretinoin and topical isotretinoin products are pharmacy-available "indicated" medicines [4][21]; UpToDate lists topical isotretinoin gel as contraindicated in both pregnancy and breastfeeding [4].

  1. After washing, dry the face; waiting about 20 minutes before applying can reduce irritation [4].
  2. Use about a pea-sized amount and spread a thin layer over the whole affected area (for example the whole face), not just on spots [4].
  3. Apply in the evening. Start every second or third night and build up to nightly over a few weeks [4].
  4. Use a moisturiser, and protect the skin from the sun during the day to reduce sunburn [1][2][4].

Burning, dryness, redness and peeling at the start are common and expected; they usually ease within 1–2 weeks and are most noticeable in the first month. If irritation is strong, use it less often rather than stopping [1][4]. While using a retinoid, avoid harsh soaps, toners, astringents, and alpha-hydroxy or salicylic acid products, which add irritation; tazarotene can also be used as short-contact therapy, applied for up to 5 minutes daily and then washed off, to reduce irritation [4]. In darker skin prone to dark marks, irritation itself can leave dark marks, so start with a lower strength and fewer applications, and choose gentler creams or lotions or a short-contact BPO wash [4]. Conventional tretinoin should not be applied at the same time as BPO (BPO in the morning, tretinoin at night; UpToDate notes that some newer tretinoin formulations are exceptions); adapalene, tazarotene and trifarotene can be used with BPO [2][4]. In pregnancy other topicals are preferred, and tazarotene is contraindicated [1][2][4].

Benzoyl peroxide (BPO)

BPO kills C. acnes, gently clears comedones and reduces inflammation, works on both inflamed and non-inflamed lesions, and no bacterial resistance to it has been reported [1][2]. Strengths range from 2.5% to 10%; start low. UpToDate's authors typically use 2.5%, as higher strengths may add little benefit but more irritation [1][4]. Test on a small area first. The US FDA also warns of rare serious allergic reactions to over-the-counter BPO and salicylic acid products and advises applying to one or two small areas for the first 3 days; throat tightness, breathing difficulty or swelling of the face or lips needs urgent care [1][4][9]. BPO bleaches hair, clothing, towels and pillowcases; wash-off and water-based forms are often better tolerated [1][2]. Improvement shows from about 3 weeks, with the full effect at 8–12 weeks [4].

Topical antibiotics

In Taiwan the usual topical antibiotics are clindamycin (first line) and fusidic acid (second line) [1]. The Taiwanese consensus puts topical retinoids and BPO first and adds a topical antibiotic only when those cannot be used, do not work or are not tolerated; topical antibiotics should not be used alone or long term, but together with BPO or a retinoid to prevent resistance [1][2]. They are generally not added while you are taking an oral antibiotic [5].

Azelaic acid, salicylic acid and others

Azelaic acid clears comedones, is antibacterial and anti-inflammatory, and fades post-acne pigmentation. In a 92-patient trial of 20% cream twice daily, about 28% more patients than with placebo had a 50–100% fall in lesions at 3 months [1][2]. The AAD guideline sees it as particularly useful for sensitive skin and darker skin types prone to dark marks, and no fetal harm is expected in pregnancy [2]. Salicylic acid (0.5–2%) is regulated in Taiwan as a special-purpose cosmetic; it is weaker than first-line drugs, and the Taiwanese consensus recommends it only as an add-on [1].

The topical antiandrogen clascoterone is approved in the US but not licensed in Taiwan [1][21], and we found no Taiwanese licence for topical dapsone gel [21]. Evidence is insufficient to recommend sulfur, glycolic acid, tea tree oil, green tea, zinc or niacinamide [2][9]. For the differences between retinoids, retinol and acids, see our guide to topical acids.

Morning and evening routine, choice of vehicle, and back acne

The example below is UpToDate's, for mild papulopustular facial acne treated with a topical retinoid and BPO; follow your own prescription [4]:

  • Morning: wash with a BPO cleanser, or wash with a gentle cleanser and apply a thin layer of BPO gel to the whole face [4].
  • Evening: wash with a gentle cleanser, dry the face, and apply a thin layer of the topical retinoid to the whole face [4].
  • How often: a topical retinoid once daily (evening); BPO usually once daily (UpToDate's drug table says once or twice daily); topical clindamycin once or twice daily, together with BPO; azelaic acid twice daily [4].

Choosing a vehicle: the right vehicle helps you keep using the treatment and depends on skin type and preference [4]. Some gels are drying and suit oily skin; creams and lotions are more moisturising; solutions are drying but spread easily over large areas such as the trunk; foams suit hairy areas; and some people use fixed combination products to simplify the routine [4].

Chest and back acne: the back is hard to reach. For mild papulopustular acne on the trunk, UpToDate's authors often start with a BPO wash (with or without a topical antibiotic) if the patient can reach the area, and mention applicators that help with the back; because truncal acne is hard to treat topically, they consider oral treatment earlier [4].

Oral antibiotics

Oral antibiotics are used for moderate-to-severe acne, usually when topicals have not worked or acne covers the trunk and other areas [1]. The Taiwanese consensus makes doxycycline (and tetracycline) first line; minocycline (more serious side effects) and erythromycin (high resistance in Taiwan) are second line [1]. Three rules: always combine with topical BPO or a retinoid, never take the antibiotic alone, and keep the course under 3–4 months [1][2]. A topical retinoid then maintains the result [4][5].

  • Doxycycline: take with food and plenty of water and stay upright afterwards to reduce stomach upset and oesophagitis; it makes skin sun-sensitive, so use sun protection [1][2].
  • Tetracycline: food, especially dairy, reduces absorption, so take it on an empty stomach; avoid in liver or kidney impairment [1][5].
  • Minocycline: may cause dizziness, blue-grey skin pigmentation, hepatitis or a lupus-like reaction [1][2].
  • Rare but important: tetracyclines can rarely cause raised intracranial pressure [5]; headache with new vision changes needs prompt medical assessment [6].
  • Tetracyclines are not for pregnancy, breastfeeding or young children: the Taiwanese consensus bars tetracycline during breastfeeding and in children aged 8 and under, while allowing doxycycline conditionally after medical assessment; the AAD guideline contraindicates the whole class in pregnancy, breastfeeding and below age 9 during tooth development, with no exception for doxycycline; the UpToDate management topic withholds it up to age 8, while its paediatric topic uses doxycycline from age 8 (see the professional section). Leave the decision for a child to the doctor; do not give these drugs to children yourself [1][2][5].
  • Do not combine with oral isotretinoin, which raises the risk of intracranial hypertension [1][6]. There is no evidence that tetracyclines reduce the effectiveness of combined pills (rifampin does) [2][5].

Why limit the course? Long-term antibiotics breed resistance in acne bacteria and other microbes and are associated with pharyngitis, gut infection, vaginal candidiasis and inflammatory bowel disease [2][5]. The Taiwanese consensus also explicitly advises against co-trimoxazole for acne because of serious reactions such as Stevens-Johnson syndrome and severe liver injury [1].

Oral isotretinoin: what patients need to know

Isotretinoin is a vitamin A derivative that shrinks the oil glands and sharply cuts oil output, normalises follicle keratinisation, and reduces C. acnes and inflammation. It is the only acne drug acting on all four mechanisms and is considered the only one that may change the long-term course of acne [5][6].

Who is it for?

The Taiwanese consensus uses it for severe, mainly nodulocystic acne, or for severe acne that has not responded to 6–8 weeks of an oral antibiotic plus topical retinoid and BPO. For less severe acne it may also be considered, after discussing cost and risks, when acne causes severe scarring, affects daily life or mood, relapses after stopping, comes with heavy oiliness, or the patient wants active treatment [1]. The AAD guideline likewise recommends it for severe or treatment-resistant acne and counts psychosocial burden or scarring as severe [2]. The Taiwanese consensus does not recommend it for children aged 12 or under, because long-term use before puberty may cause premature epiphyseal closure, hyperostosis or osteoporosis [1]. In Taiwan, oral isotretinoin can be prescribed only by dermatologists; currently licensed products include Roaccutane, Isotretor and Ozyn [20][21].

Before starting, tell your doctor about any of the following; UpToDate considers that these patients need extra counselling or closer monitoring [6]:

  • Previous contraceptive failure; uncontrolled depression or suicidal thoughts; high blood lipids or a family history of early high lipids; a personal or family history of inflammatory bowel disease; eye problems (especially dry eyes or contact lenses); heavy drinking; or extreme physical activity [6].
  • Allergy: people allergic to isotretinoin or its ingredients must not take it, and UpToDate notes that isotretinoin capsules contain soybean oil and should be used with caution in severe soy allergy [6].

Dose, duration and how to take it

The Taiwanese consensus suggests starting at 0.25–0.5 mg per kg of body weight per day, adjusting to tolerance, with a target of 0.5–1.0 mg/kg/day for 4–6 months; severe acne can be treated until fully clear, and mild-to-moderate acne can use a low dose of 0.1–0.5 mg/kg/day [1]. "Intermittent" schedules (one week a month) work less well [1][2]. Take it with food, as fat improves absorption [1][6]. Improvement usually appears after 1–2 months. A few people have a brief flare in the first month, which usually settles as treatment continues, so see your doctor rather than stopping on your own; but sudden crops of many painful, ulcerating nodules or fever need prompt care (see acne fulminans) [1][3][6]. It can be stopped abruptly without tapering [6]. Other acne medicines are usually stopped when isotretinoin starts; isotretinoin makes the skin dry, thin and fragile, and topical acne treatments are often poorly tolerated [6]. UpToDate says follow-up is typically monthly to check response and side effects (also required by the US iPLEDGE programme) [6].

"Cumulative dose" means the total taken over a course (mg per kg). Sources differ: UpToDate aims for 120–150 mg/kg to reduce relapse; Taiwan's NHI rules cap a reimbursed course at 100–120 mg/kg over 4–6 months; the Taiwanese consensus and the AAD guideline set no total [1][2][6][20]. Follow your doctor's dosing rather than adjusting it yourself.

Common side effects and how to manage them

  • Dry lips and skin: the commonest effects; skin effects make up nearly 65% of reported side effects, led by chapped lips and dry skin. Use lip balm (such as petroleum jelly), a gentle cleanser and moisturiser [1][6].
  • Dry eyes and nosebleeds: use artificial tears (contact lenses may become uncomfortable), and saline spray or petroleum jelly for a dry nose; report reduced night vision [6].
  • Sun sensitivity: use a moisturiser with SPF 30 or higher daily and reapply every 2–3 hours outdoors [6].
  • Muscle aches: commoner with vigorous exercise; if you exercise heavily, discuss it with your doctor, and report severe muscle pain [6].
  • Blood lipids and liver tests: abnormal triglycerides occur in about 7–39% and abnormal cholesterol in 7–27%, usually settling with dose changes or after treatment, which is why blood tests are done before and during treatment [1][6]. The Taiwanese consensus advises extra caution in people with obesity, high lipids or liver disease, and the NHI consent form states that hepatic or renal insufficiency is a contraindication [1][20].
  • Temporary hair shedding, brittle nails and fragile skin, which usually recover after stopping [2][6].

Pregnancy: the single most important rule

Isotretinoin causes severe birth defects (face and skull, heart, thymus, central nervous system) and miscarriage: after exposure in early pregnancy about 20% miscarry and about 20–30% of babies born have malformations; although data are limited, children who look physically normal may still have higher rates of intellectual disability and impaired neuropsychological function [6]. Anyone who could become pregnant must [1][20]:

  • Take a pregnancy test before starting; if unsure, wait until the next period begins [1][20].
  • Use strict contraception from 1 month before, throughout, and for 1 month after treatment, ideally two methods at once; the NHI consent form says from 4 weeks before until at least 4 weeks after [1][20]. The AAD guideline suggests long-acting reversible methods (such as an intrauterine device or implant) as first choice where suitable [2].
  • Have regular pregnancy tests and record the date of each last period [1]; UpToDate also advises a final pregnancy test one month after the course ends [6].
  • If pregnancy occurs, stop immediately and tell your doctor [6][20].

Isotretinoin taken by men has not been linked to fetal abnormalities [6]. Everyone, male or female, must not donate blood during treatment and for at least 4 weeks afterwards, and must never share the pills [6][20].

Depression and bowel disease: what the evidence says

Mood changes, anxiety and even suicide have been reported sporadically during treatment, but larger studies have not confirmed a higher risk, and the relationship remains uncertain [2][6]. The AAD guideline's pooled analysis of 4 observational studies gave a relative risk of 0.88 (95% confidence interval 0.77–1.00), but AAD rates this evidence as low certainty with substantial heterogeneity and notes that case reports with improvement on stopping and recurrence on restarting suggest a possible causal link [2]. In a meta-analysis of 24 cohort studies cited by UpToDate, the absolute risks of suicide, suicide attempt, suicidal ideation and self-harm were each below 0.5%; there was no rise in suicide during treatment and a lower risk of suicide attempts 2–4 years afterwards [6]. Acne itself is linked to depression, and in one study the risk was unchanged after excluding isotretinoin users [6].

Taiwanese data: a National Health Insurance cohort study comparing 9,981 acne patients who took isotretinoin with 19,962 who did not found an adjusted hazard ratio for psychiatric disorders of 1.009, that is, no increase [13]. However, it included only adults aged 20 or over (no teenagers), and although it analysed suicide-related diagnoses (adjusted hazard ratio 0.982) it did not analyse suicidal ideation, and may have missed self-paid courses, which are common in Taiwan [12][13].

In practice, your doctor assesses mood before starting and follows it during treatment. If you feel persistently low, anxious, sleep badly or have thoughts of self-harm, tell your doctor or family at once; treatment may be stopped and help arranged [1][2][6]. For inflammatory bowel disease, the AAD guideline's pooled relative risk from 7 studies was 1.13, not a significant increase [2]; a meta-analysis cited by UpToDate was also non-significant overall, but a subgroup treated for a year or more had a relative risk of 1.41, so the cautious wording is "reported, but a causal link has not been proven"; report persistent bowel symptoms during treatment [1][6].

Things to avoid during treatment

  • Tetracycline antibiotics (doxycycline, minocycline and others): combining them raises the risk of intracranial hypertension [1][6].
  • Vitamin A supplements, which can add to side effects [6].
  • Heavy drinking: alcohol is not forbidden, but excess affects triglycerides and liver tests, as well as judgement and contraception [6].
  • Headache with new vision changes: this may signal raised intracranial pressure, so contact your doctor at once; specialist assessment is needed and isotretinoin should be stopped [6].

Can I have lasers or peels during treatment?

A 6-month wait after isotretinoin used to be standard, but an expert panel found insufficient evidence to delay superficial peels, laser hair removal, fractional lasers or minor surgery; mechanical dermabrasion and traditional ablative laser should still be avoided after recent isotretinoin: the AAD guideline cites advice against full-face dermabrasion, mechanical (rotary) dermabrasion, and ablative laser of the whole face or non-facial areas within 6 months, and the UpToDate scar topic says dermabrasion usually waits 6–12 months after the course [2][6][10]. Another UpToDate topic still says patients on oral retinoids should not have chemical peels, so sources disagree [9]. Discuss any procedure with your dermatologist first.

Will acne come back after the course?

The Taiwanese consensus reports relapse in about 21–30% of patients and advises a topical retinoid for at least several months after stopping (plus BPO for those who had moderate or worse acne) [1]. In a 19,907-patient study cited by UpToDate, 23% relapsed, after a median of 7.5 months. Acne can keep improving for months after the course, so at least 5 months should pass before considering a second course, which works about as well as the first [6]. Truncal acne, starting before 16, polycystic ovary syndrome and, in women, no antiandrogen therapy predict relapse [6].

NHI coverage and self-pay

Under the NHI drug rules, oral isotretinoin is restricted to dermatologists; the consent form describes severe cystic and nodular acne unresponsive to other treatments, so the consensus options for earlier use in less severe acne may not qualify. Reimbursement needs a signed consent form plus records of failed conventional treatment or pre-treatment photos, with prior approval before it is reimbursed; each application covers one course, and a further course needs a new application with the original and recent photos [20]. Taiwanese researchers note that most isotretinoin use is in practice self-paid [12]. Clinicians can find more in our clinical summary of isotretinoin.

Hormonal treatment: combined pills and spironolactone

Hormonal treatment is only for women and girls after their first period (if the pill is only for acne, it is best delayed until a year after the first period; see below); it can work even when hormone levels are normal, and it is not used before puberty [5][7][8].

Combined oral contraceptive pills

The Taiwanese consensus suggests them for women with post-pubertal moderate or worse acne, signs of androgen excess (acne on the lower face, irregular periods, overweight, excess hair, marked oiliness or androgenic hair loss), a need for contraception (for example during isotretinoin), or poor response or quick relapse after antibiotics or isotretinoin [1]; the AAD guideline notes that benefit is not limited to these groups [2]. Results usually take 3–6 months; the Taiwanese consensus suggests a 6–12-month course with topicals [1][2]. Blood pressure is checked before starting and at follow-up, pregnancy is excluded, and a pelvic examination is not required [1][2]. Because of concerns about growth and bone density, UpToDate advises girls to wait until a year after the first period before using the pill for acne when there is no other reason for it; if there is another need, such as contraception, the doctor decides case by case [4].

Not suitable (absolute contraindications in the Taiwanese consensus): pregnancy; breastfeeding within 6 weeks of birth; age over 35 with more than 15 cigarettes a day; previous clots or pulmonary embolism; stroke; ischaemic heart disease; hypertension with cardiovascular disease; major surgery with prolonged bed rest; complicated valve disease; migraine; current breast cancer; diabetic complications; and severe cirrhosis, acute viral hepatitis or malignant liver tumours [1]. For migraine, the Taiwanese consensus includes migraine with or without aura, whereas the AAD guideline lists only migraine with aura as absolute [1][2]. This is the Taiwanese consensus list and is not exhaustive: the AAD guideline's smoking threshold is "age 35 or older and 15 or more cigarettes a day", and it lists further conditions; your doctor checks blood pressure and asks about your medical history before starting [1][2]. Progestin-only pills, injections, intrauterine devices or implants may make acne worse [2][7].

Among Taiwanese licences, some drospirenone/ethinyl estradiol pills list "moderate acne in women who want contraception and choose an oral contraceptive"; cyproterone/ethinyl estradiol products are licensed for moderate-to-severe androgen-sensitive acne or hirsutism in women of childbearing age, only after topical or systemic antibiotic treatment has failed, and some of these licences add that, although contraceptive, the product must not be used for contraception alone or combined with other hormonal contraceptives; most other pills are licensed for contraception only [21].

Spironolactone

Spironolactone is a diuretic and blood-pressure drug that blunts the effect of androgens on the oil glands. Its Taiwanese licence does not list acne, and the US FDA has not approved it for acne, so this is off-label use [2][21]; nonetheless, both the Taiwanese consensus and the AAD guideline list it as an option for women, with efficacy comparable to oral antibiotics [1][2]. The Taiwanese consensus gives a common dose of 100–200 mg a day and notes that 25–50 mg a day has worked in studies; UpToDate's authors usually start at 25–50 mg and use 50–100 mg a day [1][4][5]. Its effect is judged after 3–6 months [5].

Side effects include irregular periods, breast tenderness, frequent urination, dizziness, fatigue, headache and raised potassium [1]. Healthy young women generally need no routine potassium tests; older people, those taking ACE inhibitors, ARBs, NSAIDs or digoxin, and those with hypertension, diabetes or chronic kidney disease should consider having potassium checked and avoid a high-potassium diet [1][2]. UpToDate's authors also suggest periodic checks in healthy women over 45 [5]. It may feminise a male fetus, so it must not be used in pregnancy; contraception is needed and the drug is stopped at once if pregnancy occurs. Combining it with a combined pill reduces irregular bleeding [2][5]. More on safety: is spironolactone safe in dermatology?

Injections, extraction, peels, lasers and light: how strong is the evidence?

Corticosteroid injection into a lesion: an add-on for large, painful papules or nodules, which usually flatten within 48–72 hours [9]; a low concentration limits local dents and pale patches: triamcinolone 2.5–5 mg/mL in the Taiwanese consensus and AAD, 1.25–2.5 mg/mL in the UpToDate light-therapy topic [1][2][9].

Comedo extraction: the AAD guideline found insufficient evidence to recommend it; UpToDate treats it as an adjunct for stubborn comedones performed by clinicians, still with a scarring risk, so do not squeeze at home [2][9]. Chemical peels (glycolic, salicylic and others): evidence is insufficient; they suit mainly comedonal acne, need repeated sessions, give mild and temporary benefit, and can leave dark marks on darker skin [2][9].

Lasers and light: the AAD guideline found insufficient evidence to recommend pulsed dye and other lasers, intense pulsed light, blue/red light or photodynamic therapy, and made a conditional recommendation against adding pneumatic broadband light to adapalene [2]. UpToDate likewise notes a lack of high-quality trials [9]. A 2025 JAAD clinical review rated the evidence for blue and red LED as weak; the new 1726 nm sebaceous-gland laser reduced inflamed lesions by more than half in 87% of 104 patients at 26 weeks in an uncontrolled study, but randomised and long-term data are lacking [9][19]. These devices are an adjunct or alternative for people whose medicines have failed or who prefer not to use them, not a first-line treatment [19]. Further reading: our guide to dermatology lasers.

Scars, red marks and dark marks

Acne can leave three kinds of marks, each handled differently [3][4][10]: dark marks (post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation) are flat and brown, commoner in darker skin, and fade slowly over months or longer; red marks (post-inflammatory erythema) are flat, pink to red, commoner in lighter skin, and can last months to years; and scars are changes in skin texture (depressed or raised) that do not go away on their own.

A JAAD review cites data that post-acne dark marks persist in 65.2% of patients after 1 year and 22.3% after 5 years [16]. For dark marks: daily sun protection, a topical retinoid or azelaic acid, and, if needed, hydroquinone or careful superficial peels under medical supervision [4].

Prevention comes first: control inflammation early and avoid squeezing or picking; new firm red patches can be early scars, so mention them to your doctor [10][16]. Timing: get active acne under control before treating scars, or new lesions will leave new scars [10][16]. Options depend on the scar: ice pick scars, punch excision or focal high-strength trichloroacetic acid (CROSS); boxcar scars, CROSS for small ones, excision or punch elevation; rolling scars, subcision or fillers; widespread depressed scars, ablative or non-ablative fractional lasers, microneedling or radiofrequency microneedling; and raised hypertrophic scars and keloids, mainly steroid injections, with silicone sheeting [10][16].

Be realistic: acne scars are rarely removed completely, and several methods and sessions are usually needed; collagen remodels slowly, so further collagen-remodelling treatment waits at least 6 months [10]. Traditional ablative laser works best but needs about 2 weeks of recovery; darker skin is more prone to pigment change after ablative lasers or medium-to-deep peels, so fractional lasers, microneedling, subcision or fillers are preferable [10]. See our full guide to scar treatment.

Daily care: cleansing, moisturising, make-up and sun protection

  • Wash gently: use a mild cleanser of pH 5.5–7 rather than soap (pH 9–10), massage lightly with fingertips, and avoid scrubbing or exfoliating [3][4].
  • Do not squeeze or pick: it raises the risk of scars [4][7][16].
  • Moisturise: many acne treatments dry and irritate the skin, and daily moisturiser reduces water loss and inflammation [7].
  • Sun protection: daily, especially for people using retinoids or oral antibiotics or who have dark marks [1][4][7]. See 8 sunscreen myths.
  • Skin care and make-up: choose products labelled noncomedogenic, avoid heavy occlusive oils, consider oil-free silicone-based matte foundations, which may be less likely to block pores, and keep oily hair products off the forehead [3][7].
  • Patience and routine: use treatment daily for at least 8–12 weeks before judging it, and do not stop everything once it improves; discuss maintenance with your doctor [4][7].

Diet: milk, sugar, chocolate and supplements

The Taiwanese consensus mentions the importance of food choices but gives no specific dietary advice [1]; the AAD guideline finds the trials of low-glycaemic-load diets conflicting and the evidence on dairy, whey, omega-3 fish oil and chocolate insufficient for any recommendation [2].

  • Milk: several questionnaire-based observational studies link higher milk intake with acne, for example an odds ratio of 1.78 for more than three portions a week, with some finding the link only for low-fat or skim milk; these are associations, not proof of cause [3].
  • High-glycaemic foods: in a 12-week trial of 43 men the low-glycaemic group improved more, but also lost more weight, which may explain the benefit; another trial of 84 patients found no difference [2][3].
  • Chocolate: a relationship with acne frequency or severity has not been proven [3].
  • Supplements: evidence for zinc, niacinamide, pantothenic acid and antioxidants is limited or insufficient [2][3], and vitamin A supplements must be avoided during isotretinoin [6].

UpToDate's author does not routinely advise dietary change; a motivated patient may discuss a trial of less milk, with attention to calcium intake [4]. There is no need for strict diets that risk poor nutrition. A Taiwanese insurance-database study found that people with acne more often had peptic ulcers, irritable bowel syndrome, reflux or constipation, but this is an association, and the authors note that long antibiotic courses may play a part [12].

Pregnancy, breastfeeding, children and teens

Pregnancy and breastfeeding

Acne may improve, stay the same or worsen in pregnancy [7]. Not to be used: oral isotretinoin, topical tazarotene, oral tetracyclines, spironolactone and combined pills; other topical retinoids are also best avoided [1][2][4][5]. Commonly considered: topical azelaic acid, clindamycin and erythromycin, and BPO on limited areas; salicylic acid can be used on small areas for short periods [2][4]. The choice depends on severity and should involve the obstetrician [4]. If you are planning a pregnancy, tell your doctor before starting treatment [7]. If a topical retinoid was used before the pregnancy was known, a meta-analysis found no significant rise in major birth defects after first-trimester use, which is somewhat reassuring; however, it was too small to prove safety and case reports exist, so stop it and tell your doctor [4]. Breastfeeding: tell your doctor you are breastfeeding; tetracyclines should be avoided (AAD; the Taiwanese consensus allows doxycycline conditionally after medical assessment), UpToDate lists topical isotretinoin gel as contraindicated during breastfeeding, and AAD notes that more research is needed on acne treatment safety during breastfeeding [1][2][4].

Children and teenagers

  • Teenagers are treated much like adults, but simple routines help adherence, which should be discussed at each visit [4][8].
  • How children are treated: there are no large studies of acne treatment in infants and young children [8]. For mild acne under age 7, UpToDate suggests starting with one of a low-strength topical retinoid (such as tretinoin 0.025% cream or adapalene 0.1% gel) or BPO 2.5% cream, once daily; an oral antibiotic (erythromycin or azithromycin) is added for moderate or more inflamed acne; and oral isotretinoin is considered only for severe nodular acne that has not responded to oral antibiotics (UpToDate notes this is off-label in the US; the Taiwanese consensus advises against it at 12 and under) [1][8]. At 7–12 years the principles are the same as for teenagers, but hormonal treatments such as combined pills or spironolactone are not used [8].
  • Age limits: tetracyclines, see above (8 and under in the Taiwanese consensus, under 9 in AAD); no oral isotretinoin at 12 or under per the Taiwanese consensus; trifarotene is licensed in Taiwan from age 12, and the adapalene 0.1%/BPO combination from age 9 [1][2][21].
  • Acne at age 1–7, or sudden severe acne at any age with early body odour, early pubic or underarm hair or rapid growth, warrants a hormonal assessment [3][8].

Common acne myths

  • "Acne is only for teenagers." Wrong. Many people still have acne in their 20s and 30s, especially women [3][7].
  • "Blackheads are dirt, so scrub them away." The dark colour comes from packed skin cells, oxidised oils and melanin, and vigorous scrubbing with soaps, exfoliants or astringents can rupture comedones and worsen inflammation [3][4].
  • "Just squeeze it." Squeezing and picking raise the risk of scars [3][7][16].
  • "Stronger is faster." BPO above 2.5% may add little benefit but more irritation, and retinoid irritation rises with strength and frequency [1][4].
  • "If it hasn't worked in a week or two, switch." Most treatments need 8–12 weeks to judge [4][7].
  • "Once it clears, stop." Acne treatments mostly control rather than cure, and maintenance with a topical retinoid is usually needed [1][4].
  • "Supplements or giving up chocolate will fix it." The evidence does not support this [2][3].
  • "Isotretinoin always causes depression." Large observational studies mostly have not shown a higher risk, but the evidence is of low certainty, so mood should still be monitored [2][6][13].
  • "After isotretinoin, acne never returns." About 20–30% relapse, though most improve for the long term [1][6].

When to see a dermatologist

  • No improvement after 3 months of over-the-counter products; more severe acne should be seen sooner [9].
  • Nodules, early scarring or many dark marks, or acne that is lowering your mood or keeping you indoors [1][4][7].
  • In women, acne with irregular periods, excess hair or hair loss; in anyone, sudden severe acne [3][7].
  • Acne in a baby or a child aged 1–7 [3][8].
  • Moderate-to-severe nodular acne with joint pain or pain in the front of the chest wall [3].
  • Seek care promptly: many painful, ulcerating nodules with fever or bone pain; throat tightness, breathing difficulty, feeling faint, or swelling of the eyes, face, lips or tongue after an over-the-counter BPO or salicylic acid product; or, during isotretinoin, pregnancy, thoughts of self-harm, headache with vision changes, or severe muscle pain [3][6][9].

Frequently asked questions

How long until acne improves?

Topical treatment usually brings improvement within 12 weeks, though not necessarily complete clearance; isotretinoin usually shows benefit after 1–2 months [4][6]. Acne tends to fade with age, but in women it can last for many years [7].

Do face masks cause acne?

The guidelines and UpToDate topics used here do not discuss masks specifically. Prolonged friction or pressure from collars, straps or helmets is known to cause acne mechanica [3]; if acne on the lower face clearly worsens with mask use, see a dermatologist, who can also rule out look-alikes such as perioral dermatitis [3].

Does staying up late make acne worse?

The Taiwanese consensus lists a regular daily routine in its preface together with proper treatment, food choices and correct skin care, but gives no specific advice on sleep, and the AAD guideline and UpToDate topics used here do not say that late nights or short sleep worsen acne (AAD only notes that acne can disturb sleep) [1][2][3][4].

Can I buy acne treatments at a pharmacy?

In Taiwan some topical BPO, tretinoin and erythromycin products are pharmacist-supplied "indicated" medicines, whereas topical adapalene and clindamycin and all oral antibiotics need a prescription [21]. Do not use antibiotics long term on your own, which breeds resistance [1][2].

Can I exercise or drink alcohol on isotretinoin?

Vigorous exercise makes muscle aches more likely, so if you exercise heavily discuss it with your doctor and report severe pain; alcohol is not forbidden but should not be taken in excess [6].

Does fish oil reduce isotretinoin side effects?

In an 80-patient Thai randomised trial, 1 g of fish oil daily with 10 mg isotretinoin was non-inferior to placebo over 3 months and limited the rise in triglycerides, but did not reduce dry lips or other reported symptoms [15]. It is a single short study, not a reason for everyone to take supplements; ask your doctor first [15].

Can acne scars be removed completely?

Rarely. Several sessions and combined methods are usually needed, and the goal is clear improvement rather than flawless skin [10][16].

Does NHI cover isotretinoin?

Conditionally: the consent form describes severe cystic and nodular acne unresponsive to other treatments, and it must be prescribed by a dermatologist with a consent form and evidence that conventional treatment failed, after prior approval; in practice most patients pay themselves [12][20].

Hormonal therapy

COC contraindications differ: migraine (any migraine in the Taiwanese consensus, only with aura in AAD); the smoking threshold is ">35 years and >15 cigarettes" in the Taiwanese consensus and "≥35 years and ≥15" in AAD; AAD adds lupus with antiphospholipid antibodies, known thrombogenic mutations and fewer than 21 days postpartum, among others [1][2]. VTE rates cited by AAD (per 10,000 person-years): 1–5 without COC, 3–9 with COC, 10 with drospirenone COC, 5–20 in pregnancy and 40–65 in the 12 weeks postpartum [2].

Spironolactone: in the SAFA trial (410 adult women, 50 mg daily rising to 100 mg from week 6), treatment success at week 12 was 19% versus 6% (adjusted OR 5.18) [5]. Potassium: in 1,802 measurements from 974 women aged 18–45, hyperkalaemia occurred in 0.72% on treatment versus 0.76% of baseline measurements [5]; AAD cites 0.8% of 1,802 measurements above 5.0 mmol/L [2]. For healthy women over 45, the Taiwanese consensus and AAD decide by risk factors, whereas UpToDate's author suggests periodic checks [1][2][5]. Drospirenone 3 mg is roughly equivalent to spironolactone 25 mg, and in a small study combining them did not raise potassium [2][5].

Isotretinoin

  • Dose: the Taiwanese consensus starts at 0.25–0.5 mg/kg/day, targets 0.5–1.0 mg/kg/day for 4–6 months, and allows 0.1–0.5 for mild-to-moderate acne, noting few adverse effects at 0.1–0.3 mg/kg/day (expert experience) [1]. AAD found traditional and low (<0.5) doses not directly comparable, conditionally favours daily over intermittent dosing, and accepts standard or lidose formulations [2]. UpToDate gives 0.25–0.4 mg/kg/day as a low-dose option for moderate acne [6].
  • Cumulative dose: none set by the Taiwanese consensus or AAD; UpToDate targets 120–150 mg/kg (≥220 mg/kg may further reduce relapse, unproven); NHI caps a course at 100–120 mg/kg over 4–6 months [1][2][6][20].
  • Labs: the Taiwanese consensus checks liver tests, cholesterol and triglycerides before and during treatment, with frequency by dose; AAD advises liver tests and fasting lipids and no CBC in healthy patients; a Delphi consensus checks ALT and triglycerides within a month before starting and after reaching peak dose [1][2][6]. Triglycerides of 500–800 mg/dL call for dose reduction or a lipid-lowering drug, >800 may warrant stopping; transaminases >3 times normal warrant stopping [6].
  • Extensive nodular acne or conglobata: start below 0.5 mg/kg/day, with oral corticosteroids to prevent flares; acne fulminans is treated with prednisone 0.5–1 mg/kg/day alone for at least 4 weeks if systemic (2 weeks if not), then isotretinoin 0.1 mg/kg/day is added [4][5][6]; isotretinoin-induced acne fulminans (with or without systemic symptoms) means stopping isotretinoin immediately and treating as above [5]. Suggested work-up for acne fulminans: CBC with differential, liver tests, lipids, a pregnancy test in women, ESR/CRP if systemic, and radiographs if bone or joint involvement is suspected [3].
  • Contraception: the Taiwanese consensus names no iPLEDGE-like programme; practice follows the label, the consensus and the NHI consent form (4 weeks before and after) [1][20]. For psychiatric and bowel evidence see above; AAD advises monitoring and mentions PHQ-2/PHQ-9 [2]. The Taiwanese adult cohort reports a 95% CI of 0.422–1.696, markedly asymmetric around its point estimate, so interpret it cautiously [13].
  • Fish-oil trial: ages 18–25, IGA ≥3, isotretinoin 10 mg/day with 1 g fish oil or placebo for 3 months; difference in total lesion change −6.0 (95% CI −16.5 to 4.5; non-inferiority margin 5), IGA success 62.5% versus 60%; triglyceride change differed by −10.9 mg/dL, both groups staying within normal limits; single centre, and differing capsule appearance may have weakened blinding [15].

Comparative efficacy: network meta-analysis

Key messages: a topical retinoid plus BPO is at least as effective for inflammatory lesions as an oral antibiotic plus topical retinoid, which could reduce oral antibiotic use; antibiotics alone do little for comedones [14]. Its "P" values are ranking probabilities, not significance tests; isotretinoin did not rank highly in the IGA analysis because of few trials; median treatment was only 12 weeks, with no relapse or scarring data [14]. Evidence for maintenance: in a 12-week randomised trial of 241 patients with at least moderate improvement after adapalene plus clindamycin or clindamycin alone, continuing adapalene 0.1% gel reduced total lesion counts by 41.6%, whereas stopping led to a 92% increase [4].

Procedures and scars

Intralesional triamcinolone: 2.5–5 mg/mL in the Taiwanese consensus and AAD, but 1.25–2.5 mg/mL in the UpToDate light-therapy topic [1][2][9]; hypertrophic scars and keloids use 10–20 mg/mL, up to 40 mg/mL [10]. On procedures after isotretinoin (see above), AAD and the UpToDate isotretinoin and scar topics restrict only mechanical dermabrasion and traditional ablative lasers, whereas the light-therapy topic still advises against peels [2][6][9][10]. Scar classification also includes papular scars (often on the jawline, mostly in patients with keloids); pulsed dye laser is preferred for scar erythema; CO2 laser gave a mean 75% improvement at 18 months in an uncontrolled study of 60 patients; microneedling gave 41% improvement at 6 months in a 15-patient split-face trial [10][16].

Special populations

Infantile acne onset: the UpToDate pathogenesis topic gives 3–6 months in its text but 6–16 months (median 9) in its summary, as does the children's topic [3][8]. Ages 1–7: obtain a bone age and refer to paediatric endocrinology if advanced [8]. Isotretinoin in children: the Taiwanese consensus advises against it at 12 or under; the UpToDate isotretinoin topic says safety is not established under 12, while the children's topic notes off-label use in the US [1][6][8]. Endocrine testing is needed only with signs of androgen excess, after stopping combined pills for 4–6 weeks [2][7]; hair removal can mask hirsutism, so ask directly about male-pattern hair growth and hair-removal habits [7]; abrupt severe, atypical or recalcitrant acne should prompt consideration of androgen excess from adrenal or ovarian tumours, which may need laboratory tests and imaging [3]. Routine bacterial culture is not needed [2].

Differential diagnosis and associated diseases: additions

Key clues are comedones and lesion uniformity: folliculitis (staphylococcal, eosinophilic, pseudomonal or Pityrosporum) lacks comedones and is monomorphous, as is drug-induced acne, including glucocorticoid-induced "steroid folliculitis" [3]. Also consider Demodex folliculitis (often diagnosed after rosacea or acne treatment fails; many mites on an oil preparation support it, with clinical correlation), pseudofolliculitis barbae (beard area, mostly tightly curled hair, may leave keloidal scars), sebaceous hyperplasia (umbilicated yellowish papules on the adult forehead and cheeks that can resemble basal cell carcinoma) and keratosis pilaris (extensor upper arms and thighs, and the face in children) [3]. Moderate-to-severe nodular acne with arthritis or osteitis, particularly of the anterior chest wall, suggests SAPHO syndrome; PAPA and PASH are other autoinflammatory syndromes that include acne [3].

When to refer to dermatology

  • Nodules, scarring or marked distress; poor response after 3 months of combined topicals; isotretinoin candidates (dermatologists only in Taiwan) [1][4][20].
  • Suspected acne fulminans needs prompt referral; children aged 1–7 or suspected androgen excess also need paediatric endocrinology or gynaecology input, and abnormal initial tests may warrant referral to endocrinology, gynaecology or other specialists [3][8].
