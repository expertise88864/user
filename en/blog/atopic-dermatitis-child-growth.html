Can Atopic Dermatitis Affect a Child's Growth? What a 2026 Dupilumab Study Shows

Can severe eczema slow a child's growth? A 2026 JAAD post hoc dupilumab analysis: what it shows, its limits, and how to monitor height and sleep.

Key points in 30 seconds

Key points
  • Most children with atopic dermatitis grow normally; reduced height is mainly seen in children with severe disease, chronically poor sleep or heavy dietary restriction [2][3].
  • A 2026 JAAD analysis found that children aged 6–11 with severe atopic dermatitis started out skewed toward lower height percentiles. Among those below the 40th percentile, 31.3% on dupilumab gained at least 5 height percentiles in 16 weeks, vs 15.5% on placebo [1].
  • This is an exploratory, sponsor-funded post hoc analysis with only one year of follow-up and no adult-height data. It does not show that the injection "makes children taller".
  • A more reasonable reading: getting severe eczema under control, and letting the child sleep well, may help growth get back on track.
  • What parents can do: record height and weight regularly and watch the growth-chart trend, protect sleep, and avoid self-imposed long-term food restriction. Use topical steroids as directed; current studies have not found them to be a main cause of short stature [3].

Does severe eczema affect height?

The short answer: most children with atopic dermatitis are as tall as their peers. A US study pooling 9 national surveys with 264,326 children and adolescents found that, overall, children with eczema were not shorter. However, moderate-to-severe eczema was associated with shorter stature, and height below the 5th percentile was linked to eczema only when it came with chronic insufficient sleep (about 1.3% of children with eczema) [2]. No such difference was seen in adults [2].

A 2024 systematic review of 14 studies (50,146 patients with atopic dermatitis) found inconsistent results, mostly from low-quality evidence. Still, children with more severe disease, earlier onset, disturbed sleep and more dietary restriction appeared more prone to slower growth, and some studies found the slowdown to be temporary [3]. An older UK study observed that children with more than half of their skin affected were shorter on average, although parental height remained the strongest determinant of a child's height [4].

In other words, the children who need closer attention are those with severe, poorly controlled disease, not every child with eczema. For an overview of eczema care in children, see our guide to eczema in babies and children and our complete guide to atopic dermatitis.

Why might severe eczema affect growth?

The exact mechanism is not known. Possible factors proposed by researchers include [1][2][3]:

  • Poor sleep: night-time itch and repeated waking; chronic sleep loss may interfere with growth.
  • Chronic inflammation: researchers suspect that a persistent type 2 inflammatory state may affect bone growth.
  • Cumulative corticosteroid exposure: especially oral steroids, or long-term higher-dose inhaled corticosteroids in children who also have asthma.
  • Nutrition: for example vitamin D deficiency, or long-term restriction of many foods because of allergy worries.

These are possible contributors; none has been proven to be the main cause.

What did the new study find?

A 2026 analysis in the Journal of the American Academy of Dermatology (JAAD) (November issue) [1] is based on a large pediatric drug trial. Children aged 6 to 11 with severe atopic dermatitis were randomly assigned to dupilumab or placebo injections for 16 weeks, with topical steroids allowed in both groups. After 16 weeks, all children switched to dupilumab and were followed to week 52. The researchers went back and analysed the height of 304 children and measured a blood marker of bone formation.

  • Shorter at the start: in both boys and girls, more children than expected were in the lower height percentiles.
  • Week 16: among children who started below the 40th height percentile, 31.3% (26 of 83) on dupilumab gained at least 5 height percentiles, vs 15.5% (9 of 58) on placebo.
  • Week 52: in the same group, 50.7% (36 of 71) of children on dupilumab throughout and 40.4% (19 of 47) of those who switched from placebo reached a gain of at least 5 percentiles (the paper is inconsistent about the reference point; it may be relative to week 16).
  • Bone marker: bone alkaline phosphatase (BALP), a marker of bone formation, started near the lower end of the reference range for healthy children and rose with treatment, staying within the normal range throughout.

What does "gaining 5 percentiles" mean? A percentile shows where a child's height ranks among children of the same age and sex. Moving from the 20th to the 25th percentile, for example, means the child grew somewhat more than their previous curve predicted. The researchers state that this threshold was proposed by pediatric experts as a clinically meaningful change within one year [1].

What this does and does not mean

A reasonable reading

  • Shorter stature does occur in children with severe atopic dermatitis and is worth monitoring.
  • Once the disease was well controlled, some children who were short at the start showed signs of catching up. The researchers suggest better sleep and less inflammation may play a role, but this is unproven [1].
  • Children who received placebo first showed a similar improvement after switching, which amounts to a repeat observation of the same pattern.

What it does not mean

  • Dupilumab is not a "growth injection": the analysis looked only at children with severe disease who were short. There is no reason to use the drug for height in children of normal height or with mild eczema.
  • It does not show taller adult height: follow-up lasted one year, so the effect on final height is unknown.
  • It does not mean other treatments don't help: the study did not compare dupilumab with other ways of controlling eczema. The key point is disease control, not one particular drug.

The study's limitations in plain words

  • Post hoc and sponsor-funded: the trial was not designed to study height. It was funded by the manufacturers, several authors are company employees, and all statistics are exploratory.
  • Regression to the mean: when you select children because they measured short once, the next measurement often drifts back toward average on its own, because of measurement error, posture or timing. The 15.5% improvement in the placebo group may partly reflect this natural fluctuation (placebo children also used topical steroids and their eczema may have improved). A placebo comparison exists at week 16, but at week 52 every child was on the drug, so there is no control group.
  • Non-standardised measurements: height was not measured the same way at every site, and parental height and pubertal stage were not recorded.
  • Different reference charts: the study used US CDC growth charts, which are not identical to Taiwanese references.

What can parents do?

  • Keep the eczema under control: moisturise and use treatments regularly as directed, and treat flares early rather than waiting for them to spread. See our guide to topical treatment of atopic dermatitis.
  • Protect sleep: if your child often wakes at night from itch or is tired during the day, tell the doctor; reducing night-time itch should be one of the treatment goals.
  • Avoid self-imposed long-term food restriction: children with more dietary restriction appear more prone to slower growth [3]. Whether a child is truly allergic to a food should be confirmed by a doctor, and if a food must be avoided, a doctor or dietitian can help fill the nutritional gap.
  • Record height and weight regularly: measure at the same time of day, without shoes, with the same device, and note the date; ask the doctor to plot it on a growth chart. Look at the long-term trend rather than worrying about a single number.
  • Give the doctor the full medication history: including asthma and inhaled steroid use, and how often and how long the child has taken oral steroids.
  • Do not stop or switch medicines on your own: discuss any change with the doctor first.

Will topical steroids stop my child from growing?

This is one of parents' most common worries. Most studies have not found topical corticosteroids to be linked with shorter height. The systematic review concluded that topical steroid use was not associated with shorter stature [3]; a UK study following 80 prepubertal children for two years found height was not affected by topical steroid potency [5]; and another study found only a marginal additional effect of topical steroids, with disease extent and parental height mattering more [4].

In the JAAD study, children on dupilumab used less topical steroid than those on placebo, but the height difference remained after the researchers adjusted for topical steroid use, suggesting the result was not simply due to using less steroid cream [1].

In practice, use the strength, sites and duration your doctor recommends, and apply enough to the areas that need it. Avoiding treatment out of fear can leave eczema uncontrolled and the child waking with itch every night, which is more consistently linked to slower growth. Oral steroids or long-term higher-dose inhaled steroids are a different matter; discuss them with your doctor [1]. For potency and correct use, see our complete guide to topical steroids.

When to see a dermatologist or pediatrician

  • Widespread or recurrent eczema, or frequent itch-related sleep loss despite regular topical treatment: ask a dermatologist whether further treatment, such as phototherapy or systemic therapy, is needed.
  • Height clearly at the low end for age, a steadily falling height percentile, a clear slowdown in yearly growth, or height well below what parental heights would predict: see a pediatrician, with referral to pediatric endocrinology if needed. Do not assume eczema is the only cause.
  • Repeated or long-term oral steroids, or long-term higher-dose inhaled steroids for asthma.
  • Long-term restriction of several foods because of allergy concerns, or poor weight gain.

In Taiwan, dupilumab (Dupixent) is licensed for atopic dermatitis from 6 months of age. National Health Insurance has separate reimbursement rules for children aged 6 to under 12, requiring sufficient disease severity, failure of topical treatment and at least one systemic immunosuppressant, and prior approval. For systemic treatment options, see our guide to systemic treatment of atopic dermatitis.

Frequently asked questions

My child is shorter than classmates. Is it the eczema?

Not necessarily. A child's height depends mostly on parental height [4], as well as nutrition, pubertal timing and other health conditions. Children with mild eczema who sleep well usually grow normally [2]. Severe eczema with chronic sleep loss may contribute, but a pediatrician should still look for other causes.

Will dupilumab make my child taller?

That cannot be claimed. The study showed only that, in short children with severe disease, more reached a percentile gain on treatment, in a post hoc analysis with one year of follow-up [1]. Whether to use dupilumab depends on eczema severity and response to other treatments, not on height. For research on treatment duration and stopping, see how long dupilumab is needed.

Will long-term topical steroids stunt my child's growth?

Current studies have not linked appropriately used topical steroids with shorter height [3][5]. The bigger concern is poorly controlled disease and poor sleep. If you are worried about the amount, ask your doctor about adjustments, such as adding non-steroidal topicals or intermittent maintenance use, rather than stopping on your own.

Should my child avoid foods because of eczema?

Unless a doctor has confirmed a specific food allergy, long-term dietary restriction is not needed, and more restriction is linked to slower growth [3]. If certain foods must be avoided, a doctor or dietitian can help find substitutes.

Does my child need blood tests for bone markers?

The bone alkaline phosphatase measured in the study is a research marker for exploring mechanisms, not a routine test for treatment decisions. Whether a child needs blood tests, a bone-age X-ray or other tests should be decided by a pediatrician based on the growth pattern.

If growth has lagged, can it catch up once eczema improves?

Some research suggests growth impairment linked to severe eczema with poor sleep may be reversible [2], and this study saw some children's height percentiles rise after disease control [1]. Every child is different and final height depends on many factors, so keep following the growth chart.

For healthcare professionals

For family physicians, pharmacists, nurses and other health professionals outside dermatology

Study design

Irvine et al [1] performed a post hoc analysis of LIBERTY AD PEDS (NCT03345914; randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled phase 3, 16 weeks, ages 6–11 with severe AD) and its open-label extension PED-OLE (NCT02612454). Approved-dose arms were included: dupilumab 300 mg q4w + TCS, 200 mg q2w + TCS (n = 59) and placebo + TCS (n = 123). All patients then received 300 mg q4w + TCS to week 52, with weight-based up-titration to 200 mg q2w (<60 kg) or 300 mg q2w (≥60 kg) if IGA 0/1 was not reached. Height was measured at baseline and weeks 16 and 52, converted to age-specific percentiles using CDC reference charts, with dupilumab arms pooled. The main outcome was the proportion of children below P50, P40, P30 and P25 at baseline gaining ≥5 percentiles. Week 16 missing data were imputed as non-response; week 52 data were as observed and compared with each group's week 16 status by McNemar's test. BALP was measured retrospectively. The authors state that all analyses are exploratory with nominal P values.

Baseline: mean age 8.3–9.5 years, mean EASI 37.1–39.0, about half had asthma, about 20% had prior inhaled corticosteroid use, and 27.6–35.0% had received systemic AD therapy [1].

Key results

  • Baseline: both sexes were overrepresented in lower height percentiles vs the expected distribution (binomial test); mean BALP was 59.9–64.2 μg/L across arms, near the lower end of the 95% reference interval for healthy children aged 8–11 (expected mean cited by the authors >100 μg/L).
  • Week 16, ≥5-percentile gain: below P40, dupilumab 31.3% (26/83) vs placebo 15.5% (9/58); below P30, 31.9% vs 11.1%; below P25, 30.6% vs 11.9% (all P < .05). Below P50, 29.0% vs 15.7%, not marked as significant in the figure.
  • Odds ratio (below P40): 2.5 (95% CI 1.1–5.8; P = .0357); adjusted for cumulative TCS dose 3.1 (1.3–7.8; P = .0137); adjusted for proportion of TCS-free days 2.9 (1.2–6.9; P = .0184). TCS use was significantly lower with dupilumab.
  • Week 52 (reference point ambiguous; text says vs week 16): below P40, 50.7% (36/71) of continuous-dupilumab and 40.4% (19/47) of placebo-to-dupilumab children achieved a ≥5-percentile gain. Among continuously treated children below P40, 36.6% gained ≥10 and 18.3% gained ≥20 percentiles.
  • BALP: at week 16, 77.3 and 81.8 μg/L in the two dupilumab arms vs 65.0 μg/L with placebo (significant at weeks 8, 12 and 16); at week 52, 78.6 (300 mg q4w) and 82.1 μg/L (placebo-to-dupilumab); the 200 mg q2w arm is 82.1 in the text but about 85 in Fig 3, with only 19 children contributing; all within reference intervals, with similar patterns by sex and no difference by asthma status. In a limited number of samples, median CTX-1, OC and P1NP rose from below the lower reference limit into the reference interval. Liver tests (bilirubin, ALT, AST) showed no clinically meaningful changes.

Limitations and interpretation

  • Post hoc, exploratory analysis with nominal P values; sponsored by Sanofi and Regeneron, with several company-employed authors and sponsor-funded medical writing support.
  • Regression to the mean: low-percentile groups were selected on a single baseline height, and 11.1–15.7% of placebo patients met the threshold at week 16; week 52 has no concurrent control, and the McNemar comparisons are within-group only.
  • Height measurement was not standardised across sites, with no familial height potential or Tanner staging. Our inference: a 5-percentile change over 16 weeks may approach routine measurement error.
  • Only one year of follow-up and no adult height; BALP is a surrogate, with no bone density or fracture outcomes; CDC charts may not fit Taiwanese children.
  • Minor internal inconsistencies: the Methods give n = 123 for 300 mg q4w while Table I gives n = 122; Fig 1 shows 83/153 girls below P50 but prints 64.2% (about 54.2% by count). These do not change the main findings but are worth noting when citing.
  • Consistency: a US real-world retrospective cohort using TriNetX (by Taiwanese authors) found an RR of 1.15 (95% CI 1.12–1.18) for falling below P5 in children with AD, and among children with AD, dupilumab vs conventional systemic immunomodulators had an RR of 0.69 (0.57–0.84) for falling below P5; disease severity data were lacking, so residual confounding cannot be excluded [6].

Practical approach (our suggestions)

  • In children with moderate-to-severe AD, measure height and weight regularly with a consistent method, plot them on a growth chart, and follow percentile trends and growth velocity rather than single values.
  • Ask about sleep (night-time scratching, awakenings), dietary restriction and cumulative corticosteroid exposure (oral, inhaled, extensive high-potency TCS).
  • When height is low or growth slows, assess the usual causes first (family height, nutrition, pubertal timing, other chronic disease, endocrine problems) rather than attributing it to AD alone.
  • Treatment choice should still rest on disease severity, prior response and reimbursement rules; "promoting growth" should not be the main reason to choose dupilumab. There is no evidence supporting routine BALP monitoring.

Taiwan NHI: dupilumab for children aged 6 to under 12 (§13.17.2, 22 Sep 2026 version)

  • Prescribers: dermatologists, or pediatricians with an allergy, immunology and rheumatology subspecialty.
  • Eligibility: chronic moderate-to-severe AD suitable for systemic therapy, lesions for ≥6 months, EASI ≥16 and IGA ≥3 (EASI area counts only red eczematous skin, not dryness, scaling or excoriations alone).
  • Treatment failure: after a full course, two assessments within 3 months (≥4 weeks apart) still meeting the severity criteria. Prior treatment must include medium-or-higher-potency TCS and a topical calcineurin inhibitor used regularly for 12 weeks, plus at least one of methotrexate, azathioprine or cyclosporin (adequate doses: methotrexate 10 mg/week, azathioprine 1.0 mg/kg/day, cyclosporin 2.5 mg/kg/day, for at least 12 weeks without response, or with objective adverse effects or contraindications). Topicals may be stopped for skin atrophy or infection, and systemic doses lowered for intolerance or special constitutions, if documented in the record.
  • Prior authorisation; 6-month courses, with renewal every 6 months requiring photographs and EASI 50 vs pre-treatment; reapplication after more than 3 months off treatment counts as a new application. Patients previously approved for psoriasis biologics need at least two years free of psoriasis symptoms, or a biopsy excluding psoriasis.
  • Weight-based dosing: 15 to under 30 kg, 600 mg loading (two 300 mg doses) then 300 mg every 4 weeks; 30 to under 60 kg, 400 mg loading (two 200 mg doses) then 200 mg every 2 weeks; ≥60 kg, 600 mg loading then 300 mg every 2 weeks; at least EASI 50 is required at week 16.
  • Stopping and pausing: stop if EASI 50 is not reached after the first 6-month course, or for malignancy or helminth infection; pause after 1 year if EASI ≤16, with reapplication allowed on relapse (at least 50% relapse or EASI ≥16).
  • Applicability: about 30% of PEDS participants had prior systemic therapy, whereas NHI requires prior failure of a systemic immunosuppressant, so treated Taiwanese children may differ in disease course and prior exposure.

When to refer

  • Dermatology: persistent moderate-to-severe disease or sleep disruption despite regular topical therapy, or when phototherapy, systemic therapy or biologics are being considered.
  • Pediatric endocrinology: height clearly low for age, slowing growth velocity, a falling percentile, height inconsistent with parental heights, or clearly early or delayed puberty.
  • Pediatric allergy/immunology or nutrition: long-term multiple food restrictions or poor weight gain.
