Key points in 30 seconds

Key points
  • A viral wart (verruca) is a benign growth caused by human papillomavirus (HPV) infecting the epidermis, common on the hands and feet of children and young adults [1][14]. Most clear by themselves within months to years, but the timing is hard to predict [2][14].
  • For a wart that does not hurt or bother someone with normal immunity, watching is a reasonable choice; pain, loss of function, distress about looks, persistence or weak immunity are reasons to treat [1][14].
  • Salicylic acid has the most consistent evidence, with a real but modest effect: in the 2012 Cochrane review, which pools trials, the chance of cure was about 1.56 times that with placebo (an inactive comparison) [2]. Pooled, freezing was clearly better neither than placebo nor than salicylic acid; the one trial that favoured freezing did so only for warts on the hands [2].
  • Where not to use salicylic acid yourself: the face (contraindicated in the 2014 British Association of Dermatologists guideline, "the British guideline" below) [1]; numb feet (peripheral neuropathy) or areas that heal poorly [1][14]. Also not on eyelids, genitals, broken or infected skin, or anything that may not be a wart (general safety advice). With diabetes, poor circulation, weak immunity, pregnancy or breastfeeding, see a doctor before choosing.
  • Irregular growth, ulceration, a lesion that resists treatment, or a long-standing or unresponsive lesion beside or under a nail (especially with weak immunity) needs checking to be sure it is not skin cancer [14][33].
  • NHI freezing items list "viral warts"; HPV vaccines prevent genital warts and related cancers, and their licence does not cover skin warts of the hands and feet [38][40]. We found no Taiwanese society guideline for skin warts and no randomised wart-treatment trial in Taiwanese patients.
Just found a wart-like growth: five things to do
  1. Check the warning signs first: with any of them, do not paint or pare it; see a doctor.
  2. Check the site: face, eyelid, genital or anal area, or under a nail means a doctor first.
  3. Do not pick, bite, cut or scrape; on hairy skin with warts use an electric razor or do not shave [14].
  4. Take a dated photo and note size and number so change is easy to see.
  5. If it is clearly an ordinary hand or foot wart and none of the above applies, choose between watching, home salicylic acid and seeing a doctor.

What is a wart, and how does it form?

Viral warts come from human papillomavirus (HPV) infecting keratinocytes, the cells of the outermost layer of skin, the epidermis [1]. There are many HPV types (sources say from more than 150 to more than 200) [1][14], and different types favour different sites [14]. The virus tends to enter through small breaks, such as abraded or waterlogged skin, and the wart shows later: the British guideline says weeks to months [1] and another source gives an incubation of about 2–6 months [14], so it is hard to say where it was caught.

No drug specifically kills HPV. Most treatments destroy the infected epidermis, which can also trigger an immune response that clears the rest [1]. A wart that has gone does not prove that all virus has gone from nearby skin, so recurrence is common [14].

How to read the numbers on this page

A "relative risk" (RR) is the chance of cure with treatment divided by that in the comparison group: 1 is equal and 1.56 is 56% higher; a "confidence interval" that crosses 1 may mean no difference. "Arm percentages" show how many people were really cured. Because warts often clear alone, "cure rates" without a comparison group overstate the effect: the British guideline notes that about 20–30% of placebo-treated warts were cured in trials lasting 3–4 months [1].

Where does the evidence come from, and how old is it? "The British guideline" is the 2014 treatment advice of the British Association of Dermatologists [1]. "Cochrane" means the 2012 Cochrane review, which put 85 trials together with searches only to May 2011; its authors say trial quality was poor and effects were probably overstated [2]. "The Chinese guideline" dates from 2022 [3]. The first two are more than 10 years old, and we found no Taiwanese guideline. A "placebo" is an inactive comparison that shows how many people would have cleared anyway.

The British guideline also lists average cure rates across trials: salicylic acid 49% (0–69% in different trials) and placebo 23% (5–73%) [1]. These are averages of separate trials, not two groups compared within one trial, so 49% minus 23% is not the size of the effect.

How common are they, and who gets them?

Surveys of Taiwanese schoolchildren

Taiwan's dermatologist-examined school data are about 20 years old: Kaohsiung County in 2004 examined 4,067 children aged 6–11 and found warts in 2.4% (95% confidence interval 1.9–2.9%), highest at age 10–11 and similar in boys and girls [5]; Magong, Penghu in 2005 examined 3,273 and found 2.81% (2.24–3.38%), nearly all on the hands or arms [6]. Neither survey examined feet, so plantar warts are missing and these are not totals. Both used purposive samples; the Kaohsiung response rate was 52% and its authors think prevalence may be underestimated [5][6]. A third survey, from Taitung County, is known to us only as quoted in the Penghu paper: plantar warts 1.78% [6]. Cases clustered in a few classes in Kaohsiung but not in Penghu [5][6].

A 2013 national NHI sample did not list warts separately [7], and we found no Taiwanese figures for adults, natural course or the whole population. Foreign estimates vary widely with age and site: the British guideline gives about 5–30% of children and young adults [1]; 33% of Dutch children aged 4–12 had warts when hands and feet were examined [21]; among new college students in Beijing it was only 1.4% [22].

Who is more likely to get warts

  • Family members or classmates with warts: both were associated with a higher chance in foreign schoolchildren [33].
  • Nail biting, picking, manicure trauma, wet work or sweaty hands: linked to periungual warts [33].
  • Handling raw meat, poultry or fish: more hand warts [14][2].
  • Bare feet in changing rooms and pools: listed as a risk factor for plantar warts [2].
  • Atopic dermatitis or weak immunity (such as HIV or organ transplantation): warts may be extensive and stubborn [14]. In Korean claims data, kidney, liver or stem-cell transplant recipients had 3.73 times the risk of cutaneous warts of controls (95% confidence interval 3.49–3.98); this is an association, not Taiwanese data [36].

How do warts spread? Swimming, school and sharing

Warts spread mainly by direct skin contact; injured or softened skin is easier to infect, and people without visible warts can carry the virus [14]. They also spread on one's own body: nail biting can carry virus between fingers and elsewhere [33], and shaving or abrading the normal skin beside a wart can spread it [1].

Towels, floors, pools? Sources differ: the British guideline says warts can spread through the environment but that how long the virus survives outside the body is not known [1], the Chinese guideline lists contact with clothing and objects (weak recommendation, very-low-quality evidence) [3], while another source says spread by objects has been proposed but not proven [14]. A US study found HPV genetic material on barbers' clippers, but it did not test infectivity or link it to patients, so it does not prove spread [37].

  • Swimming and shared showers: wearing slippers may reduce spread [14]; no source says people with warts may not swim. Covering a plantar wart and using your own towel is sensible, though no study in our sources shows it reduces spread.
  • School, sport and work: no source we used asks people to stay away from school, sport or work because of warts, or to have them cut off first, and none addresses limits for food or healthcare work; follow your workplace if it has a rule.
  • Changing rooms, shoes and pets: bare feet in changing rooms and pools are a risk factor for plantar warts [2], and athletic activity is associated with them [33]. Our sources do not address shared shoes or socks, or pets.
  • Should the family be checked? No source we used advises screening the household. In the Dutch research about half of the children had warts their parents had not noticed [21], so a look at bath time is enough.
  • Do not share files or pumice stones used on a wart, and do not reuse them on healthy skin or nails [14].

Will it go away? Children and adults

Children: a Dutch study followed 333 schoolchildren with warts and concluded that about half are wart-free within 1 year (complete resolution in 52 per 100 person-years, 95% confidence interval 44–60); younger children and those with a non-Caucasian skin type cleared faster, while type, size and number predicted little [21]. "About two-thirds by 2 years" comes from other studies and reviews [1][14][2], not from this one. A British cohort quoted in that paper found 93% (337 of 364 cases) resolved within 5 years in children aged 11 [21].

Adults: usually slower; the British guideline says 5–10 years is not uncommon [1]. Among Beijing college students, 24 of 78 untreated people (30.8%) cleared within 1 year and another 20 (25.6%) between years 1 and 2; but nearly four in ten were lost to follow-up and these are not Taiwanese data [22]. No source we used gives figures for warts lasting more than 10 years, and Taiwan has no natural-course study.

In people with weak immunity, warts may be more numerous, larger and harder to clear [1]. While being watched, warts can also spread or persist unpredictably [14]; the Cochrane review thinks warts still present after many months to a year are probably less likely to clear without treatment [2].

Wart, corn or something else?

The most useful clues are clotted capillaries (black dots or pinpoint bleeding) on gentle paring and hidden skin lines [1][14]. A corn may also hide skin lines but lacks these capillaries; the colour of black heel pares away and skin lines continue [14]. The popular "side-squeeze hurts more than pressing" test is not mentioned in our sources and should not be relied on alone. Doctors look for black to red dots with a dermatoscope [14]; if doubt remains or cancer is suspected, the doctor decides on a biopsy [3].

Warning signs: when to see a doctor promptly

  • Possible cancer: wart-like lesions that grow irregularly, ulcerate or resist treatment raise concern for squamous cell carcinoma or amelanotic melanoma [14]. Bleeding, non-healing, fast-growing, unusually coloured or changing lesions should not be self-treated either (general safety advice; bleeding, rapid growth and atypical pigment are listed for genital warts in the literature [16]).
  • Beside or under a nail: for long-standing lesions, those in immunosuppressed people (even on several digits) and those unresponsive to treatment, the literature advises a biopsy to exclude squamous cell carcinoma [33].
  • Feet: do not put salicylic acid on numb feet (neuropathy) or areas that heal poorly [1][14]; with diabetes or poor circulation see a doctor first, and do not pare or freeze it yourself (these last two are this site's cautious advice). A wound, swelling, pus or blackening needs same-day care.
  • Weak immunity, or unusually many, severe or persistent warts: consider an immune problem [1].
  • Many flat wart-like or tinea versicolor-like spots since childhood: think of epidermodysplasia verruciformis (EV), which carries a high skin-cancer risk and needs referral; it can also appear later in life with weak immunity (such as transplantation or HIV) [15].
  • Genital or perianal warts in a child: arrange a prompt medical assessment. Many children probably acquire them without sexual contact, around birth, through care such as bathing and nappy changes, or from their own hand warts; but the doctor must also seriously consider possible sexual abuse. It becomes more likely with age and is less likely under age 2, but age alone cannot exclude it, and HPV typing cannot show how it was caught [17].
  • What the assessment involves: the doctor talks with the caregivers, a trained professional talks with the child if old enough, and there is a physical examination, with tests for other sexually transmitted infections or referral to a child-protection specialist when needed; if findings are suggestive, the literature says to report according to local policies [17]. This protects the child and is not an accusation.
  • After treatment: return for spreading redness, pus, fever, very large blisters or severe pain.

Treat or wait? Watching, home acid or the clinic

Who can watch: people with normal immunity whose wart does not hurt, limit walking or work, or bother them. The British guideline calls this entirely acceptable [1], and the Cochrane review also supports waiting for recent warts that cause little bother; after many months to a year without clearing, clearing alone is probably less likely, a point at which to reconsider treatment [2]. The Chinese guideline advises removing warts as early as possible (strong recommendation, low-quality evidence), but mainly on the basis of genital-wart guidelines [3]. Both views exist; how much it bothers you matters.

When treatment is more worthwhile: pain or loss of function, distress about looks or social life, long persistence, or weak immunity [14]. Our sources do not contain the claim that one untreated wart becomes twenty, and none of their trials measured whether treatment reduces infecting others or growing new warts. The Chinese guideline says treatment may weaken HPV infectivity without necessarily eradicating it, on limited evidence [3]. Warts can spread while being watched [14], so weigh the choice with a doctor.

Plantar warts are especially stubborn. The EVerT trial in Britain and Ireland enrolled 240 people aged 12 and over with plantar warts; complete clearance at 12 weeks was 14% with 50% salicylic acid (17 of 119) and 14% with freezing (15 of 110); by self-report at 6 months it was 31% and 34% [20]. The Cochrane authors therefore called continued freezing for plantar warts alone highly questionable [2], while the Chinese guideline still gives it a weak recommendation [3].

Think about the burden too: in EVerT, 58–60% sought treatment for pain and 25–33% because they could not swim [20]. In another trial 69% were satisfied after freezing, 24% after salicylic acid and 22% after waiting; among people with common warts, 31% found the freezing course a considerable burden and 54% the salicylic acid course (all foreign data) [2]. The distress is real and worth telling the doctor.

Salicylic acid at home: steps, safety and when to stop

Who can use it: people with a clear hand or foot wart, unbroken skin, normal feeling, good healing and no red flags [1][14]; with diabetes, poor circulation, weak immunity or pregnancy ask a doctor first. Salicylic acid slowly dissolves the skin's outer layer; it is painless and done at home but needs patience [14].

  1. Soak for about 5 minutes to soften thick skin [14].
  2. File gently the thick surface of the wart; do not abrade the healthy skin beside it (this may spread virus) or draw blood [1]. Keep the file or pumice for the wart only and do not share it [14].
  3. Dry the skin and put the medicine on the wart only, avoiding healthy skin around it; the skin must be dry [14].
  4. Cover as the product label says; the usual foreign advice is daily application, and if taped, the tape and acid can be changed every 48 hours [14].
  5. Do it daily and note the size. If irritation is marked, use it less often [14].
  6. Do not go beyond 12 weeks without a clinician's assessment [14]; the British regimen is 3–4 months [1], so come back if it is not improving.
  • When it is cured and you can stop: no visible wart and, on palms and soles, skin lines that have come back [14]; EVerT also used normal skin on close inspection [20]. A clear surface does not prove that the virus is gone [14]. The Chinese guideline counts as cured complete clearance at 4 weeks with no recurrence for at least 6 months [3], so keep watching for some months and go back if it regrows.
  • Strength: 17–50% is usual abroad, 40–50% only for thick palms and soles [14]; the British guideline gives 15–26% for hands and 15–40% for soles [1]. In Taiwan, 14 valid licences for topical salicylic acid list warts, but only 10% and 17% are printed in product names [40]; the 17% product is a plaster and 10% is below the British range for hands; forms include liquids, ointments and pads [14]. Follow the label and do not concentrate it yourself. No 40% product name appears in the Taiwanese licence data [40].
  • Where not to use it: the face (listed as contraindicated in the British guideline) [1]; numb areas (peripheral neuropathy) or areas that heal poorly, such as neuropathic feet [1][14]. Do not use it yourself on eyelids, genital or anal skin, broken, infected or eczematous skin, or anything that may not be a wart (not all printed in the sources; general safety advice).
  • Ask a doctor first: diabetes or poor circulation (the literature limits tape plus salicylic acid to people without diabetes, peripheral artery disease, neuropathy or skin erosions) [14]; weak immunity; pregnancy, breastfeeding and children (our sources give no age or area limits and nothing on pregnancy safety, see special groups).
  • Stop and see a doctor: raw, bleeding or blistered skin, severe pain, swelling, pus, blackening or fever. All but very low strengths can cause chemical burns [1]; mild irritation is common [14]. An itchy rash may be contact allergy to other ingredients [1].

Does duct tape work?

The first trial (61 people, ages 3–22) favoured tape over freezing: 85% vs 60% [14]; but counted by group size in Cochrane it was 22/30 vs 15/31, RR 1.52 (0.99–2.31), an uncertain difference [2]. Two more rigorous trials saw no benefit: 16% vs 6% at 6 weeks in 103 children (p = 0.12), and 21% vs 22% at 2 months in 90 adults; pooled RR 1.43 (95% confidence interval 0.51–4.05) [14][2]. The verdict is uncertain: the two later trials used clear tape, which was harder to keep on [14]. Tape can also cause redness, itching, eczema or bleeding [2]. The literature allows combining it with salicylic acid only without diabetes, peripheral artery disease, neuropathy or skin erosions [14]. Trying it amounts to watching, so do not let it delay needed care.

Over-the-counter freezing sprays (propane or dimethyl ether) are not as cold as liquid nitrogen; the British guideline thinks them probably less effective, though one randomised trial found them equal [1]; the Cochrane review did not assess them [2]. Do not use them on the face, genitals or beside nails, or on feet with diabetes, numbness or poor circulation (general safety advice).

Freezing at the clinic: what happens, how often, and aftercare

The doctor pares thick skin, then sprays or dabs liquid nitrogen until the wart and about 2 mm of skin around it turn white and frozen [14]; the British guideline keeps a frozen halo for 5–30 seconds depending on site and size [1]. Liquid nitrogen is at −196 °C [33], and it hurts. Thick or plantar warts may get two freeze-thaw cycles (freeze, then thaw) [14]. EVerT advised a gentle first freeze that people could tolerate [20].

  • Interval: often every 2–3 weeks [1][14]. The British guideline says most trials found better clearance at 3 months with every 2–3 weeks than every 4 weeks, often similar after 6 months; shorter intervals bring more reports of adverse effects such as pain and blisters [1]. Cochrane found no significant difference in long-term cure between 2, 3 and 4 weeks; in one trial pain or blistering was 29%, 7% and 0% at 1-, 2- and 3-week intervals (possibly because people were seen sooner) [2]. NHI requires at least one week between freezes (same or different procedures); that is a billing minimum, not the advised medical interval [38].
  • How many: usually several, up to about 6 in the British guideline [1] and 3.6 on average in EVerT (maximum 4) [20]; if 6 bring no response, changing the method is reasonable [14]. More is not always better: in one trial people not cleared after about 4 sessions showed no added benefit from another 3 months (but the design was not ideal and many dropped out) [1]. No source we used says a single freeze cures 30–50%; cure after a full course varies widely: generally about 20–50% according to Cochrane [2], 0–69% in the British guideline [1] and 14% to more than 90% in another source [14].
  • Is a longer freeze better? Pooling 4 trials, Cochrane found aggressive freezing better than gentle (RR 1.90, 1.15–3.15) [2], at the price of more pain and blisters and possibly scarring [1].
  • What about pain: a pooled analysis by Taiwanese authors of 3 foreign trials (228 people) did not support routine numbing cream before freezing; only thick skin in children hurt less, in one trial (another trial was of genital warts with only 10 minutes of cream) [10]. These are not Taiwanese patients. Tell the doctor if you fear pain; painful treatment is generally avoided in young children [1][14].
  • What to expect: anything from mild redness to a blood blister, with pain and tenderness, usually healing in 4–7 days [14]; in EVerT 10 people (9%) asked to stop the freeze early [20]. Occasionally blistering spreads virus to nearby skin and the wart becomes larger [14].
  • Washing, water, dressings and walking: our sources do not say whether to bathe or get it wet, whether to open a blister, what to apply or how long a scab lasts; EVerT only mentions padding after treatment as local practice [20]. Before leaving, ask how to wash, what to do if a blister breaks, whether long walks or sport are fine and what to take for pain. Return for spreading redness, pus, fever, severe pain or an unusually large blister (general safety advice).
  • Marks that may remain: lighter or darker skin, more likely in darker skin [1][14]; scarring is more likely with more aggressive freezing [1]; overly aggressive freezing beside a nail can harm the nail matrix (the root that makes the nail) and permanently deform the nail [14][33]. How long marks last is not stated.
  • Sites needing care: fingers (severe pain, nerve injury) [14], and areas near superficial nerves, tendons or nails or with poor circulation [1].
  • Tell the doctor before freezing about diabetes or poor healing, peripheral vascular disease, weak immunity or immunosuppressants, numb skin, dialysis, Raynaud's (fingers turning white or blue in the cold), cold urticaria (rash in the cold), unexplained blood disorders, cryoglobulinaemia or cryofibrinogenaemia (rare diseases in which blood proteins clump in the cold), and collagen or autoimmune disease. EVerT excluded these people [20], so its results do not directly apply.

Other clinic procedures: shaving, curettage and cautery are common for filiform warts [1][14], but the British guideline rests on case experience without high-quality studies [1] and Cochrane found no randomised trials of surgery [2]; surgery on the sole can leave a painful scar [14]. Lasers and photodynamic therapy follow.

Other treatments: what evidence, and what in Taiwan?

These are small or early studies, not standard care, mostly without Taiwanese data: intralesional acyclovir (Egypt, 68 people with plantar warts: 68.6% of warts cleared vs 61.1% with freezing, more painful) [30]; non-thermal plasma (US, 112 warts in 14 people aged 4–21, 51.8% vs 60.7% with freezing) [29]; saturated salt foot soaks (China, 17 people with stubborn plantar warts, 15 clear at 8 weeks, no comparison group) [31]; Er:YAG plus Nd:YAG (Israel, 24 people with stubborn warts, 48% vs 29% cleared after one session) [32]; needle-free 5-FU (China, 76 people with palm and sole warts, 63.16% vs 21.05% with freezing, complete response not defined [26]; 5-FU is contraindicated in pregnancy [19]); HPV vaccine, see below.

Face, nails, soles and flat warts: what to watch

Face and flat warts

For warts on the face, do not use salicylic acid or freezing sprays yourself: the British guideline lists acid paints as contraindicated on the face [1], and doctors often avoid acid and freezing for flat facial warts because of irritation and pale marks [14]. Flat warts do not hurt, and the British guideline says clearance can often be awaited [1]; creams such as tretinoin, imiquimod or fluorouracil may be used [14], though none is licensed for skin warts in Taiwan [40] and no valid topical fluorouracil licence was found [40]; it is also contraindicated in pregnancy [19]. Irritation from these creams can leave dark marks, more so in darker skin [14], and destructive treatment scars more easily on the face and backs of the hands [1]. Do not shave over filiform warts in the beard area [1][14].

Warts on the eyelids, lips, or edges of the nostrils or mouth: our sources do not address them specifically. Do not paint or freeze them yourself; see a dermatologist, and for eyelids an eye doctor if needed (general safety advice).

A 2001 report from a hospital in Changhua, Taiwan, treated 7 people with pigmented flat warts that had failed freezing and topical retinoic acid with a 532 nm laser; none recurred at 6 months, but 2 turned light brown and took about 4 months to recover. With no comparison and so few people, it cannot show efficacy [8].

Around and under the nail

Periungual and subungual warts are harder to treat than warts elsewhere, and recurrence is high in adults [33]. Overly aggressive freezing can harm the nail matrix and permanently deform the nail [14][33]; because of pain and scarring, expert (ungraded) advice is generally to avoid freezing and, in children, to use topical keratolytics or watch [33].

The same paper thinks intralesional bleomycin may come first in adults [33], which differs from the British grade [1] and is off-label in Taiwan [40]; it is painful, causes necrosis and scabbing, with Raynaud's reported on digits [33], and is not advised in children, pregnancy, weak immunity or vascular disease [14]. Nail biting and picking may spread the warts, so it is best to stop [33]. For long-standing, immunosuppressed or unresponsive cases the literature advises a biopsy for squamous cell carcinoma [33].

Soles

Plantar warts have lower cure rates, probably because of the thick skin [1]. Deal first with pain and pressure: pare thick skin, use padding and well-fitting shoes (padding and shoes are unstudied comfort measures), then choose acid or freezing with modest expectations. Keep the acid on the wart and off normal skin [1]. Do not use salicylic acid on numb feet or areas that heal poorly [1][14]; with diabetes or poor circulation see a doctor first and do not pare it yourself (this site's cautious advice).

Children, pregnancy, weak immunity, diabetes

Children

Children's warts are mostly short-lived and tend to clear within 1–2 years [1], so there is no rush to treat. Painful treatment should be avoided in young children if possible [1][14]. If needed, the British options are salicylic acid 15–40% or gentle freezing (every 2 weeks for 3–4 months) [1], guided by a doctor. Our sources give no minimum age or maximum area for salicylic acid, so let the doctor decide; bleomycin is not advised for children [14]. Imiquimod in Taiwan is licensed for adults only [40].

Genital or perianal warts need a prompt assessment. Many children probably acquire them without sexual contact, so do not start by blaming yourself or anyone else; but the doctor must seriously consider possible sexual abuse, and neither age nor HPV type can rule it out, see the warning signs [17]. Our sources do not state Taiwan's reporting rules.

Pregnancy and breastfeeding

Tell the doctor if you are, may be or plan to be pregnant, or are breastfeeding. Our sources have no safety data on salicylic acid or freezing for hand and foot warts in pregnancy; most limits below come from the genital-wart literature. A wart that does not bother you can be discussed with the doctor and left until after birth.

  • Contraindicated in pregnancy: podophyllotoxin (contraindicated in the British guideline [1]; another source contraindicates both it and podophyllin [19]; the Chinese guideline prints "not recommended" in its recommendation and "prohibited" in its text [3]); 5-FU (potential fetal harm) [19]. Oral acitretin is teratogenic [33].
  • Avoid or not advised in pregnancy: imiquimod and sinecatechins (too few data: generally not recommended, and imiquimod should be avoided, per one source [19]; for imiquimod the Chinese guideline prints "not recommended" in its recommendation and "prohibited" in its text [3]); bleomycin is not advised [14]; cidofovir was teratogenic in animals, with no human pregnancy data [19].
  • Usable by a doctor in pregnancy (genital warts): trichloroacetic acid [3][19]; freezing (safe and effective per one source [19], a weak recommendation in the Chinese guideline [3]).
  • Not addressed by our sources: whether and how long to use contraception with podophyllotoxin, 5-FU, imiquimod and the like; tretinoin, cantharidin or MMR injection for warts in pregnancy; safety in breastfeeding. If you could become pregnant, ask the doctor before starting.

Weak immunity

People with transplants, chemotherapy, HIV or biologics or immunosuppressants may have many large, resistant warts, and treatment often aims to reduce bulk and discomfort rather than clear them [1]. Do not stop immunosuppressants yourself. Sudden increase, large size, ulcers or long-standing lesions at a nail raise concern for skin cancer or a deeper immune problem [1][33]. Among 13 cases at a medical centre in Taoyuan in 2003–2019 (5 acquired EV, 8 generalised verrucosis), all 5 EV cases were immunosuppressed; this is a small single-hospital series [9].

  • Treatments needing special care: bleomycin is not advised [14]; for nail warts, destructive methods such as bleomycin injection and freezing risk skin breakdown and infection [33]; MMR is a live attenuated vaccine, with more adverse effects in weak immunity, and needs particular caution [34]; cidofovir has only case-level evidence (British grade D) and can harm the kidneys when given intravenously [1]; HPV vaccine as treatment is unproven [14][28].

Diabetes and poor circulation

See a doctor first; do not pare, use acid or freeze it yourself (not paring or freezing is this site's cautious advice). Salicylic acid should not be used with neuropathy or poor healing [1][14]; freezing needs caution with poor circulation [1]; the EVerT trial excluded diabetes, peripheral vascular disease and other poor healing, so its results do not apply [20].

One week is the billing minimum; shorter intervals bring more reports of pain and blisters [1].

HPV, vaccines and genital warts: do not mix them with skin warts

Skin warts and genital warts are different diseases. Hand and foot warts are mostly HPV 1, 2, 4, 27 and 57 [1]; genital warts are mostly types 6 and 11, spread mainly by sexual contact, with an incubation usually of 3 weeks to 8 months [16]. Treatment and tests differ: do not use genital-wart medicines on hand or foot warts, nor hand-and-foot salicylic acid on genitals.

  • Suspected genital or perianal warts: see dermatology, urology or gynaecology. Warts at the urethral opening go to urology; warts near the anus often need anoscopy or a colorectal opinion; women should have a speculum examination and cervical screening, plus checks for other sexually transmitted infections [16].
  • Genital warts in brief: types 6 and 11 carry a low cancer risk and malignant change is rare, but co-infection with high-risk types is common and the risk is higher with weak immunity, so keep to the checks; about one-third regress within 4 months without treatment, and they may recur [16]. Tell current partners; condoms help but do not fully prevent spread; imiquimod and sinecatechins may weaken condoms [18].
  • Vaccines: the nine-valent vaccine (Gardasil 9) covers HPV 6, 11, 16, 18, 31, 33, 45, 52 and 58; in Taiwan it is licensed for ages 9–45 to prevent related precancerous lesions, cancers and genital warts, not skin warts [40]. The common skin types 1, 2, 3, 4 and 10 are not in it. Taiwan's adult HPV vaccine guidance does not mention skin warts at all [4].
  • Who may have it (for prevention): the Taiwanese guidance strongly recommends it for women and men through age 26; at 27–45 by shared decision (weak for women, very weak evidence for men); not recommended over 45 (limited evidence); adults need 3 doses (0, 1–2 and 6 months). Contraindicated with hypersensitivity to a component (including severe allergy to yeast or after a previous dose). Recommended in HIV (waiting until virologically suppressed with CD4 of at least 200 may be considered) and weakly in transplant recipients (before, or at least 1 year after, transplantation) and autoimmune inflammatory rheumatic disease [4]. The 2025 guidance says women born in 1994 or later are publicly funded through school, others pay themselves [4]; check the health authorities' latest notice, including for men.
  • Vaccines do not clear warts that already exist: the Taiwanese guidance says prophylactic vaccines have no direct therapeutic effect on existing lesions [4]; they are also not recommended to treat genital warts [18]. For hand and foot warts the effect is unproven [14], and the Taiwanese licence does not cover them [40].
  • Where research stands: a Korean open study of 45 people found 62.2% complete response (no control group; the route is given only as "injected") [27]; a French retrospective series of 18 (78% immunosuppressed) had 11% complete remission at 1 year, with 94% also using other treatments and 44% of responders relapsing; the authors registered a placebo-controlled trial with no results in our sources [28]. Small trials of injecting vaccine into warts are cited inconsistently [34].

Pregnancy and breastfeeding: the Taiwanese guidance has only safety data and does not say whether to vaccinate in pregnancy, so ask a doctor [4]; the genital-wart literature says the vaccine is neither approved nor studied in pregnancy [19]. Foreign trials cited in the guidance's section on men through age 26 give 89.4–90.4% efficacy against anogenital warts (four-valent vaccine) [4]; that is prevention and not about hand and foot warts.

How to prevent warts

No large study proves which habit prevents hand and foot warts. These are steps from the literature or plain sense; do what you can.

  • Do not pick, bite or cut warts on yourself or others [14]; stop nail biting and picking [33].
  • Slippers in shared showers and changing rooms may reduce spread [14].
  • Do not share wart files; near warts use an electric razor or do not shave [14]. Not sharing razors is general hygiene advice.
  • Treat dry, cracked skin, hand eczema and athlete's foot: broken skin is easier to infect [14].
  • Warts spread by skin contact [14]. Our sources do not discuss whether to avoid ordinary social contact (such as holding hands or hugging) and do not ask for time off school; the point is not to pick or bite and to protect broken skin.

Common myths about warts

Myth 1: warts always go away, so never treat them?

Half right. Without symptoms and with normal immunity you can wait [1]: about half of children clear within 1 year [21], while adults are slower and 5–10 years is not uncommon per the British guideline [1]. But "never treat" is wrong: pain, disruption, distress, persistence or weak immunity are reasons to treat [14]. Conversely, "one wart becomes twenty unless treated, and treatment stops spread" is not supported by our sources either.

Myth 2: banana peel, garlic or vinegar cures warts?

Our sources contain no efficacy studies of banana peel or vinegar; garlic has one non-randomised trial, and pure garlic irritates skin [1]; plant evidence is thin and some plant saps burn [1][14]. Herbal treatment, homeopathy and hypnosis are listed as insufficient evidence in the British guideline, and a homeopathy trial found no difference from placebo; for acupuncture it lists one small trial (ear acupuncture for flat warts 16/30 vs tretinoin 1/30) [1]; Chinese herbal medicine, coix seed and moxibustion for warts are not specifically addressed. Because warts can clear alone, "it worked" may be coincidence. Do not put acid, vinegar or garlic on skin. The home option with randomised trial evidence is salicylic acid; tape is uncertain.

Myth 3: one freeze or laser clears it?

Usually several sessions. Freezing is mostly every 2–3 weeks, and changing the method is reasonable after about 6 without response [1][14]; full-course cure varies widely by source (generally about 20–50% per Cochrane) [2], and was only 14% for plantar warts in EVerT [20]. The British guideline gives salicylic acid strength A and freezing strength B, not "1+/A" for both [1]. Laser trials abroad also took several sessions and were not clearly better than freezing [14][25]. "One CO2 laser or cautery clears 80%" has no high-quality support, and scarring is possible [1][33].

Myth 4: squeeze it or cut it off yourself?

No. The literature advises against manipulating your own or anyone else's warts [14]; scraping the skin beside one can spread virus [1]. The virus is in the epidermis, not the dermis, so "it lives in the dermis and regrows in 1–2 weeks" has no support in our sources; the real problems are pain, bleeding, infection and scars. Filiform warts can be snipped by a doctor [14]. See one for bleeding, swelling or pus.

Myth 5: HPV vaccine is only about cervical cancer, nothing to do with warts?

It prevents genital warts and several cancers (not only cervical but also anal, oropharyngeal and others, for women and men) [40][4], but not the common hand and foot warts, and it does not treat existing ones [4]. The claim that it "still has skin-related benefit" is not supported by evidence. "Prevents about 90% of genital warts" mixes two things: about 90% is the share of genital warts caused by those types [16], while the four-valent vaccine had 89.4–90.4% efficacy in men [4].

Myth 6: plantar warts are corns; just file them down?

They differ. A plantar wart is HPV, shows clotted capillaries on paring and hides skin lines; a corn lacks those capillaries [1][14]. "Filing helps corns and only spreads warts" is not quite right either: paring is part of wart care before acid or freezing [1][14], but file only the wart [1] and do not share the tool [14]; with diabetes or numb feet do not do it yourself (this site's cautious advice). If unsure, see a dermatologist.

Myth 7: warts always turn into cancer?

Ordinary hand and foot warts rarely become cancer [3]. The worry is cancers that look like warts: irregular, ulcerated or resistant [14], or long-standing beside a nail [33]; also the rare EV, where over 60% develop cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma, including in situ (early cancer still in the epidermis), at an early age [15].

Myth 8: warts are contagious, so isolate the child?

Warts do spread, but no source asks for isolation or time off school; spread is by skin contact and possibly the environment, and cases clustered in Kaohsiung but not in Penghu schools [5][6]. Teach children not to pick [14] and not to share nail tools or razors.

Myth 9: do warts in pregnancy harm the baby?

Two questions. Hand and foot warts: our sources have no data showing harm to the baby. Genital warts: they may grow and bleed in pregnancy [3] and need obstetric assessment. HPV can be passed to the baby; the most severe outcome is the rare juvenile-onset respiratory papillomatosis (warts in the throat and airway, usually diagnosed at age 2–5 and sometimes needing several operations): a Danish registry study estimated about 7 per 1000 affected mothers [19]; the Chinese guideline prints 4 per million [3], with a different denominator.

Treating the warts may not lower the chance of passing HPV to the baby; caesarean delivery has not been proven to prevent it and is not advised for that reason alone [19]. Caesarean is advised when warts could block the birth canal or cause heavy bleeding, in both the Chinese guideline and other literature [3][19]. Podophyllotoxin and 5-FU are contraindicated in pregnancy and imiquimod should be avoided, see pregnancy [19].

When to see a doctor, and which one?

  • See dermatology or family medicine first: unsure if it is a wart; no better after 12 weeks of home salicylic acid [14]; painful or affecting walking; multiplying; on the face, beside a nail or genital; diabetes, numb feet, weak immunity or pregnancy.
  • Same day: after treatment, spreading redness, pus, fever, blackening or severe pain; a foot wound with diabetes or poor circulation; heavy bleeding.
  • Refer to dermatology: suspected skin cancer, long-standing nail lesions, many warts with weak immunity, suspected EV or failed treatment; genital warts go to dermatology, urology or gynaecology, and a child's genital wart needs prompt assessment.

Before the visit: how long, how it has changed, what you used and for how long, pain, diabetes or weak immunity, all medicines, pregnancy; bring photos. There are no shortcuts: pick a method that suits you, keep expectations modest and review whether to change it, rather than only adding sessions. For other viral skin diseases see mpox and shingles; other questions are in the dermatology FAQ.

Frequently asked questions

Do ointments recommended online work?

First sort them out: salicylic acid has randomised evidence, "real but modest" [2], and results vary, with only 14% at 12 weeks in EVerT [20], so a fixed "50–70% clearance" is not supported by our sources. Imiquimod on hand or foot warts is off-label [40]. Do not buy prescription medicine yourself.

How long until a wart goes?

About half of children are wart-free within 1 year [21]; adults are slower [1]. Treatment has no guaranteed time: home salicylic acid is used for 12 weeks (3–4 months in the British regimen) [14][1]; freezing takes several sessions every 2–3 weeks, and changing is reasonable after 6 without response [14]. Weak immunity may take longer.

Do warts come back?

Yes, recurrence is common, and a vanished wart does not prove nearby virus is gone [14]. There is no reliable recurrence rate, only scattered figures: in a questionnaire on freezing of hand warts, 83% thought they were cured at the end but only 57% were clear after a median of 19 months [2]; in one trial none of 33 people cured by freezing and followed up had relapsed at 12 months [25]; there are no data for salicylic acid. Recurrence of nail warts is high in adults [33]. If it returns, go back and confirm it is a wart.

Will it leave a scar or dark mark?

Possibly. Freezing can lighten or darken skin, more in darker skin, and scars more when aggressive [1][14]; surgery on the sole can leave a painful scar [14]. The British guideline says treatment ideally should not scar [1]. How long marks last is not stated.

Do I need laser or HPV typing?

Not necessarily. Lasers were not clearly better than conventional treatment or freezing in trials [14][25]. Ordinary hand and foot warts are diagnosed by appearance [14]; pathology and typing are for atypical, suspicious or uncertain lesions [3].

Are freezing and laser covered by NHI?

Freezing items (51017C, 51021C and 51022C) list viral warts, billed once per visit and at least one week apart [38]. No laser item lists warts; the injection items do not name warts, so ask the clinic [38]. The claim that "NHI covers intralesional bleomycin" has no supporting rule. Ask the clinic about prices.

How to tell a plantar wart from a corn, and NHI?

Look for dots and skin lines: warts show clotted capillaries on paring and hide the lines, and corns lack those capillaries [14][1]. "Side squeeze hurts more" is not reliable. Plantar warts count as viral warts in the NHI freezing items [38]; corns are not warts, so ask the clinic about care and coverage.

Does HPV vaccine help skin warts?

It is unproven so far [14]. The vaccine prevents genital warts and related cancers but does not cover the common skin types [40]; using it to treat warts is experimental, with mixed results [14][27][28].

Can I exercise or swim with warts?

No source says no. If a plantar wart hurts, try padding and well-fitting shoes; wear slippers around shared showers and pools and do not share wart files [14]. Skin softened by water is easier to infect [14], so dry off.

May I use an over-the-counter freezing kit?

They are not as cold as liquid nitrogen and probably less effective per the British guideline, though one randomised trial found them equal [1]; Cochrane did not assess them [2]. Do not use one on the face, genitals, beside nails, on feet with diabetes, numbness or poor circulation, or on an uncertain lump (general safety advice).

Periungual, children, immunosuppressed, EV

  • Periungual: an expert, ungraded review favours bleomycin first in adults (injected or multipuncture) or cryotherapy with topical 5-FU, imiquimod or salicylic acid; it generally avoids cryotherapy because of pain and scarring, and if used, pares first, gives two cycles and spares the proximal nail fold. Singal and Grover: 80 people, 250 warts, 3 U/mL, mean 2.61 sessions, severe pain and necrosis 1.2%, recurrence 3.2% at 8–10 weeks. Children: watching, topical keratolytics or immunotherapy, with bleomycin usable in adolescents but usually avoided in younger children because of pain ([14] advises against it in children); no placebo controls. Incubation is weeks to more than 1 year [33].
  • Children: watching is fine; avoid painful treatment when young [1]. One trial: imiquimod plus 15% salicylic acid vs cryotherapy, 81% vs 67% at 3 months, not significant, better only in a plantar subgroup [14]. In the Dutch cohort treated children cleared less often (hazard ratio 0.6), which the authors attribute to selection [21].
  • Anogenital warts in children: most may be acquired non-sexually (caregivers, autoinoculation, perinatal), but abuse needs serious consideration. In a retrospective study of 55 children under 13, compared with those under 4, abuse was 12.1 times as likely at 8–12 years (95% confidence interval 2.3–63.6) and 2.9 times at 4–8 years (0.7–12.4, not significant); be more suspicious above age 2, but neither age nor typing excludes it. Evaluation covers interviews with caregivers and child, examination and screening for other sexually transmitted infections; report suggestive findings according to local policies, and refer to a child-abuse specialist if not comfortable. Exceptions: adolescents reporting consensual activity, and immunosuppressed children with multiple non-genital warts and no other suggestive findings [17]. Taiwan's reporting rules are not in our sources.
  • Pregnancy: podophyllotoxin is contraindicated [1][19], as are podophyllin and 5-FU [19]; avoid imiquimod, sinecatechins are generally not recommended and interferon is contraindicated [19] (imiquimod in the Chinese recommendation: "not recommended" 1A, text "prohibited") [3]; bleomycin is not advised [14]; cidofovir is teratogenic in animals [19]; trichloroacetic acid is acceptable (1A [3]; preferred drug in [19]); cryotherapy is safe and effective per [19], and cryotherapy or surgery is 2C in the Chinese guideline [3]. These concern genital warts; contraception, breastfeeding, tretinoin and MMR are not addressed; there are no data on salicylic acid or cryotherapy for skin warts in pregnancy, and trials excluded pregnant and breastfeeding women [24][30].
  • Immunosuppressed: often aim to debulk; in 72 paediatric oncology patients (retrospective) complete resolution was 24.0% during active cancer treatment and 63.3% when not on it; destructive methods such as bleomycin injection and cryotherapy risk skin breakdown and infection (bleomycin not recommended in [14], alternated with salicylic acid or 5-FU in [33]; destructive methods are avoided when several digits are involved [33]) [33][14]; MMR is live attenuated and needs caution [34]; Korean transplant aHR for cutaneous warts: kidney 4.35, liver 2.78, stem cell 2.77 [36]. Keep a low threshold for biopsy of ulcerated, persistent or unresponsive lesions [33][14].
  • EV: polymorphic flat papules and tinea versicolor-like macules since childhood; diagnosis needs biopsy plus HPV testing, with TMC6/TMC8 for suspected inherited forms; about 500 cases worldwide. No cure; retinoids help briefly; strict sun protection; actinic keratoses, Bowen disease and low-risk squamous cell carcinoma can be frozen, low-risk and higher-recurrence-risk cancers excised, avoid radiotherapy; lifelong review, yearly without cancer and possibly every 3–6 months after invasive cancer. Acquired EV starts later, mainly with transplants and HIV, and treatment is to reduce immunosuppression or treat the cause [15]; the Chinese guideline also asks for sun education (1D) [3].
  • Recalcitrant warts and inborn errors of immunity: many such patients have no obvious immune defect [35], but unusually severe or prolonged warts should prompt a search for one [1]; known inborn errors mostly involve T cells [35]. Tree-man syndrome is extremely rare and includes CD28 deficiency; WHIM shows warts, recurrent infections and neutropenia [35][15]. HPV vaccine was generally unsuccessful for EV or tree-man lesions, but the vaccine does not contain those types [35].

Infection control in the clinic

The virus survives in liquid nitrogen, so dispense a small amount into a disposable cup rather than dipping swabs into a shared flask [14]. Plume from laser or high-temperature evaporation may carry HPV; the Chinese guideline advises precautions against contact with the particles (2C) and states at 2B that plume could transmit HPV [3]. Taiwan's adult HPV vaccine guidance says HPV DNA can be found in the environment and surgical smoke without evidence of viable spread by these routes, and that frequently exposed clinicians may consider vaccination for self-protection [4].

What Taiwan lacks, and when to refer

  • We found none from Taiwan: a cutaneous wart guideline, randomised trials, adult or population prevalence, recent plantar-wart and typing data, natural course, recurrence, or studies specifically on pools or meat handling.
  • All intervals, strengths and criteria here come mainly from foreign data; the NHI one-week gap is only a billing rule.
  • Refer: suspected malignancy or long-standing nail lesions, multiple warts with weak immunity, suspected EV, genital warts in a child, no response to 6 freezes or 12 weeks of salicylic acid.
