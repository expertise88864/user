Key points in 30 seconds

Key points
  • Psoriasis is a chronic inflammatory skin disease driven by immune dysregulation. It is not a fungal infection and it is not contagious; in Taiwan's insurance data, diagnosed prevalence rose from about 0.18% in 2006 to about 0.86% in 2017 [1][18][13].
  • The commonest form is plaque psoriasis: sharply bordered red plaques with thick scale, typically on the scalp, elbows, knees and lower back; it can also affect only the nails, skin folds or palms and soles [15].
  • By the Taiwanese consensus, psoriasis is moderate to severe if BSA, PASI or DLQI is 10 or more; involvement of special sites such as the face, scalp, palms, soles, genitals or nails can count as moderate to severe even when small [1].
  • Treatment steps up with severity from topicals to phototherapy, tablets, biologics and targeted small molecules. There is no cure, but most people can achieve good control. Taiwan's NHI covers biologics for moderate-to-severe disease that meets set criteria and passes prior review [1][30].
  • Psoriasis is more than skin deep: watch for joint pain or swelling, morning stiffness and a whole swollen finger (sausage digit), which suggest psoriatic arthritis, and look after weight, blood pressure, glucose, lipids and mood. Stopping smoking and managing weight help [1][17][18].

What is psoriasis?

Psoriasis is an immune-mediated chronic inflammatory skin disease: keratinocytes differentiate abnormally and proliferate too fast, immune cells infiltrate the dermis and small blood vessels increase [1]. It was once seen as simply skin growing too fast; we now know the core problem is immune. Dendritic cells and macrophages release interleukin-23 (IL-23), which drives Th17 and related T cells to make IL-17; IL-17 makes keratinocytes proliferate and release more inflammatory signals, a self-sustaining loop [15][16]. Normal epidermal cells take about 27 days to move from the base to the surface; in a plaque it takes about 4 days, so scale piles up [15].

Is it inherited? About 40% of patients have a family history of psoriasis or psoriatic arthritis, and identical twins are more often both affected than non-identical twins; more than 100 genetic loci are involved, with HLA-Cw6 most strongly linked to early-onset and guttate psoriasis [15]. Carrying risk genes does not mean you will get it; environmental factors such as smoking, obesity, infection and medicines also matter [15].

How common is it? Adult prevalence ranges from about 0.5% to 11.4% worldwide and rises with distance from the equator [15]. In Taiwan, insurance data give a one-year prevalence of about 0.19% to 0.235% (2006 data), lower than in Western countries, Japan, Korea and Malaysia; the incidence of new psoriasis vulgaris fell from 43.33 to 23.14 per 100,000 between 2002 and 2016, although the consensus notes that overall prevalence is still rising [1]. Another insurance study (counting anyone with one diagnosis code from a dermatologist or rheumatologist) found prevalence rising from about 0.18% to 0.86% between 2006 and 2017, while incidence stayed at 62–65 per 100,000 person-years; in Taiwan, age at first diagnosis peaks at 30–39 and again at 80–89 years, more people are diagnosed after 40 than before, and adult men outnumber women [13]. Definitions differ between studies, so the figures are not directly comparable. Worldwide there is no clear sex difference (though adult men predominate in Taiwanese insurance data); it can start at any age, with peaks at 30–39 and 50–69 years, and about a third of adult patients first had symptoms before age 20 [15][21].

Types of psoriasis

The Taiwanese consensus lists plaque, guttate, inverse, pustular (generalized and localized), erythrodermic and special-site psoriasis [1]. One person can have several types at once, and the type can change over time [15].

  • Plaque: the commonest type, 55% to 79% of cases across studies. Red, sharply bordered plaques with thick silvery scale, from under 1 cm to over 10 cm, usually symmetrical, often on the scalp, elbows, knees and buttock crease [15]. In darker skin, redness is less obvious and plaques may look violet or brown [15][21].
  • Guttate: a sudden crop of many small (2–15 mm) red spots, mainly on the trunk and proximal limbs; commonest in children and adults under 30, often 2–3 weeks after a streptococcal sore throat [26]. Some clear within weeks to months; others go on to plaque psoriasis [26].
  • Inverse (flexural): in the armpits, groin, genitals, buttock crease or under the breasts; shiny with little or no scale and often mistaken for a fungal or bacterial infection. Genital involvement particularly affects quality of life [15].
  • Scalp: very common and often the first sign in children; unlike seborrhoeic dermatitis, plaques often extend beyond the hairline to the forehead, around the ears and the back of the neck [15][21].
  • Nail: 10% to 55% of patients have nail changes, including pits, separation of the nail from its bed (onycholysis), yellow-brown oil-drop patches, thickening under the nail and crumbling; it is easily confused with fungal nail infection [25][15]. Nail psoriasis is more often accompanied by psoriatic arthritis [25].
  • Palmoplantar: thick, scaly skin on the palms and soles that cracks painfully and can limit walking and work [15]. Palmoplantar pustulosis, with recurring sterile pustules on the palms and soles, is strongly linked to smoking; whether it is a form of psoriasis is still debated [28].
  • Erythrodermic (emergency): most of the skin (75% or more) becomes red and peeling, sometimes with fever, chills and a fast heart rate. Loss of the skin barrier risks dehydration, electrolyte problems and serious infection, and most patients need admission [27].
  • Pustular: generalized pustular psoriasis (GPP) is rare but potentially life-threatening, with sudden sheets of pinhead sterile pustules on inflamed skin, fever and feeling unwell, and repeated flares; localized forms affect only the palms and soles or the ends of the fingers and toes [2][15][28].

Symptoms and course

  • Itch is common, though some people have none; cracks on the hands and feet hurt [15].
  • Koebner phenomenon: new psoriasis appears at sites of skin injury such as scratches, cuts or tattoos [15][26].
  • It waxes and wanes: plaque psoriasis is usually chronic with varying severity; healed plaques may leave darker or lighter marks that take months or longer to fade [15][18].
  • Impact on life: even limited psoriasis can cause real distress; depression, anxiety and suicidal thoughts are more common than in the general population, especially in young people and women [1][17].

Triggers and aggravating factors

  • Infection: streptococcal throat infection is the clearest link, with guttate psoriasis; HIV infection can also trigger or worsen psoriasis [15][26].
  • Medicines: beta blockers, lithium and antimalarials (such as hydroxychloroquine) are the most often implicated; immune checkpoint inhibitors for cancer can also trigger it. If a medicine seems linked, talk to the prescriber rather than stopping it on your own [15]. A joint Taiwanese dermatology–cardiology consensus notes that evidence linking blood-pressure drugs to flares is of low certainty, so any class (including beta blockers) can be used as usual unless a specific drug clearly worsens your psoriasis [3].
  • Stopping oral steroids suddenly can trigger pustular or erythrodermic psoriasis; oral steroids are generally not recommended for psoriasis [27][28][24].
  • Smoking: odds ratios for developing psoriasis are 1.39 with a smoking history and 1.93 in current smokers, possibly rising with amount and duration [17].
  • Obesity and alcohol: in a study of about 80,000 women, a BMI of 30 or more at age 18 carried a relative risk of 1.73 for later psoriasis; in another of more than 82,000 women, 2.3 or more drinks a week carried a relative risk of 1.72. Heavy drinking is also linked to more severe disease and poorer treatment response [17].
  • Stress is often blamed, but a meta-analysis found insufficient data to confirm a link [15].

How is it diagnosed? Are blood tests or a biopsy needed?

Most plaque psoriasis is diagnosed by examining the skin; no blood test confirms it and genetic testing is not used [15]. The doctor will look at the whole skin including the scalp, nails and genital area, and ask about family history and medicines; a small skin biopsy (usually a 4 mm punch) is taken only when the diagnosis is uncertain, sometimes with a fungal stain [15].

Common look-alikes include seborrhoeic dermatitis (fine greasy scale around the eyebrows and nose), eczema, nummular eczema, lichen simplex chronicus and fungal infection (tinea), which a microscopy test (KOH) can confirm [15]. Let a doctor confirm it rather than trying creams on your own. Nail changes often need a clipping tested for fungus to exclude or detect onychomycosis [25]. Guttate psoriasis must be told apart from pityriasis rosea and syphilis [26].

How is severity assessed, and what is the goal?

The Taiwanese consensus uses four tools: body surface area (BSA), the Psoriasis Area and Severity Index (PASI), the physician's global assessment (PGA) and the Dermatology Life Quality Index (DLQI) [1]. To estimate area, the patient's whole palm with the fingers together is about 1% of body surface [18].

Treatment goals: for moderate-to-severe disease, a 75% improvement in PASI (PASI 75) defines success; in Taiwan a 50%–74% improvement with a clearly better quality of life (DLQI 5 or less) is also acceptable, while less than 50% improvement calls for a change of treatment whatever the quality of life [1]. The optimal target is minimal disease activity: PASI improved by 90% or more, clear or almost clear on physician assessment (IGA 0/1), an absolute PASI of 1 or less, or BSA of 1% or less [1].

Treatment: the ladder from creams to biologics

Psoriasis cannot yet be cured, but many treatments are proven to relieve symptoms and control the disease well [1]. The choice depends on severity and sites, comorbidities such as arthritis, pregnancy plans, response to previous treatment, and your lifestyle and preferences [1][18]. Because treatment does not cure, most people need long-term treatment to keep the benefit [18].

  • Mild: mainly topical treatment plus general care [1].
  • Moderate to severe: phototherapy or conventional systemic drugs (methotrexate, ciclosporin, acitretin); if these are not enough, not tolerated or unsuitable, biologics or targeted small molecules are used instead or added. When disease is severe and light or conventional drugs are unlikely to succeed, a biologic or small molecule can be the first choice [1], although NHI reimbursement still requires prior phototherapy and conventional drugs (with separate rules for erythrodermic disease, certolizumab in pregnancy and generalized pustular psoriasis; see the FAQ) [30].
  • Combining treatments: topicals, phototherapy and systemic drugs can be combined to boost efficacy, balance risks and improve long-term control [1].
  • Guttate (small spots after a sore throat): it may clear within weeks to months, and treatment is optional; for widespread spots narrowband UVB is the mainstay, with topical steroids or vitamin D analogues as alternatives. Antibiotics are needed only for an active streptococcal infection (sore throat, tonsillar pus, fever); routine antibiotics or tonsillectomy lack evidence and are not routinely advised [26]. If it recurs several times a year or does not respond to light or creams, tonsillectomy or systemic drugs may be considered (limited evidence) [26], best assessed by a dermatologist.

How to use topical treatments

Topicals are the mainstay for mild psoriasis and are often added to phototherapy or systemic treatment for remaining thick plaques [1][18]. Success depends on regular, sustained use; a greasy, messy or complicated regimen often gets abandoned, so tell your doctor if a product is hard to use and ask about another formulation or a simpler routine [18].

  • Topical corticosteroids: mild potency first for the face, skin folds, thin skin, children and older people; moderate or high potency for other sites in adults. A potent steroid once a day is enough; do not use it for more than 4 weeks without a review, and reduce frequency as your doctor advises once improved [1]. Side effects include skin thinning and acne-like spots; long-term use over large areas can suppress the adrenal glands [19]. For amounts and potency classes see our guide to topical steroids.
  • Vitamin D analogues and combinations: calcipotriol and calcitriol can be used alone or with a steroid; the calcipotriol–betamethasone combination comes as ointment, gel or foam and on the body the ointment works better than either ingredient alone; on the scalp the gel beats calcipotriol alone [1][19]; a scalp meta-analysis shows a modest edge over a steroid alone (risk ratio 1.22) [19], whereas the Taiwanese consensus considers superiority over betamethasone alone unclear [1]; the ointment suits the nails [1]. Irritation is the main side effect; with large amounts watch blood calcium, and the safe upper limit of calcipotriol is 100 g a week [1][19].
  • Tazarotene (a topical retinoid): once each evening on plaques only, avoiding normal skin and folds; it is often paired with a steroid to boost effect and reduce irritation. Do not use it in pregnancy [1][19].
  • Topical calcineurin inhibitors (tacrolimus, pimecrolimus): suited to the face and skin folds, twice daily; burning and itch at first usually settle; avoid applying straight after bathing. Their use in psoriasis is off-label [1]. The boxed cancer warning on the label dates from US concerns in 2006; a link has not been proven [19].
  • Adjuncts: emollients and moisturizers are essential support; salicylic acid and high-strength urea soften thick scale and help other medicines penetrate [1][19]. The Taiwanese consensus (2024) states that coal tar preparations are not available in Taiwan and that products contain about 2%–3% pine tar [1]; TFDA data, however, list a tar shampoo whose indications include psoriasis and a coal-tar ointment not indicated for psoriasis [31].
  • How much: your doctor estimates amounts by body area or fingertip units; using too little is a common reason for poor results [1][18].

Improvement usually starts within weeks, and steroids typically work clearly within 4 weeks [19]. If proper use for 4–8 weeks is not enough, or long-term heavy use of potent steroids is needed to keep control, discuss adding or stepping up treatment [19]. For creams and injections for nail psoriasis, see How to treat nail psoriasis.

Caring for special sites

  • Scalp: foams or solutions are usually easier than ointments in hair (some people prefer oils or ointments); soften thick scale with a salicylic acid (2%–6%) or tar shampoo, leaving tar shampoo on for 5–10 minutes; a steroid shampoo is a poor main treatment for people who wash their hair no more than once a week [19]. Avoid frequent vigorous combing, brushing or shampooing (Koebner phenomenon) [21]. The calcipotriol–betamethasone gel suits the scalp [1].
  • Genitals and skin folds: psoriasis is not contagious, including through sexual contact [18]. These areas are prone to steroid side effects, so a mild steroid for short periods or a topical calcineurin inhibitor is usual; tazarotene is avoided and phototherapy usually spares the genitals [19][20]. Redness here can also be fungal or bacterial infection, so let a doctor check [15]. Tell your doctor if it affects your sex life or confidence [18][1].
  • Palms and soles: thick plaques absorb medicine poorly, so superpotent steroids are often needed, boosted by overnight occlusion and keratolytics [19][22]; targeted phototherapy suits these sites [18]. If creams fail or walking or work is affected, systemic treatment is considered earlier, and NHI has criteria for recalcitrant palmoplantar psoriasis [18][30].
  • Nails: nails grow slowly, so patience is needed: topical treatment is usually reviewed after 3 months, and a second-line option is tried if there is no response after 3–6 months; injections into the nail matrix are moderately effective for some changes but painful; for more extensive or disabling nail disease biologics are the preferred first-line option with the best evidence [25].

Staying clear

Plaques often return after stopping [19]. You can restart when they recur, or use maintenance: on the trunk and limbs (sites at low risk of skin thinning), once better, apply the steroid on two days a week (such as weekends), or a vitamin D analogue on weekdays and the steroid at weekends, then reduce further if stable [19]; for the face, folds and genitals follow your doctor's plan rather than using steroids long term on your own [19]. Contact with your doctor about a week after starting a new cream helps you use it correctly; once stable, follow-up every 6–12 months is usual [18].

Phototherapy

Even in the biologic era, phototherapy remains a good option when there is no contraindication [1]. Narrowband UVB (NB-UVB) is used most because it works better; broadband UVB is an alternative for those who cannot tolerate it [1].

  • Frequency: at least twice a week is needed for improvement, and more sessions clear the skin faster; once clearly better, weekly maintenance can follow [1]. A clear effect is usually seen within 2–3 months [20].
  • During sessions: wear goggles, and men should shield the genitals [1]. Before a session avoid calcipotriol on plaques (UV breaks it down), and salicylic acid or sunscreen (they block UV); a thin layer of mineral oil or white petrolatum can actually improve light penetration [1].
  • Missed sessions: after a break of more than a week the dose is lowered, and after more than 4 weeks it restarts from the beginning, so keep to the schedule [1].
  • PUVA (oral psoralen plus UVA): the tablet is taken 1–2 hours before light, and sunlight must be avoided for 12 hours afterwards [1]. It can act faster but long-term use raises skin cancer risk, so it is used less now [1][20][19].
  • Excimer laser or light: treats only the plaques, suiting small stubborn areas such as the scalp, palms and soles, 2–3 times a week [1][18]. For devices see our guide to dermatology lasers.

Phototherapy does not treat psoriatic arthritis, and it is usually unsuitable for people with light-sensitive disorders or a history of melanoma or multiple skin cancers [20][23].

Tablets: conventional drugs and targeted small molecules

  • Methotrexate: taken once a week, never daily; it starts low (5–7.5 mg a week) and is raised stepwise according to response and blood tests, with a usual range of 7.5–25 mg a week (guidelines differ slightly on the upper limit) [1][5]. Folic acid, at least 5 mg a week within 24–48 hours after the last methotrexate dose, reduces side effects [1]. Regular blood tests check blood counts and liver and kidney function, and alcohol should be avoided [1][5]. Both women and men should stop it at least 3 months before trying to conceive [1].
  • Ciclosporin: fast-acting, for severe psoriasis that needs quick control; it can harm the kidneys, raise blood pressure and, long term, increase skin cancer risk, so continuous use is preferably limited to 1 year and at most 2, with blood pressure and kidney checks [1][20].
  • Acitretin (an oral retinoid): not immunosuppressive and suitable for long-term maintenance, often combined with light [1][23]. It causes dry lips and skin and raises blood lipids [20]. It causes birth defects: women who could become pregnant must use two effective methods of contraception from 1 month before, throughout and for at least 3 years after treatment, and must not donate blood during treatment or for 3 years afterwards, and should avoid alcohol during treatment [1][6]. The NHI consent form states at least 24 months of contraception after stopping, but the consensus and international guidance say 3 years, so follow 3 years [30][1].
  • Apremilast (a PDE4 inhibitor): taken twice daily with a one-week dose build-up; about 15%–20% get diarrhoea at the start, and low mood and weight loss need watching; latent TB screening is not required. It suits people with infections, cancer, metabolic disease or impaired liver or kidney function who cannot take other systemic drugs; it is contraindicated in pregnancy [1].
  • Deucravacitinib (a TYK2 inhibitor): 6 mg once daily; TB must be assessed before starting and it should not be used during serious infection [1]. At week 16, 53% to 58% reached PASI 75, compared with 35% to 40% on apremilast [1].

Biologics: what to know about injections

Biologics are antibody drugs that block a specific signal in the inflammatory loop: IL-17 inhibitors (secukinumab, ixekizumab, brodalumab, bimekizumab), IL-23 inhibitors (guselkumab, risankizumab, tildrakizumab), the IL-12/23 inhibitor ustekinumab and TNF-alpha inhibitors (adalimumab, certolizumab, etanercept) [1][20]. As a group they reach PASI 90 more often than conventional tablets [5]. Most are injected under the skin, from once a week to once every 12 weeks depending on the drug [1][20]. See our overview of biologics and targeted small molecules.

  • Screening before starting: active and latent TB are assessed and hepatitis B and C tested; carriers are monitored for reactivation during treatment [1]. NHI applications must include latent TB screening records, and treatment cannot start until latent TB therapy has run for 4 weeks [30]. Active infection, TB, sepsis and moderate-to-severe heart failure rule biologics out [1].
  • Hepatitis B or C carriers can still be treated, but need testing first and regular blood checks. In a study from 15 Taiwanese hospitals, hepatitis B reactivated in about 10.6% of treatment courses in people with hepatitis B markers: about 20.7% in carriers (surface antigen positive) and about 4.5% in those with past, resolved infection. Risk was highest with TNF inhibitors, then IL-12/23 and IL-17 inhibitors, and lowest with IL-23 inhibitors. Most reactivations were found on blood tests; about 5.6% developed hepatitis and none had fulminant liver failure [14]. Carriers without preventive antiviral treatment reactivated more often; a liver specialist decides whether to use it, and NHI does not reimburse preventive antivirals for psoriasis biologics, but antivirals are reimbursed if hepatitis B flares during immunosuppressive treatment [14][30].
  • Vaccines: evidence supports influenza, pneumococcal and shingles (non-live) vaccines for people with psoriasis; the recombinant shingles vaccine is advised for everyone over 50 and for those under 50 on a biologic plus another systemic drug [1]. Live vaccines are generally avoided during treatment, so check timing with your doctor [1][20].
  • During treatment: see your doctor early for fever, a cough lasting more than two weeks, weight loss or an infected wound; IL-17 inhibitors more often cause candida (thrush), and are usually avoided in inflammatory bowel disease (Crohn disease or ulcerative colitis) [20]. Under NHI rules, biologics other than certolizumab are not reimbursed during pregnancy or breastfeeding, so discuss pregnancy plans early [30].
  • How soon it works: response is usually judged at weeks 12–16, and some IL-17 inhibitors act faster than IL-23 inhibitors [1][20]. Not responding to one biologic does not mean others will fail [1].
  • How is one chosen? No single drug suits everyone; you and your doctor weigh injection frequency (weekly to every 12 weeks), speed (some IL-17 inhibitors act faster), arthritis (most biologics also treat joints), inflammatory bowel disease (avoid IL-17 inhibitors), multiple sclerosis or heart failure (avoid TNF inhibitors), pregnancy plans (certolizumab), hepatitis B (lowest reactivation risk with IL-23 inhibitors), a history of depression or suicidal thoughts (the French guideline suggests against brodalumab or apremilast in such patients [6]; the Taiwanese consensus notes a suicidal-ideation warning for brodalumab and advises watching mood on apremilast [1]), and NHI criteria [20][6][1][14][30].
  • How long? Most people need long-term treatment, so do not stop on your own; NHI also has a rule to pause treatment after 2 years (see the FAQ) [1][30].

For whether yearly blood tests are needed on biologics, see Do psoriasis biologics need yearly blood tests?

Daily care: skin, weight, smoking, alcohol and mood

The Taiwanese consensus states that stopping smoking, limiting alcohol, exercise, a healthy weight, a balanced diet and regular screening for related conditions all reduce the health impact of psoriasis; in people who are overweight, calorie restriction plus exercise lessens psoriasis and improves quality of life [1].

  • Moisturize: a daily emollient makes scale less visible and reduces itch and cracking; petrolatum or a thick cream straight after bathing works best [19][22].
  • Lose excess weight: in a randomized trial, overweight patients lost an average of 15.8 kg over 16 weeks and their PASI fell by 2.3 points (0.3 in controls, not statistically significant), while quality of life improved significantly [17]. People with a BMI of 30 or more are less likely to reach PASI 75 on systemic treatment [17].
  • Stop smoking, cut down alcohol: smoking is linked to a lower chance of reaching treatment goals and cessation is strongly advised; heavy drinking is linked to more severe disease and poorer response, and raises the risk of liver damage from methotrexate [17][10][20].
  • Diet: there is no evidence that particular foods must be avoided because of psoriasis. The US National Psoriasis Foundation review found high-quality support for a low-calorie diet to lose weight in overweight people; a gluten-free diet is only for confirmed coeliac disease or positive gluten antibodies; supplements such as fish oil, vitamin D, selenium and vitamin B12 lack evidence [18]. The Taiwanese consensus also notes that fish oil does not meaningfully lower PASI [1].
  • Chinese medicine and folk remedies: many Taiwanese patients try Chinese medicine, but trials are small and varied, so no reliable conclusion on efficacy is possible; topical indigo naturalis has small trials and needs larger studies [1]. Tell your doctor about any herbal products or supplements.
  • Protect skin and nails: avoid scratching, picking and injuring the skin (Koebner phenomenon); for nails, avoid manicures and nail biting, wear gloves for wet work or chemicals, keep nails short, dry them well and moisturize [15][25].
  • Sunlight: some people improve with summer sun, but avoid sunburn; therapeutic light should be dosed by a doctor [22][26][1].
  • Mood and relationships: the emotional burden is not always proportional to the extent of skin disease; low self-esteem, effects on relationships and sex life, and effects on work and employment are common [17][18]. Psychological distress may also blunt treatment response [17]. Clearing the skin helps, and counselling or psychiatric care can help too; tell your doctor directly about low mood, anxiety or thoughts of self-harm [18][1].

Related conditions: joints, heart, metabolism and mood

Psoriasis is regarded as a multisystem chronic inflammatory disease [15]. The Taiwanese consensus notes markedly higher risks of cardiovascular disease, diabetes, metabolic syndrome, chronic kidney disease, depression, anxiety and sleep disorders; all-cause mortality among Taiwanese patients is about 16% higher than in the general population but has been falling since 2006 [1]; in an insurance study the hazard ratio was 1.16 after adjusting only for age, sex and year, not for severity or other illnesses [13].

Psoriatic arthritis: the comorbidity to catch early

About 7% to 42% of people with psoriasis develop psoriatic arthritis (PsA), usually after the skin disease, and skin severity generally does not match arthritis severity [17]. The proportion is lower in Taiwanese patients than in Western countries [1], at about 12.7% to 13.4% (similar to Japan), possibly underdiagnosed [4]; in insurance data, the share of patients with psoriatic disease who had PsA rose from 6.3% in 2003 to 12.7% in 2013 [32]; in insurance data more than 85% of Taiwanese patients with PsA receive only anti-inflammatory painkillers or conventional tablets [3][4]. Arthritis can cause permanent joint damage, so early detection and treatment matter [20].

Watch for joint pain or swelling, especially stiffness on waking (lasting more than 30 minutes, per the Taiwanese position paper) [4]; back pain; a whole finger or toe swelling like a sausage (dactylitis); and inflammation where tendons attach to bone (enthesitis) [18][15]. About 80% to 90% of people with PsA have nail changes [25]. Tell your dermatologist about these symptoms; when needed, a rheumatologist will join the assessment and treatment [6][7]. Phototherapy and topicals do not treat arthritis [20].

Heart and metabolism

  • In meta-analyses, odds ratios in psoriasis are 1.66 for obesity, 1.42 for metabolic syndrome, 1.58 for hypertension and 1.59 for diabetes [17].
  • Heart attack risk rises most in severe disease and in younger patients: the adjusted relative risk is 3.10 at age 30 with severe psoriasis and 1.36 at age 60 [17].
  • Taiwanese insurance studies also found more cardiac arrhythmia (adjusted hazard ratio 1.34) and parkinsonism (1.74) in people with psoriasis [17].
  • Taiwanese insurance data: within 5 years of diagnosis the hazard ratio for acute heart attack was 2.10, and the adjusted hazard ratio for ischaemic stroke in new-onset psoriasis was 1.27 [3].
  • Taiwanese screening advice: a full cardiovascular risk check at diagnosis (blood pressure, glucose, lipids, ECG, uric acid, kidney function, weight, BMI, waist); then repeat at national adult health-check intervals (every 3 years at 40–64, yearly from 65); measure blood pressure at home using the 722 rule (7 days, twice daily, two readings each), with 130/80 mmHg as the home threshold for hypertension. Severe psoriasis, moderate or high cardiovascular risk, or symptoms such as chest pain, breathlessness, leg swelling or palpitations warrant cardiology referral [3].

Other associated conditions

  • Fatty liver: in an Italian study, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease affected 47% of patients versus 28% of controls [17].
  • Inflammatory bowel disease: 10% of people with Crohn disease had psoriasis versus 2% of controls [17]. Bowel symptoms (persistent diarrhoea, blood in stool) affect drug choice [20].
  • Mood: the odds ratio for depression is 1.49, and in one study of 217 patients about 5% reported active suicidal thoughts [17]. In a Taiwanese study of a 2-million-person insurance sample (2000–2015), 11.5% of people with psoriasis had a diagnosis of depression, versus 8.3% of controls of the same age, sex and income; among people with psoriasis, after adjustment for other factors, depression was more common in women, with lower income, and with liver cirrhosis, kidney disease, or heart or cerebrovascular disease. This shows an association only and cannot tell which came first [33].
  • Eyes and others: blepharitis, conjunctivitis and uveitis may be more common; the Taiwanese consensus also lists higher risks of non-melanoma skin cancer, lymphoma, colon cancer (especially in women) and sexual dysfunction in men [15][1].

Pregnancy, breastfeeding, children and older adults

Pregnancy and breastfeeding

People with psoriasis can have children. During pregnancy psoriasis improves in 40% to 60%, worsens in 10% to 20% and stays stable in the rest; after delivery it usually stays the same or worsens [24]. Ideally pregnancy is planned when the disease is stable on the lowest effective, safest treatment [24]. Severe psoriasis in pregnancy is linked to lower birth weight, so disease control still matters [1]. A Taiwanese study linking insurance data with the birth registry for 2001–2012 (4,058 women with psoriasis) also found higher odds of pre-eclampsia, severe post-partum haemorrhage, preterm birth, low birth weight and stillbirth than in women without psoriasis, with no increase in birth defects; still, more than 98% delivered live babies, and the authors see no need to advise women with psoriasis against pregnancy [34]. Such studies show association only and cannot prove that psoriasis itself is the cause, and the study did not analyse disease severity or medicines used in pregnancy [34].

  • Can be used: emollients; mild-to-moderate topical steroids first, potent ones only second-line and as briefly as possible (heavy use is linked to fetal growth restriction); when systemic treatment is needed, narrowband UVB is first-line; folic acid is advised in early pregnancy [1], and the face is shielded during light to limit melasma [24]. Vitamin D analogues have no pregnancy safety data and are used only when benefit outweighs risk; topical calcineurin inhibitors can be used with caution [1].
  • Biologics: if needed, the Taiwanese consensus recommends certolizumab, which barely crosses the placenta, as first-line [1]; NHI also allows certolizumab during pregnancy and breastfeeding without prior treatment failure, provided severity meets the application criteria [30].
  • Not allowed or must be stopped: methotrexate (stop at least 3 months before trying), acitretin (contraception for 3 years after stopping), tazarotene, apremilast, deucravacitinib and PUVA [1][24][6][5].
  • The baby's vaccines: if biologics were used after 16 weeks of pregnancy, live vaccines should generally be avoided for about the first 6–7 months of life (depending on the drug; ask your doctor) (a baby who received BCG developed disseminated infection), so tell the paediatrician [24][6].
  • Breastfeeding: do not apply topical steroids to the nipples; with heavy use of potent steroids the consensus advises pausing breastfeeding during treatment, so discuss it with your doctor; certolizumab can be used while breastfeeding [1].
  • Sudden widespread pustules and fever in pregnancy (pustular psoriasis of pregnancy, usually in the third trimester) is an emergency: seek care immediately [15][28].

Children and teenagers

Plaque psoriasis is also commonest in children, but the face, scalp and skin folds are involved more often than in adults; infants may show bright red patches in the nappy area (napkin psoriasis), and about 30% of children have had a guttate episode, often linked to streptococcal infection [21]. Obesity is the commonest comorbidity in childhood psoriasis (odds ratio 4.29), while arthritis affects fewer than 6% [21]. In Taiwanese insurance data (2000–2013), about 7.7% of people with psoriasis were diagnosed before age 18, at a mean age of about 12; diagnosed prevalence in children and teenagers rose from 0.03% to 0.07%, teenagers were affected more than children aged 12 or under, and boys and girls about equally [37].

  • Most children are controlled with topicals; steroids of suitable strength and amount do more good than harm, and topical calcineurin inhibitors are not for children under 2 [1][22].
  • Carefully selected children can have moderate phototherapy; some consensus members feel children find it hard to keep still and may go straight to a biologic [1]. Drugs licensed in Taiwan for childhood psoriasis include etanercept (severe disease, age 6+), ustekinumab, secukinumab and ixekizumab (age 6+), guselkumab and risankizumab (age 6+ and at least 40 kg) and apremilast (age 6+ and at least 20 kg); adalimumab is licensed in Taiwan for adult psoriasis only [31].

Older adults

Psoriasis starting after 65 is usually milder, with fewer nail and joint problems [1]. Doses need not be changed for age alone, but older people often have reduced liver or kidney function and several illnesses and medicines, so check interactions, start at the lowest effective dose, and monitor liver and kidney function, infection and viral reactivation [1]; methotrexate generally needs a lower dose and regular kidney checks in older people [5].

Common myths about psoriasis

  • "Psoriasis is a fungal infection." No. It is an immune-mediated inflammatory skin disease [1]; fungal infections (tinea, nail fungus) are look-alikes that may need to be excluded with microscopy or culture [15][25].
  • "It spreads to family members." It is not contagious [18]. Relatives are more likely to have it because of shared genes, not contact [15].
  • "Steroid creams make it worse the longer you use them." Chosen for the site and used as directed, steroids are effective, inexpensive first-line treatment; problems come from prolonged heavy use of potent steroids without review, or long use on the face and folds [1][19].
  • "It is only skin." Psoriasis is linked to arthritis, cardiovascular and metabolic disease, depression and anxiety, and needs whole-person care [1][17].
  • "You must avoid many foods and all sunlight." There is no evidence that particular foods must be avoided; weight loss and less alcohol are what the evidence supports [18][1]. Sunlight need not be avoided entirely, but sunburn should be [22][26].
  • "Biologics wreck your immunity." They block specific signals; in trials, serious infections on IL-23 inhibitors were as common as or less common than on placebo [20]. Screening for TB and hepatitis first, and seeking care early for signs of infection, keeps risk low [1].
  • "It goes away by itself." Some guttate psoriasis does clear, but plaque psoriasis is usually chronic [26][15]; undertreatment prolongs suffering, and delay with arthritis can cause permanent damage [18][20].

When to seek care promptly

  • Seek care immediately: widespread redness and peeling, or sudden sheets of pustules on red skin, with fever, chills or weakness; this may be erythrodermic or generalized pustular psoriasis [27][28].
  • Seek care immediately: a dry cough with breathlessness but no cold symptoms while on methotrexate (possible drug-induced pneumonitis) [5].
  • See your doctor soon: fever, a persistent cough or a red, oozing wound while on immune-modifying drugs or biologics [1][20].
  • See your doctor soon: swollen or painful joints, morning stiffness, a sausage digit or back pain [18].
  • Arrange a dermatology review: not good enough after 4–8 weeks of proper topical use, disease on the face, palms, soles, genitals or other life-affecting sites, or a clear effect on mood [19][1].

Frequently asked questions

Is psoriasis contagious?

No. It is an immune-mediated disease, not an infection, and touching the skin will not spread it [18][1].

Will it go away? Is it lifelong?

Plaque psoriasis is usually chronic and fluctuating; there is no cure, but most people achieve good control with current treatment [1][18]. Guttate psoriasis is likelier to settle: in a 10-year follow-up study, 48% of people whose psoriasis began as guttate had minimal disease without treatment, versus 20% of those with plaque onset [26].

Do biologics have to be taken for life?

Not necessarily, but most people need long-term treatment to stay well. The Taiwanese consensus generally advises against stopping: in trials, about 20% to 30% of responders who stopped and restarted the same biologic did not regain their response [1]; when disease is stable, the dose may be reduced or the interval lengthened, with some risk of losing effect [1]. NHI pauses treatment after 2 years if PASI is 10 or less, and you can re-apply after a relapse [30]. Discuss it with your doctor rather than stopping on your own.

How does Taiwan's NHI cover psoriasis drugs?

Topical vitamin D analogues and their steroid combinations are normally limited to 30 g a week [30]. Biologics need prior approval: broadly, lesions for at least 6 months with PASI 10 or more (or stubborn palmoplantar or chronic erythrodermic disease), and failure or unsuitability of adequate phototherapy plus at least two of methotrexate, acitretin, ciclosporin, apremilast and deucravacitinib, each for at least 3 months; renewal is every 6 months and requires PASI 50 versus baseline (PASI 75 for risankizumab) [30]. Apremilast and deucravacitinib require failure of phototherapy plus methotrexate or ciclosporin [30]. See our summary of NHI rules for dermatology drugs.

What does an NHI application need, and what if I do not qualify?

Biologics need prior review submitted by your doctor, with at least 6 months of records, detailed phototherapy dose records, doses, dates and reasons for stopping at least two systemic drugs, a PASI calculation, at least 4 photos for palmoplantar disease, and latent TB screening and treatment records; renewal is every 6 months, and a gap of more than 3 months counts as a new application [30]. If you do not yet qualify, the usual options are completing adequate phototherapy and conventional drugs, the apremilast or deucravacitinib criteria, or the psoriatic arthritis route when its criteria are met (prior approved apremilast counts as a first-line DMARD there) [30].

Can I get pregnant if I have psoriasis?

Yes. Plan pregnancy when the disease is stable and talk to your doctor early about pregnancy-compatible treatment: topical steroids, narrowband UVB and, if needed, certolizumab; methotrexate must be stopped at least 3 months beforehand, and pregnancy must be avoided for 3 years after acitretin [1][24].

What should I eat? Do I need to avoid foods?

There is no evidence that particular foods must be avoided. Weight loss if overweight has the best evidence; drink less and stop smoking; otherwise eat a balanced diet. A gluten-free diet is only for confirmed coeliac disease or positive gluten antibodies, and supplements such as fish oil lack evidence [1][18].

Does phototherapy cause skin cancer?

Most studies show no clear rise in skin cancer with narrowband or broadband UVB, but men with long-term broadband UVB (over 300 sessions) without genital shielding may have more genital tumours, so shielding is needed [20][1]; PUVA does, so it is used cautiously, cumulative doses and sessions are recorded, and skin checks continue long term [20][1][6].

Do biologics increase cancer risk?

The evidence is broadly reassuring but still accumulating: a meta-analysis of 20 placebo-controlled trials in more than 6,800 people with psoriasis found no increase in any cancer with TNF inhibitors, and 5-year and registry data for ustekinumab show no rise in serious infection or cancer [17][20]. Non-melanoma skin cancer has, however, been reported to increase with biologics in rheumatoid arthritis, so the Taiwanese consensus advises regular skin cancer checks on biologics; long-term ciclosporin and PUVA are clearly linked to higher skin cancer risk [17][1]. In a Taiwanese insurance nested case-control study (people newly diagnosed with psoriasis from 2008; 4,188 who later developed cancer versus 8,376 who did not), methotrexate, retinoids, etanercept, adalimumab and ustekinumab showed no statistically significant link with cancer for any duration of use, but few people used biologics and IL-17 and IL-23 inhibitors were not analysed; ciclosporin used for more than 12 months in total within the 5 years before diagnosis was linked to a higher risk of cancer of all types combined [36]. This is an observational study and shows association only [36]. If you have had cancer, choose treatment together with your doctor [6]; NHI excludes biologics for people with malignancy or pre-malignancy [30].

Can I have vaccines while on biologics?

Yes, and non-live vaccines such as influenza, pneumococcal and recombinant shingles vaccines are recommended; biologics can continue during and after COVID-19 vaccination [1]. People on methotrexate may pause it for 2 weeks after a non-live vaccine to improve the response; discuss the timing of live vaccines with your doctor [20][6].

I have hepatitis B or C. Can I have biologics?

Yes, but hepatitis B and C must be tested before starting, with regular liver tests and viral loads during treatment [1][14]. Taiwanese data show that hepatitis B carriers have a higher chance of viral reactivation, lowest with IL-23 inhibitors and highest with TNF inhibitors; carriers often see a liver specialist about preventive antiviral tablets [14]. People with active hepatitis should have it treated before starting [4].

Epidemiology and pathogenesis: additional points

  • Taiwan: incidence is higher in older people and those born preterm; psoriasis and vitiligo are strongly associated in both directions, and psoriasis overlaps substantially with atopic dermatitis genetically, immunologically and clinically [1].
  • Taiwanese epidemiology, additional: the insurance study above used a one-code definition; patients rose from 40,420 (2006) to 201,657 (2017), PsA prevalence was 77 per 100,000 in 2017, and the mortality HR of 1.16 had a 95% CI of 1.13–1.19 [13]. Another insurance study (2003–2013; at least 2 visits coded ICD-9 696.0/696.1) found that the prevalence of psoriasis without arthritis rose from 15.54 to 21.90 per 10,000, and the share of patients with psoriatic disease who had PsA rose from 6.3% to 12.7%; in 2013 only 1.2% of psoriasis-only and 15% of PsA patients received biologics; the authors note that people who do not seek care are missed, so prevalence may be underestimated [32].
  • Taiwan: psoriasis and depression (Hu 2019): cross-sectional study of a 2-million-person insurance sample (drawn in 2010, data 2000–2015), 17,086 with psoriasis and 1,607,242 without (each diagnosis requiring ≥2 claims); period prevalence of depression 11.52% vs 7.73% (crude prevalence ratio 1.49, 1.43–1.55); after 1:1 matching for age, sex and income, crude OR 1.44 (1.34–1.54) and OR adjusted for comorbidities 1.33 (1.24–1.43); within psoriasis, depression was associated with age 20–50 (versus <20), female sex (aOR 1.80, 1.63–1.99), low income, liver cirrhosis, kidney disease, cardiovascular disease (aOR 1.52, 1.33–1.73) and cerebrovascular disease (aOR 2.16, 1.88–2.47) [33]. Limitations: the cross-sectional design cannot establish timing, and severity and lifestyle data were lacking [33].
  • Inflammatory macrophages are the main source of IL-23 in plaques, and long-lived IL-17A-producing CD8+ resident memory T cells in the epidermis are linked to recurrence at previously healed sites [16]. GPP centres on the IL-36 pathway; some patients carry IL36RN mutations (biallelic in about 8% to 39% across studies), more often those without prior psoriasis [28].
  • Other drug triggers include paradoxical psoriasiform eruptions with TNF inhibitors, immune checkpoint inhibitors, interferon-alpha and imiquimod [15][16].

Registry data cited suggest biologics overall do not raise cardiovascular risk, and in Taiwanese data methotrexate was linked to lower cerebrovascular risk in patients without arthritis (HR 0.39) [3].

The biggest S3–French difference is first-line systemic therapy: S3 (weak recommendation) suggests a first-line-label biologic or deucravacitinib for first systemic treatment, especially in particularly severe disease; the French guideline puts methotrexate, adalimumab or ustekinumab first for patients without symptomatic comorbidities, reserving IL-17 or IL-23 inhibitors or infliximab for BSA >50% and high burden, partly for biosimilar cost and local reimbursement reasons, and places deucravacitinib after biologic failure [5][6]. The Taiwanese consensus and NHI still put phototherapy and conventional drugs first, with first-line-label agents possible upfront in severe disease (NHI still requires prior treatment) [1][30].

Switching, dose reduction and interval extension

  • Taiwanese consensus: failure to reach PASI 50 at 12–16 weeks is primary failure; switching for inefficacy can occur at the next scheduled dose without washout, whereas switching for safety may need a gap; failure of one biologic does not predict failure of another, but prior failure predicts weaker response and shorter drug survival; dose reduction may be considered in stable disease at the risk of lower efficacy and anti-drug antibodies; stopping is generally not advised [1].
  • UpToDate authors' approach: primary failure on IL-23 → IL-17 and vice versa; secondary failure on IL-17 or IL-23 → often another drug in the same class; failure on TNF inhibitors or ustekinumab → IL-23 or IL-17 [20].
  • Tapering: the French guideline recommends considering dose reduction or interval extension after at least 1 year of control (complete remission or PASI <2), with patients told about relapse risk [6]. In a guselkumab trial, patients with PASI 0 at weeks 20 and 28 who moved to 16-weekly dosing had PASI 0 at week 68 in 69.1% (81.1% with 8-weekly), while PASI <3 was similar (91.9% vs 92.6%) [20].
  • NHI: switching to a different mechanism because PASI is >10 restarts the 2-year clock; with PASI <10 and PASI 50 achieved but PASI >1, BSA >3% or visible sites (scalp, face, nails, hands) affecting life, a lateral switch is allowed at renewal, with time counted together [30].
  • Biologic persistence in Taiwan: among 1,397 new users in 2015–2017 insurance data (men:women about 4:1), 1-year/2-year persistence was 94.2%/84.9% for ustekinumab, 96.2% for secukinumab (too few at 2 years), 66.0%/29.9% for etanercept and 59.8%/40.3% for adalimumab; the adjusted HR for discontinuation with ustekinumab versus adalimumab was 0.289 (0.247–0.339) [12]. Interpret with care: a gap was defined as about twice each drug's dosing interval, so definitions differ, and persistence of etanercept and adalimumab rose markedly with longer gaps; there were no response data or reasons for stopping; the NHI rule requiring PASI 50 every 6 months also shapes persistence; the study was funded by Janssen [12].

Special populations: additional points

  • Pregnancy and breastfeeding: Taiwanese Table 9: acitretin and methotrexate contraindicated in pregnancy and lactation; ciclosporin conditional; certolizumab first-line if needed; contraception after stopping: bimekizumab 17 weeks, brodalumab 12, ixekizumab 10, risankizumab 21 [1]; the French washouts before conception are ixekizumab 10 weeks, brodalumab 8, bimekizumab 17, guselkumab 12, risankizumab 21 and tildrakizumab 17, with a suggested switch to certolizumab, and ciclosporin, etanercept, adalimumab, infliximab, ustekinumab or secukinumab also possible [6]. French: stop biologics in the third trimester where possible (except certolizumab and etanercept); after maternal biologics beyond 16 weeks of gestation, avoid live vaccines in infants up to 6 months [6]. UpToDate: stop infliximab at about 30 weeks and delay infant live vaccines to 7 months, with similar caution for adalimumab and etanercept [24].
  • Pregnancy outcomes in Taiwan (Huang 2021): insurance data linked to the birth registry, 2,350,339 singleton births in 2001–2012 (2,350,330 in the abstract), 4,058 of them to mothers with psoriasis (ICD-9 696.1, at least two diagnoses by dermatologists or rheumatologists); GEE adjusted for age, nationality, obstetric history, residence, income, occupation, birth year and Charlson index. Maternal: pre-eclampsia aOR 1.57 (1.31–1.89), pregnancy-related hypertension 1.5 (1.28–1.75), severe post-partum haemorrhage 1.57 (1.36–1.82), caesarean delivery 1.06 (1.01–1.12); offspring: stillbirth 1.48 (1.11–1.96), preterm birth (<37 weeks) 1.13 (1.02–1.25), low birth weight 1.27 (1.14–1.41), SGA 1.12 (1.02–1.23), 5-minute Apgar <7 1.53 (1.07–2.19); any birth defect 0.87 (0.75–1.00), not increased [34]. Limitations: no data on BMI, smoking or alcohol, and miscarriage could not be assessed; severity and medicines in pregnancy were not analysed; observational association, not causation [34].
  • Methotrexate and conception, a divergence: the Taiwanese consensus and UpToDate advise both sexes to stop methotrexate at least 3 months before conception [1][24]; the French guideline sets a 24-hour washout for women and suggests men may continue, while S3 part 1 gives no washout period [6][5]. The patient section follows the Taiwanese consensus.
  • Paediatric doses (Taiwanese Table 10): methotrexate 0.2–0.7 mg/kg/week and ciclosporin 2.5–5.0 mg/kg/day (tapered once stable, not long term) are conditional, and ciclosporin is not approved for childhood psoriasis; Table 10 marks acitretin "contraindicated in children ≤12 years and women of childbearing potential", and its paediatric safety and efficacy are not established (listed doses 0.3–0.5 to start, up to 1 mg/kg/day maintenance); ustekinumab at weeks 0 and 4 then every 12 weeks (<60 kg 0.75 mg/kg, 60–100 kg 45 mg, >100 kg 90 mg); secukinumab weekly for 5 weeks then monthly (<50 kg 75 mg, ≥50 kg 150 mg, up to 300 mg); ixekizumab 25–50 kg 80 mg then 40 mg every 4 weeks, >50 kg 160 mg then 80 mg every 4 weeks, <25 kg 40 mg then 20 mg every 4 weeks [1]. UpToDate: mainly topicals under age 4; biologics preferred when systemic therapy is needed [22][23].
  • Paediatric epidemiology in Taiwan (Lin 2024): insurance data 2000–2013, ≥3 outpatient visits or 1 admission with a dermatologist or rheumatologist (ICD-9 696.1/696.0); 17,535 patients under 18 (PsO 16,129, PsA 2,022, of whom 1,406 had PsA codes only), 7.7% of all 226,088 patients with psoriasis, mean age at onset 12.0 ± 4.6 years; prevalence in children and teenagers rose from 0.03% to 0.07% for PsO and from 0.003% to 0.014% for PsA; incidence fell slightly for PsO (19.81 to 17.55 per 100,000 person-years) and rose for PsA (1.02 to 5.06); higher in teenagers than in children aged 12 or under, with no sex difference; among those with both PsO and PsA, PsA came first in 27.07% of children and 13.46% of teenagers [37]. The authors note that data end in 2013 and that coding may misclassify [37]. A 2004 dermatologist survey of 4,067 schoolchildren aged 6–11 in Kaohsiung County found no psoriasis (purposive sampling, summer, 52% response), which shows only that psoriasis is uncommon at school age and is not a prevalence estimate [39].

Acute GPP: the Taiwanese GPP consensus

  • Definition and diagnosis: ERASPEN 2017 is adopted: primary, sterile, macroscopically visible pustules on non-acral skin, relapsing (>1 episode) or persistent (>3 months); a major flare is BSA ≥10% with systemic inflammation (e.g. fever >38 °C, white cells >12×10⁹/L) [2]. Diagnosis is clinical; histology and genetic tests help but are not required; exclude AGEP (EuroSCAR 8–12 definite, usually resolving within 15 days), IgA pemphigus and subcorneal pustular dermatosis [2].
  • Adult treatment: first-line in both acute and maintenance phases is an oral retinoid or spesolimab (IV for flares, SC for prevention); next-line options are ciclosporin, methotrexate and oral steroids (not routinely or long term), and among biologics brodalumab, guselkumab, TNF inhibitors, secukinumab, ixekizumab, risankizumab and ustekinumab [2]. In Effisayil 1, pustules cleared completely at week 1 in 54% vs 6% with placebo [2]. In pregnancy certolizumab is suggested for acute flares (retinoids contraindicated); retinoids are first-line in infants and adolescents [2].
  • Support and targets: most need admission; assess infection, electrolytes, hypocalcaemia, albumin and liver and kidney function; stop suspect drugs; use topical steroids and vitamin D cautiously on extensive pustules or erosions (watch calcium) [29][2]. Reasonable goals: no new pustules within 2–3 days, fever gone within 3 days, pustules cleared within 7 days [29].
  • GPP flares in Taiwan (Lu 2024): retrospective study in the Chang Gung hospital network, 2008–2021, of adult moderate-to-severe GPP flares (GPPGA total ≥3 and pustule score ≥3; GPP in pregnancy and prior psoriasis excluded): 34 patients, 81 flares; mean age 47.1, 41.2% male; 64.2% of flares were recurrent, 32.1% had fever at diagnosis, and recurrence averaged 0.6 flares per person-year; 10 of the 14 patients tested had an IL36RN mutation [38]. Systemic treatment was used in 96.3% of flares (acitretin 85.2%, ciclosporin 45.7%, methotrexate 18.5%), each biologic in <10% of flares (etanercept 9.9%, adalimumab 7.4%); at week 12 only 6.2% of flares were classed as “Response”, 39.5% as partial response and 33.3% as no response (about 21% lost to follow-up); within 12 weeks arthritis accompanied 25.9% of flares and skin infection 8.6%, with no deaths; during follow-up 8 patients (23.5%) developed psoriasis vulgaris [38]. Single hospital network, no control group, small sample and several internal inconsistencies in the paper, so these are descriptive figures only [38].
  • NHI: spesolimab (rule 8.2.4.6.2) is restricted to dermatologists or rheumatologists and requires an IL36RN mutation, GPPGA total ≥3 with pustule score ≥2 and pustular erythema >10% BSA, with prior approval; usually one 900 mg dose, plus a second if still active after 1 week (GPPGA total ≥2 and pustule score ≥2) [30]. Other biologics licensed for GPP (rule 8.2.4.6.1) are limited to women aged 18 or over who could become pregnant with pustular erythema >10% BSA and systemic symptoms such as fever and leucocytosis, for 4 weeks per application (up to 8), at least 12 weeks apart [30]. In Taiwan, brodalumab and guselkumab are licensed for adult pustular psoriasis [31].

Erythrodermic psoriasis

  • Defined as erythema of ≥75% BSA (definitions range from 75% to >90%); check vital signs, blood count, chemistry and infection, and stop triggering drugs; systemic steroids already in use should be tapered very slowly as psoriasis treatment starts; most need admission for fluids, electrolytes, temperature control, nutrition and wet dressings [27]. Reported mortality ranges from 9% to 64%, mostly from bacterial infection [27].
  • No consensus on drug choice: UpToDate favours ciclosporin (5 mg/kg/day in severe disease, <1 year; complete remission within a year in 67% of 33 patients) or infliximab first-line [27]; the French guideline suggests IL-17 or IL-23 inhibitors (expert opinion) with infliximab as an alternative, and referral to a specialist centre [6]; a 2025 systematic review (mostly case reports) found non-biologic systemic drugs reasonable first-line, favoured IL-17 inhibitors and risankizumab among biologics, and found phototherapy poorly effective [9]. NHI: ciclosporin can be first-line systemic therapy [1]; chronic erythroderma (≥75% BSA, >1-year history, ciclosporin 5 mg/kg/day for ≥6 months with relapse to PASI >10 or BSA >30% within 3 months of stopping) qualifies for biologics without phototherapy after methotrexate and acitretin fail, limited to 6 months [30].

Perioperative management

  • National Psoriasis Foundation 2024 (grade C evidence, mostly from RA and IBD): continue TNF, IL-17, IL-23 inhibitors, ustekinumab, methotrexate, ciclosporin and apremilast for low-risk surgery; for intermediate- or high-risk surgery methotrexate, ciclosporin, apremilast, IL-17, IL-23 inhibitors and ustekinumab are likely safe to continue, case by case; hold TNF inhibitors for one dosing interval before hip or knee arthroplasty; acitretin need not be stopped for any surgery; evidence is insufficient for JAK inhibitors and deucravacitinib [8]. The French guideline is broadly aligned [6].
  • The Taiwanese consensus is more conservative: continue for low-risk surgery; decide case by case for intermediate or high risk, stopping for 3–5 half-lives, or about 4–5 half-lives before elective high-risk surgery [1]. Decide jointly with the surgeon and anaesthetist [6].

Psoriatic arthritis: screening, referral and EULAR 2023

  • None of the guidelines supplied names a screening tool; asking at each visit about joint pain, morning stiffness, back pain and dactylitis suffices, and UpToDate mentions the PEST questionnaire as a resource [15][20][7]. Refer suspected cases to rheumatology for diagnosis, with joint care when skin disease is significant [6][7].
  • EULAR 2023 highlights: target remission or low disease activity, with at least 50% improvement in 3 months and the target by 6 months; start a conventional synthetic disease-modifying antirheumatic drug (csDMARD) promptly for polyarthritis or oligoarthritis with poor prognostic factors (structural damage, raised acute-phase reactants, dactylitis, nail involvement), preferring methotrexate when skin involvement is relevant; use a biologic DMARD (bDMARD) after inadequate response to at least one csDMARD; JAK inhibitors come after bDMARDs, with caution at age 65+, in smokers and with cardiovascular, cancer or thrombosis risk; choose a monoclonal anti-TNF for uveitis; taper rather than stop in sustained remission [7].
  • Taiwanese TRA/TAPSI position paper (2021; 28 clinicians, modified Delphi, 70% agreement threshold): multidisciplinary care and early diagnosis (nail changes, sacroiliitis, dactylitis, enthesitis, morning stiffness >30 minutes, swollen finger or toe joints); target clinical remission or minimal disease activity; start biologics with a TNF or IL-17 inhibitor, considering IL-12/23 for contraindication, intolerance or inadequate response; usually biologic monotherapy, though methotrexate with adalimumab, infliximab or golimumab may reduce anti-drug antibodies; TNF monoclonal antibodies for uveitis, TNF monoclonal antibodies or IL-12/23 for IBD; screen for TB and hepatitis B and C before csDMARDs or biologics, avoiding TNF inhibitors and favouring IL-17 inhibitors in TB-positive patients; avoid intramuscular steroids (psoriasis flares) and use hydroxychloroquine rarely; strongly advise stopping smoking and weight loss [4]. Its list of Taiwan-approved PsA drugs (as of 2020) does not include bimekizumab or IL-23 inhibitors, whereas EULAR 2023 lists IL-23 inhibitors when skin involvement is relevant [4][7]. In Taiwan, PsA cases rose from 2,351 to 7,470 in 2003–2013; 55.78% were managed by dermatologists [4]; in 2013 only 8.01% of PsA patients under dermatologists received biologics, versus 26.70% under rheumatologists [32][4].
  • NHI (rule 8.2.4.4, peripheral arthritis): prescribed by rheumatologists or dermatologists; ≥3 tender and ≥3 swollen joints on two assessments ≥4 weeks apart; NSAIDs plus at least two DMARDs (sulfasalazine, methotrexate or ciclosporin first-line, then leflunomide for 3 months) for ≥6 months; ustekinumab, guselkumab and risankizumab only after TNF, IL-17 or JAK inhibitors and others; response by PsARC at 12 weeks (24 weeks initially for those three) [30]. Exam notes: Fitzpatrick Ch.65 Psoriatic arthritis.

When to refer

  • Moderate-to-severe or special-site disease, poor control after 4–8 weeks of topicals, diagnostic doubt needing biopsy, or when phototherapy or systemic treatment is considered: refer to dermatology [1][19][15].
