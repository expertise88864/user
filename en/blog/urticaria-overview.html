Key points in 30 seconds

Key points
  • Urticaria is caused by skin mast cells releasing histamine and other mediators: itchy, shifting wheals that usually fade within 24 hours without a trace, sometimes with deeper swelling called angioedema that can last up to 72 hours. It is not contagious [1][14][17].
  • Up to 6 weeks is acute and usually settles within days to weeks; beyond 6 weeks is chronic, mostly without an outside cause (spontaneous), more than half autoimmune-driven, and rarely due to food allergy [1][14].
  • Chronic urticaria does not need broad allergy panels; basic tests are a blood count and an inflammation marker, with anything further guided by the history [1].
  • The first step is a daily non-sedating second-generation antihistamine, increased up to 4-fold under medical supervision if needed. If control is still poor, omalizumab, dupilumab or remibrutinib can be added, then ciclosporin. The first three are licensed in Taiwan for chronic spontaneous urticaria, but NHI has no urticaria reimbursement rule for any of them [1][41][42].
  • Seek urgent care if hives or swelling come with throat tightness, hoarseness, breathing difficulty or wheeze, dizziness or near-fainting, vomiting or abdominal pain; recurrent swelling without itch that does not respond to antihistamines also needs medical assessment [2][15][24].

What is urticaria, and how common is it?

Urticaria (hives) is defined by wheals, angioedema, or both [1]. The key cell is the skin mast cell: once activated it releases histamine and other mediators that stimulate nerves (itch), widen blood vessels (redness) and let fluid leak out (swelling) [1][14].

  • Wheals: superficial, sharply outlined swellings, usually with a red flare, intensely itchy (sometimes burning). Each one typically fades within 30 minutes to 24 hours, leaving normal skin; they vary in shape, may merge and blanch on pressure [1][14]. Redness can be hard to see on darker skin [4].
  • Angioedema: swelling of the deep dermis, subcutis or mucosa, typically the lips, eyelids, cheeks, hands, feet or genitals; it feels tight, painful or burning rather than itchy and resolves more slowly, up to 72 hours [1][17].

An important clue: if individual lesions stay in place longer than 24 hours, hurt or burn more than they itch, leave bruising or dark marks, or come with fever or joint pain, this is not typical urticaria. Urticarial vasculitis and other conditions should be considered, usually with a skin biopsy [5][14]. If unsure how long a lesion lasts, circle it with a pen and note when it disappears [14].

How common is it?

Urticaria is very common: about 20% of people have at least one episode in their lifetime [1][14]. Chronic spontaneous urticaria affects about 1% of the world population, most often women aged 30 to 50, with about 70% of patients female [4].

Taiwanese data: a National Health Insurance database study (cases defined by at least two outpatient urticaria diagnoses in a year at least 6 weeks apart, plus an antihistamine prescription) found an annual prevalence of chronic urticaria of 0.69% to 0.79% in 2009–2012 and about 0.50% new cases each year. Of 177,879 patients in 2012, 56% were women and the commonest age band was 40–49 years (18.1%) [28]. The female share is lower than in overseas specialist series; the authors suggest men may skip daytime hospital clinics but use evening or weekend clinics [28].

How long does it last?

Acute urticaria usually settles on its own: about two-thirds resolve within 6 weeks, and among them about half improve within a week, 75% within two weeks and 90% within three weeks [14]. It is not possible to predict at onset who will develop chronic urticaria [14]; reported rates of progression vary widely, from 8% or less, to about 1 in 10, to one-third [4][11][14].

Chronic spontaneous urticaria also goes away, but slowly. International literature gives an average duration of 2 to 5 years, with 30% to 50% remitting within a year [16]; a JAMA review cites cumulative remission of 17% at 1 year, 45% at 5 years and 73% at 20 years [4]. The figures come from different populations: most people improve within a few years, some have a long course, and relapse after remission can happen [1][4]. Current drugs control symptoms well but have not been shown to shorten the disease [17]. Chronic spontaneous urticaria rarely signals another disease; it is not an allergic reaction, rarely poses an immediate danger, and symptoms can be controlled in most people [17].

Acute, chronic, spontaneous, inducible: how is urticaria classified?

The 2026 international guideline classifies urticaria on two axes [1]: duration (up to 6 weeks is acute, beyond 6 weeks chronic) and a definite trigger or not (spontaneous urticaria has no consistent physical trigger; inducible urticaria always appears with a specific stimulus such as rubbing, cold, heat, sweating or sunlight, and never without it). The 2021 Taiwanese Dermatological Association consensus adopted the same 6-week cut-off [2].

  • Chronic spontaneous urticaria (CSU): the commonest chronic type. Overseas data show about 57% have wheals only, 37% wheals plus angioedema and 6% angioedema only [4]. Stress or infection can worsen it, but that does not make it inducible urticaria [1].
  • Chronic inducible urticaria (CIndU): symptomatic dermographism, cold, delayed pressure, solar, heat, vibratory angioedema, cholinergic, contact and aquagenic urticaria. Symptoms usually start within 10 minutes of the trigger and fade within 1 to 3 hours after it stops [1]. See "Inducible urticaria" below.
  • The two often coexist: reported rates of inducible urticaria among people with chronic spontaneous urticaria range from over 10% to about one-third [1][4][6].

What sets it off? Triggers, aggravating factors and food

Acute urticaria: infection first, then drugs and food

Acute urticaria more often has an identifiable cause, yet none is found in 30% to 50% [14]. Common ones include infections (colds and other viral, bacterial or parasitic infections; hives may appear days or even weeks later), drugs (antibiotics such as penicillins, NSAID painkillers, opioids, contrast media), foods (such as eggs, nuts, fish or shellfish; true IgE-mediated food reactions usually start within 30 minutes of eating and fade within hours to a day), stinging insects and latex [14][15].

Taiwanese children: among 953 children seen at the Changhua Christian Hospital paediatric emergency department with a first episode of acute urticaria, the physician-judged causes were infection 48.4%, food 23.5%, unknown 13.2% and drugs 11.5%; infection dominated in younger children and food in adolescents [36]. These are single-centre, history-based data and not national figures [36].

Chronic urticaria: mostly an internal process

In more than half of people with chronic spontaneous urticaria, the body makes autoantibodies that activate mast cells: IgE against its own proteins (autoallergy, type I) or IgG against IgE or its receptor (type IIb autoimmunity), and some have both [1]. So it is not weak immunity; it is closer to the immune system over-reacting to the self.

Food is rarely the cause of chronic urticaria: the international guideline states that IgE-mediated food allergy is extremely rarely the underlying cause of chronic spontaneous urticaria; a positive food IgE blood test may reflect a coexisting allergy rather than the cause [1]. A true food allergy would clear within 24 hours of avoidance [1]. Some people notice certain foods worsen symptoms, but once the urticaria remits they can usually eat the same foods again [16].

Things that make chronic urticaria worse

  • NSAID painkillers (aspirin, ibuprofen, diclofenac): the commonest aggravating drugs; the guideline says up to one-quarter of patients worsen with them, usually within 1 to 4 hours [1][17]. Acetaminophen (paracetamol) or COX-2 inhibitors are generally safer [1], and once urticaria is controlled on antihistamines most patients tolerate NSAIDs [17].
  • Stress and lack of sleep: up to one-third of patients feel stress makes their urticaria worse [1][17].
  • Infections (colds, sinusitis), heat (hot showers, humidity), pressure or friction from tight clothing or straps, alcohol, the menstrual cycle in some women, and taking antihistamines irregularly [17].
  • Chronic infection or inflammation: such as Helicobacter pylori infection, gastritis, reflux oesophagitis or bowel parasites. Their link with urticaria is often unclear in the individual, but they should be treated if present, partly because some are associated with cancer [1]. Raw fish contaminated with the parasite Anisakis simplex can also cause urticaria [2][14].

Taiwanese data: of 62 patients at the National Taiwan University Hospital urticaria clinic, the 59 who reported aggravating factors most often named weather (79.7%, especially hot weather 50.8%), followed by contact items, foods, seasonal change, bathing and stress [34]. The 2021 Taiwanese consensus also advises minimising stress, overheating, alcohol and coffee, and avoiding aspirin, NSAIDs, codeine, morphine, ACE inhibitors, Chinese herbs and supplements, and fragranced or spicy foods [2]; whether a prescribed medicine should be changed is your doctor's decision, so do not stop it yourself.

Do not stop prescribed low-dose aspirin on your own: low-dose aspirin for heart or vascular protection does not always need to be stopped; if needed, your doctor can arrange a 4-week switch to another antithrombotic to see whether it matters [1].

Do I need a special diet?

The international guideline treats a "pseudoallergen-low" or "histamine-low" diet as a diagnostic trial for selected patients only: limited to 2 to 3 weeks and never at the cost of delaying effective treatment, and unproven in rigorous double-blind studies [1]. Over-diagnosing "histamine intolerance" just leads to unnecessary restrictions and supplements [1]. If one particular food consistently makes you worse, avoid that food; long, broad elimination diets are not needed [1][17].

Angioedema: what if there is swelling but no hives?

Most angioedema, like wheals, is driven by mast cells and histamine and responds to antihistamines [1]. But recurrent swelling that never comes with wheals raises the possibility of bradykinin-mediated angioedema: it does not itch, lasts longer (often more than 48 hours) and does not respond to antihistamines, steroids or even adrenaline [1][7].

  • ACE inhibitor blood-pressure drugs: a common drug cause, linked to about 30% of angioedema seen in overseas emergency departments [25]; swelling may start within a week of starting or after years of use, most often of the lips, tongue or face [24]. Deaths from massive tongue swelling have been reported [24]. The key treatment is stopping the ACE inhibitor: if you suspect it, contact your doctor promptly so it can be stopped and your blood-pressure treatment adjusted, and go to an emergency department at once if your tongue or throat swells or breathing is difficult [24]. Remission usually follows within days of stopping, rarely up to 6 months [1]. ARBs and DPP-4 inhibitor diabetes drugs (gliptins) are also reported, much less often [1][25].
  • Hereditary angioedema (HAE): a rare inherited disorder, often with a family history, causing recurrent swelling of the limbs or face, abdominal attacks or laryngeal swelling [7][13][40]. Blood tests for C4 and C1 inhibitor (C1-INH) level and function are used to screen [1][13].

Taiwanese data: Mackay Memorial Hospital described 3 unrelated families and added 1 previously published Taiwanese family, 19 patients with type I HAE in all. Eight (42.1%) were asymptomatic carriers; of the 11 symptomatic patients, 5 (45.5%) had facial or laryngeal swelling, 2 needed emergency tracheostomy, and 1 later died of a further laryngeal attack; diagnosis was delayed by a mean of 8.45 years after symptom onset [40]. It is a small family series, but the message holds: recurrent unexplained swelling, especially with recurrent abdominal pain or a family history, should be investigated [40].

Which tests are needed, and which are not?

Urticaria is diagnosed mainly from the history and skin examination. Lesions have often gone by the time of the visit, so photos taken during a flare help a lot [1][14]. Your doctor will ask how long each lesion lasts, whether there is angioedema, what medicines were taken beforehand (including painkillers, over-the-counter products and supplements), recent infections, links with cold, heat, rubbing or exercise, and systemic symptoms such as fever or joint pain [1][14].

  • Acute urticaria: the international guideline strongly recommends against routine tests; targeted testing is reserved for a history strongly suggesting food or drug (especially NSAID) allergy [1].
  • Chronic spontaneous urticaria: basic tests are a differential blood count plus CRP and/or erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR); in specialist care, total IgE and IgG anti-thyroid peroxidase (anti-TPO) may be added to help predict response to omalizumab; further tests depend on the history [1]. The 2021 Taiwanese consensus also lists D-dimer as a routine test [2].
  • Inducible urticaria: provocation tests confirm the type and find the threshold, such as stroking the skin with a blunt object or placing an ice cube in a thin plastic bag on the forearm [1][4].
  • Not recommended: costly broad screening, such as IgE panels for a hundred foods or inhalants, or routine screening for infections [1][4][14]. Large allergen panels are expensive and unlikely to give useful information [14].

Practice in Taiwan: in a 2013 national survey, 48% of people with chronic spontaneous urticaria who had seen a doctor had had allergy-screening blood tests, which the authors attributed to a widespread belief that urticaria is always allergen-driven [33]. A National Taiwan University Hospital study found that 61.3% of patients with chronic urticaria had at least one positive IgE to common allergens (mostly house dust mite), yet dust mite-positive and -negative patients did not differ in disease duration, symptoms or treatment response, and food IgE results were mostly negative [34].

Tracking your urticaria: UAS7, UCT and AAS

Chronic urticaria fluctuates and is often quiet on the day of the visit, so the international guideline recommends assessing activity, impact and control with questionnaires at every visit [1]. Three tools are used most:

  • UAS7 (urticaria activity score over 7 days): each day, score wheals 0 to 3 (0 none; 1 fewer than 20 in 24 hours; 2 20 to 50; 3 more than 50 or large confluent areas) plus itch 0 to 3 (0 none; 1 present but not bothersome; 2 troublesome but not interfering with daily activity or sleep; 3 severe enough to interfere with daily activity or sleep), then add up 7 days, for a maximum of 42 [1].
  • UCT (urticaria control test): 4 questions about the past 4 weeks, scored out of 16; 16 means complete control, 12 or more well controlled, below 12 poorly controlled [1][5]. It works for all forms of chronic urticaria, including inducible types [1].
  • AAS and AECT: track swelling activity and control in people with angioedema; an AECT of 10 or more indicates good control [1].
  • Quality of life: the guideline recommends the CU-Q2oL (wheals) and AE-QoL (angioedema) [1]; the 10-item DLQI used across dermatology exceeds 10 in about 40% of people with chronic spontaneous urticaria overseas, indicating a marked impact [4]. You can work out your DLQI with the DLQI calculator on the tools page.

Keeping a daily UAS diary for a month often reveals patterns, such as flares after painkillers [1]. The UAS7 calculator on this page can do the daily sums for you. As for targets, the international guideline aims for no symptoms at all (UAS7 of 0, UCT of 16) [1]; the 2021 Taiwanese consensus, noting that about 40% of patients reach a UAS7 of 0 and about 80% reach 5 or less, set the target at UAS7 ≤5 [2].

Treatment strategy: the 2026 international treatment ladder

The international guideline aims to treat until the disease is gone and to reach complete control while respecting safety and quality of life. The principle is "as much as needed, as little as possible": step up or down according to control and reassess every 3 to 6 months whether treatment is still needed [1]. In chronic spontaneous urticaria, medicines should be taken continuously, not only during flares [1].

  1. A standard-dose second-generation antihistamine taken every day (strong recommendation) [1].
  2. If control is poor, increase the same drug up to 4-fold (strong recommendation; this is off-label and should be done under medical supervision) [1].
  3. If control is still inadequate after 2 to 4 weeks on a high dose (or sooner if symptoms are intolerable), add omalizumab (strong recommendation), dupilumab or remibrutinib (conditional recommendations) to the antihistamine; the choice can depend on comorbidities, and they can be switched if control is inadequate [1].
  4. Still inadequate: add ciclosporin (conditional recommendation) [1].
  5. Beyond that: other therapies, with too little evidence for a recommendation, considered only in special circumstances [1].

Acute urticaria usually needs only a second-generation antihistamine: continue until lesions have cleared and no new ones have appeared for a day or two, then stop. If you were taking more than the standard dose, taper over several days, because some people rebound when it is stopped abruptly, particularly with cetirizine [14].

What does the Taiwanese consensus say?

The 2021 Taiwanese consensus follows the same order but counts updosing as a separate line, giving four lines: standard-dose second-generation antihistamine, then up to 4-fold after 2 to 4 weeks, then omalizumab after a further 2 to 4 weeks, then ciclosporin if omalizumab is insufficient after 3 to 6 months [2]. The main differences: the Taiwanese target is a UAS7 of 5 or less rather than complete control, and the consensus predates the trial results for dupilumab and remibrutinib, so neither is included [1][2]. To avoid confusion, this article describes the order as steps rather than mixing the two numbering systems.

Steroids: only short courses for flares

Oral steroids work quickly, but the international guideline strongly recommends against long-term systemic steroids or depot steroid injections. For an acute flare, a short oral course can be considered: in adults about 20–50 mg/day of prednisone equivalent for up to about 10 days [1]. Long-term use raises the risk of high blood sugar, high blood pressure, osteoporosis, infection and weight gain [4]. Overseas data show at least 20% of patients with chronic spontaneous urticaria receive oral steroids, for a median of 118 days [6]; in a 2013 Taiwanese survey, about half (47%) of those who had seen a doctor had taken oral steroids [33]. Needing repeated steroid courses is a signal to refer for advanced treatment [14][18].

Stepping down once controlled

After 3 to 6 months of complete control, your doctor can step treatment down gradually: lower the antihistamine dose, lengthen the interval between biologic injections, and watch for relapse [1][5]. Relapse during step-down does not mean treatment failed; people who relapse after stopping omalizumab usually respond again when it is restarted [18].

Antihistamines: how to take them, for how long, and are they safe?

Second-generation H1 antihistamines are first-line for every type of urticaria [1]. Those with urticaria evidence include bilastine, cetirizine, desloratadine, ebastine, fexofenadine, levocetirizine, loratadine, mizolastine and rupatadine, and head-to-head data are too limited to name a best one [1]. In Taiwan, the licensed indications of most cetirizine and fexofenadine products, all levocetirizine, desloratadine, bilastine and ebastine products, and some loratadine products include urticaria [42].

  • Take it every day, not just when itchy: regular use works best in chronic spontaneous urticaria, and erratic use leads to poor control [1][17]; on a given dose, further improvement is not expected after 1 to 2 weeks [1]. In inducible urticaria, regular or before-exposure use depends on the trigger, for example daily use through winter for cold urticaria [1].
  • Increase the dose only under medical supervision: the international guideline strongly recommends increasing the same drug up to 4-fold when the standard dose fails, strongly recommends against going beyond 4-fold, and advises against combining two different antihistamines [1]. However, a 2026 meta-analysis of 211 randomised trials found diminishing extra benefit above the standard dose and a likely rise in sleepiness, noting that the 4-fold recommendation rests mainly on expert opinion and few trials [10]. Whether and how far to increase is worth discussing with your doctor [10]. Higher doses are often split into twice-daily doses [14].
  • Will it make me drowsy? Most second-generation drugs rarely do, though cetirizine and levocetirizine may be slightly more sedating and can be taken in the evening [14]; the meta-analysis found bilastine, desloratadine and fexofenadine probably no more sedating than placebo [10].
  • First-generation antihistamines (chlorpheniramine, diphenhydramine, hydroxyzine): sedating, impair driving and reaction time, and cause anticholinergic effects such as dry mouth, blurred vision and urinary retention. The guideline advises against them as routine first-line treatment, and they are best avoided in young children and older adults [1][14].
  • Is long-term use safe? The guideline notes no serious adverse events reported with updosed second-generation antihistamines since 2000, and no loss of effect over time (no tachyphylaxis) [1]. A Taiwanese allergic rhinitis cohort found a rising dementia risk with cumulative antihistamine dose; the guideline judged the study not robust enough to change recommendations and treats it as a safety signal to monitor [1].

Taiwanese data: in a National Taiwan University Hospital study, patients with chronic idiopathic urticaria took standard-dose levocetirizine or desloratadine daily for 6 weeks; 60.7% of the 56 with complete data responded well, and those with allergic rhinitis or atopy were more likely to respond poorly [34].

When antihistamines are not enough: omalizumab, dupilumab, remibrutinib and ciclosporin

These drugs are for chronic spontaneous urticaria still uncontrolled on high-dose antihistamines; they are usually added to the antihistamine and managed by a specialist [1][18]. The guideline acknowledges that biologics and targeted drugs are costly and that ciclosporin has safety limits, so choices should be discussed with the patient [1]. For an overview of biologics, see our guide to biologics and small-molecule drugs in dermatology.

Omalizumab (anti-IgE antibody)

The only add-on drug with a strong recommendation in the international guideline [1]. It is injected under the skin, 300 mg every 4 weeks, regardless of IgE level or body weight [1]. In randomised trials, 36% on 300 mg became completely symptom-free (UAS of 0) versus 5.6% on placebo [4]. Many feel better in the first month and most respond within 4 months (3 doses), so about 16 weeks is enough to judge [18]. People with low IgE and type IIb autoimmunity respond less or more slowly [4][5]. It does not cure the disease and relapse can follow stopping, but re-treatment usually works [18]. Pregnancy reports so far show no sign of birth defects [1]. Anaphylaxis occurs in about 1 to 2 per 1,000 patients, after any dose, mostly within 2 hours but occasionally days later; stay for observation as your clinic arranges, and seek emergency care for wheeze, throat tightness or dizziness after going home [15][19].

Taiwan: the omalizumab licences (including a biosimilar approved in 2026) cover add-on treatment of chronic spontaneous urticaria in patients aged 12 and over with an inadequate response to H1 antihistamines [42], but the NHI reimbursement rules (22 September 2026 version) cover only severe persistent asthma, with no urticaria rule [41].

Dupilumab (anti-IL-4 receptor alpha antibody)

This is the same biologic used for atopic dermatitis. The guideline gives it a conditional recommendation: probably less effective than omalizumab, but effective even with low IgE and worth considering with coexisting atopic dermatitis, asthma or nasal polyps [1]. In a pooled analysis of 289 patients from two phase 3 trials, 30.6% were completely symptom-free at week 24 versus 15.9% on placebo, with improvement from week 3 [27]; its onset is slower than omalizumab's [18]. In patients uncontrolled on omalizumab, the trial missed its primary endpoint [5]. Its Taiwanese licence covers chronic spontaneous urticaria uncontrolled by H1 antihistamines in patients aged 12 and over [42]; NHI rules exist only for asthma and atopic dermatitis [41]. For atopic dermatitis, see our atopic dermatitis guide and how long to stay on dupilumab (an atopic dermatitis study).

Remibrutinib (oral BTK inhibitor)

An oral tablet, 25 mg twice daily, that blocks the activation signal inside mast cells [18]. The guideline gives it a conditional recommendation and notes it needs no injections or routine laboratory monitoring [1]. In two phase 3 trials (470 and 455 adults), 49.8% and 46.8% had well-controlled disease (UAS7 of 6 or less) at week 12 versus 24.8% and 19.6% on placebo; 31.1% and 27.9% were symptom-free versus 10.5% and 6.5%; improvement started as early as week 1 [26]. Petechiae (small bleeding spots) were more common than with placebo (3.8% vs 0.3%) [26]. It is metabolised by CYP3A4 and can interact with some medicines; some experts would avoid it in cirrhosis, bleeding disorders, anticoagulant use or with potentially interacting medicines, and there are no data in children, pregnancy or breastfeeding [18]. Tell your doctor before surgery or vaccination: the manufacturer advises stopping it 3 to 7 days before and after surgery depending on the procedure, and vaccines are best given before starting, or 1 week after stopping, with the drug resumed 2 weeks later [18]. Taiwan licensed it on 14 October 2026 for adults with chronic spontaneous urticaria still symptomatic on H1 antihistamines [42]; the 22 September 2026 NHI rules predate the licence and contain no entry for it [41].

Ciclosporin

An immunosuppressant that the guideline suggests for patients unresponsive to licensed treatments or unable to obtain them (conditional recommendation), noting it is not licensed for urticaria [1]; its risk-benefit is far better than long-term steroids [1]. Neither the Taiwanese ciclosporin licences nor the NHI rule (8.2.1) mention urticaria [41][42]. The dose is weight-based and set by the doctor, with blood pressure and kidney function monitored before and during treatment [5][18]. Side effects include high blood pressure, reduced kidney function, excess hair growth and gum overgrowth; it is usually avoided in kidney disease, poorly controlled hypertension and, in most cases, older adults [18].

Real-world experience in Taiwan

Taichung Veterans General Hospital analysed 409 patients with antihistamine-refractory chronic spontaneous urticaria (omalizumab 97, ciclosporin 85, hydroxychloroquine 227), all paying out of pocket. Median drug survival was 7.6, 7.2 and 4.6 months, and 29.2% of those who stopped omalizumab did so for financial reasons [35]. This is a single-centre retrospective study; drug survival reflects cost and tolerability as well as efficacy [35].

H2 blockers, montelukast and other drugs

The international guideline has removed H2 antihistamines, leukotriene antagonists (such as montelukast), hydroxychloroquine, methotrexate and dapsone from the recommended pathway because evidence is too limited, and makes no recommendation for or against combining H1 and H2 blockers [1]. Montelukast carries rare but confirmed neuropsychiatric side effects that patients should be warned about [1]. The 2014 Taiwanese consensus included H2 blockers and leukotriene antagonists as second- and third-line options; the 2021 update removed them [2][3]. Drugs in development (such as the anti-KIT antibody barzolvolimab, in phase 3 trials) are not yet recommended treatments [5].

Inducible urticaria: rubbing, cold, heat, sweating, pressure and sunlight

Inducible urticaria has a fixed trigger and appears only on exposure [1]. Avoidance helps but rarely gives full control, and the guideline warns against letting avoidance shrink daily life, for example telling people with cholinergic urticaria to stop all exercise or people with solar urticaria to stay indoors [1]. Treatment follows the same ladder as chronic spontaneous urticaria: second-generation antihistamines first, and omalizumab works in most inducible types [1][5].

Pregnancy, breastfeeding, children and older adults

Pregnancy and breastfeeding

The international guideline suggests the same treatment pathway in pregnancy and breastfeeding, used with more caution after weighing risks and benefits [1]. Systemic drugs are generally avoided early in pregnancy, but pregnant women still deserve good treatment; emergency visits for flares during pregnancy have been associated with preterm birth [1].

  • No birth defects have been reported with second-generation antihistamines; loratadine (by extension desloratadine) or cetirizine (by extension levocetirizine) are preferred. Updosing in pregnancy can only be suggested cautiously because safety studies are lacking, and first-generation antihistamines should be avoided [1].
  • Omalizumab use in pregnancy has shown no sign of birth defects so far [1]; ciclosporin has been associated with preterm birth and low birth weight [1]; remibrutinib has not been studied in pregnancy [18].
  • Breastfeeding: all H1 antihistamines pass into breast milk in small amounts, and first-generation ones occasionally make the baby drowsy, so second-generation drugs are advised [1]; the 2021 Taiwanese consensus considers breastfeeding acceptable during omalizumab treatment [2].

Children

Chronic urticaria in children resembles adult disease and follows the same pathway, adjusted for licensed age and weight, with second-generation antihistamines first; first-generation drugs impair school performance and are not recommended first-line [1]. The 2021 Taiwanese consensus also no longer recommends first-generation drugs under age 12 [2]. In Taiwan, the urticaria indications of omalizumab and dupilumab both start at age 12 [42]. Some antihistamine formulations are licensed for younger children, such as desloratadine syrup (from age 1) and bilastine orodispersible tablets (ages 2 to 11, weighing at least 15 kg); licensed ages differ by product [42]. Oral steroids should be used only very sparingly in children [1].

Taiwanese children: among 1,120 children seen at the Changhua Christian Hospital emergency department with a first episode of acute urticaria, the mean duration was 6.6 days, most (54.8%) cleared in 4 to 7 days and only 5.4% lasted beyond 15 days [37]. At Kaohsiung Chang Gung Memorial Hospital, 21 of 65 children (32%) hospitalised for antihistamine-unresponsive acute urticaria had serological evidence of Mycoplasma pneumoniae infection, and none became chronic over 6 months [38]. These are single-hospital data, and the latter applies only to the few children admitted [37][38].

Older adults

Second-generation antihistamines work at least as well in people over 60 as in younger adults [4]. The 2021 Taiwanese consensus advises reviewing medicines (ACE inhibitors, aspirin, other NSAIDs), checking kidney and liver function and managing comorbidities; first-generation antihistamines often cause urinary retention, dry mouth, worsening glaucoma and central nervous system effects, and raise the risk of falls and traffic accidents; bilastine or omalizumab can be considered with multiple comorbidities or kidney or liver impairment [2]. With reduced kidney function, levocetirizine and desloratadine doses need adjusting [17]. In an older person with widespread itch and hive-like patches, early bullous pemphigoid should also be considered [1][14].

Daily care and self-management

  • Take medicines regularly; once better, do not stop or cut down on your own, but discuss step-down at your next visit [1][17].
  • Keep a diary and photos: record your UAS daily and photograph flares, along with medicines, painkillers, alcohol and late nights, and show your doctor [1].
  • Avoid overheating: hot showers and humid heat often trigger hives; wear loose clothing and reduce pressure and friction from straps and waistbands [17]. The 2014 Taiwanese consensus also lists cooling lotions among non-drug measures [3].
  • Choose painkillers carefully: if NSAIDs make you worse, say so when seeing a doctor or buying over-the-counter medicines, and ask about acetaminophen instead [1][17]. Do not stop prescribed low-dose aspirin yourself [1].
  • Alcohol, stress and sleep: hives may worsen after alcohol and are harder to control under stress or sleep loss [17]; the guideline also encourages stress-reducing measures [1].
  • Creams do little: topical steroids do not help urticaria (with a possible exception for pressure urticaria on the soles), and topical antihistamines are not advised [1][14]. For proper use of steroid creams, see our topical steroid guide.
  • Look after your mood: itch, poor sleep and uncertainty often bring anxiety or depression; overseas about one-third of patients have at least one psychiatric disorder [6], and Taiwanese NHI data also show more psychiatric diagnoses in people with chronic urticaria [29]. If you are struggling, tell your doctor.

Common myths about urticaria

  • "It must be something I ate." Not necessarily. No cause is found in 30% to 50% of acute urticaria [14]; in Taiwanese paediatric emergency data, infection (48.4%) was more common than food (23.5%) [36]. Chronic urticaria is rarely caused by food allergy [1].
  • "It keeps coming back because my immunity is weak." No. More than half of chronic spontaneous urticaria involves autoantibodies activating mast cells, closer to an over-reacting immune system [1]. The guideline warns that blaming "histamine intolerance" often leads to needless diets and supplements [1].
  • "Antihistamines damage the brain and are addictive, so avoid them." Second-generation antihistamines can be taken regularly long term; the guideline notes no serious adverse events reported with updosing and no loss of effect over time [1]. The real cautions are sedation and anticholinergic effects of first-generation drugs [1][14], and possible rebound if a higher-than-standard dose is stopped abruptly, so taper [14].
  • "Hives are contagious." They are not. They come from your own skin mast cells releasing mediators, but others often assume they are infectious, which can keep patients at home [1][17].
  • "Chronic urticaria never goes away, so it means lifelong pills." Most people remit within a few years (average 2 to 5 years), though timing varies [4][16]. Meanwhile, medicines control symptoms in most people, and treatment can be stepped down once stable [1][17].
  • "An allergy blood test will find the cause." Broad allergy panels are not recommended for chronic urticaria [1][14]. Nearly half of Taiwanese survey respondents who had seen a doctor had allergy screening [33], yet a National Taiwan University Hospital study found dust mite IgE status unrelated to disease duration, symptoms or treatment response [34]. Targeted testing makes sense only when episodes clearly follow a particular food or drug, usually within 2 hours [14].
  • "A steroid shot is the quickest fix." The guideline strongly recommends against depot steroid injections and long-term systemic steroids; for flares, at most a short oral course of about 10 days [1].
  • "Steroid cream will clear it." Topical steroids do not help urticaria [1], yet in the 2013 Taiwanese survey they were the medicine doctors prescribed most often (66%) [33].
  • "Seafood, spicy food and 'triggering foods' must be strictly avoided." Long, broad avoidance is not needed. The Taiwanese consensus advises avoiding fragranced or spicy foods and cutting down on alcohol and coffee [2]; the international guideline limits special diets to a 2–3-week diagnostic trial [1]. Once the urticaria remits, foods that seemed to worsen it can usually be eaten again [16].
  • "Stop the pills as soon as the rash is gone." In chronic urticaria, treatment is stepped down gradually by your doctor after 3 to 6 months of complete control [1][5]; stopping on your own can lead to relapse, and some people rebound when a higher-than-standard dose is stopped abruptly [14].

When to seek urgent care, and when to see a dermatologist

Seek urgent care (call 119 or go to an emergency department)

  • Hives or swelling with breathing difficulty, wheeze, hoarseness, throat tightness or swelling of the mouth or throat [15][14].
  • With dizziness, near-fainting or confusion, or vomiting, diarrhoea or cramping abdominal pain: this may be anaphylaxis [2][14][15].
  • A rapid reaction within minutes to 2 hours of a food, drug or sting, together with any of the symptoms above [14][15]. If you have been prescribed an adrenaline auto-injector, use it as instructed and still go to the emergency department [21][42].
  • Tongue or throat swelling without hives (for example on an ACE inhibitor or with hereditary angioedema): such swelling does not respond to antihistamines, and laryngeal swelling can be fatal [1][24].

Ordinary chronic spontaneous urticaria rarely affects the airway [4][17]; the situations above are the danger signs to rule out. In Taiwanese licence data, adrenaline auto-injectors hold 2 non-cancelled, in-date licences in the "restricted to physician use" category, with an indication for immediate self-use by people with a history of allergic reactions [42].

See a dermatologist soon

  • Individual lesions lasting over 24 hours, painful, leaving bruises or pigmentation, or with fever, joint pain or abdominal pain [4][5][14].
  • Recurrent swelling without hives, or swelling that does not respond to antihistamines and lasts more than 2 to 5 days [1][4].
  • Symptoms beyond 6 weeks, or needing repeated steroid courses [14].
  • A UCT still below 12 after 2 to 4 weeks of regular high-dose second-generation antihistamines [4].
  • Suspected food or drug allergy (a clear time link to exposure; an allergy specialist is also appropriate), or cold urticaria with past systemic symptoms [14][20].

Frequently asked questions

How long until urticaria goes away?

About two-thirds of acute urticaria clears within 6 weeks, most within one to three weeks [14]. Chronic spontaneous urticaria lasts 2 to 5 years on average, with 30% to 50% remitting within a year; some last longer or relapse after remission [1][16]. Either way, treatment lets most people live normally in the meantime [17].

How long should I take antihistamines, and are they safe long term?

For acute urticaria, take it until the rash has gone and no new lesions appear for a day or two [14]. For chronic urticaria, take it daily and let your doctor step down gradually after 3 to 6 months of complete control [1][5]. Second-generation antihistamines have a good long-term safety record [1].

Can I drive while taking antihistamines?

First-generation antihistamines cause sedation and slower reactions in more than 20% of people, so avoid driving or operating machinery on them [14]. Most second-generation drugs have little effect, though cetirizine and levocetirizine make some people drowsy; bilastine, desloratadine and fexofenadine are probably no more sedating than placebo [10][14]. After a dose increase, make sure you are not drowsy before driving [10].

Does NHI cover biologics for urticaria?

Under the NHI drug reimbursement rules of 22 September 2026, omalizumab, dupilumab and remibrutinib have no urticaria rule (omalizumab and dupilumab rules cover asthma, atopic dermatitis and other conditions) [41]. Although all three hold Taiwanese licences for chronic spontaneous urticaria [42], urticaria use is usually self-paid, as in the Taichung Veterans General Hospital study [35]. Rules change, so check with your doctor.

My child broke out in hives while on medicine for a cold. Is it a drug allergy?

Not necessarily. Infection is the commonest cause of acute urticaria in children [36], and in a child taking antibiotics, virus-related hives and antibiotic allergy are hard to tell apart; a later drug challenge is sometimes needed [14]. Tell the doctor when the medicine was started and when the hives appeared, rather than labelling the child allergic yourself.

What are the tiny bumps after exercise or a hot bath?

Probably cholinergic urticaria: tiny 1–3 mm wheals with large red flares when body temperature rises, often preceded by tingling [8][20]. But dizziness, wheeze or throat tightness during exercise may be anaphylaxis: call 119 or go to an emergency department at once (see "Seek urgent care" above) [15][20]. Larger hives during exercise alone also need prompt medical review to rule out exercise-induced anaphylaxis [8][20].

Can I take antihistamines during pregnancy?

Discuss it with your doctor. The guideline prefers loratadine or cetirizine, with no birth defects reported; studies show reassuring data in large numbers of pregnant women and minimal passage into breast milk [1][14]. Whether to increase the dose or add other drugs is a medical decision [1].

Endotypes and biomarkers

Type I (autoallergic) accounts for about 50%, with IgE against self-antigens such as TPO and IL-24; type IIb meeting all three PURIST criteria (positive ASST, IgG anti-IgE/FcεRI immunoassay, positive BAT or BHRA) is only about 8%, and a positive ASST alone has poor specificity [5][7]. Laboratory tendencies: type I has high or normal total IgE (≥100 or 43–100 IU/mL); type IIb has low total IgE (≤43 IU/mL), IgG anti-TPO ≥34 kU/L, basopenia, eosinopenia and raised CRP [1][7]; the JAMA review adds clinical clues to autoimmune disease: female sex, symptoms on more than 5 days a week, angioedema, nocturnal symptoms and an inadequate response to antihistamines and omalizumab [4]. The guideline stresses these cut-offs are indicative only and not for definitive endotyping [1]. Clinically, total IgE >100 IU/mL predicts a better omalizumab response [7]; low IgE, high thyroid antibodies and anti-FcεRI antibodies predict a poor one [8]; dupilumab efficacy is independent of low IgE [1][27]; ciclosporin may be especially effective in low-IgE type IIb disease [1]. In the National Taiwan University Hospital cohort, ASST was positive in 37.3% (22/59) and unrelated to severity [34].

Omalizumab: updosing, step-down and relapse

Evidence for updosing (600 mg, every 2 weeks) is limited: the network meta-analysis found no study comparing high with standard doses, and in non-randomised data about 69% (95% CI 59%–78%) of standard-dose non-responders responded to higher doses [9]; some experts do not exceed 600 mg every 4 weeks and stop after a 4-month trial if control remains inadequate [18]. After 3 months of complete control the interval can be extended (off-label) [1]; the JEADV review extends it by one week at a time up to 8 weeks, then stops [5]. An international review of anti-IgE therapy cites a 2007 US allergy-society joint task force recommendation (modifiable by the clinician; the home-injection criteria are US FDA criteria): observation for 2 hours after each of the first three injections and 30 minutes thereafter, with an adrenaline auto-injector prescribed and carried for at least 24 hours after each injection; criteria for home self-injection include no prior anaphylaxis and 3 doses given in the clinic [19]. More than half relapse within months of stopping; a meta-analysis by a Taipei Medical University Hospital team (10 studies; 488 relapsers, 479 non-relapsers) found no significant association between baseline total IgE and relapse (mean difference 47.910, 95% CI −11.528 to 107.347; P = .114), with high baseline activity, long duration and slow or incomplete control before stopping more relevant [12].

It may weakly inhibit P-gp, so take care with narrow-therapeutic-index drugs such as digoxin; platelet counts do not fall, but platelet GPVI signalling is inhibited, and petechiae and minor mucocutaneous bleeding may be seen [18].

Ciclosporin dosing and monitoring

The guideline gives 3–5 mg/kg/day [1]; the JEADV and JAMA reviews give 1–5 mg/kg/day with 3 mg/kg/day as a practical start and favour lower doses [4][5]; some experts use 3 mg/kg/day in two divided doses (100–150 mg twice daily for most adults) and prefer the modified formulation [18]. Check renal and hepatic function, blood pressure, blood count, lipids and infection screening at baseline and regularly thereafter [5]; response often appears within 2–4 weeks and exceeds 70% by 12 weeks [5]. Maintain the controlling dose for 3 months, then taper over months; 6–9 months usually suffices [18]. Avoid in chronic kidney disease, poorly controlled hypertension and most older adults [18]. In Taichung, the median real-world starting dose was only 1.0 mg/kg/day, and 7.7% of those who stopped (5 of 65) did so because of adverse effects [35].

Taiwan real world: Sheu 2026 drug-survival study

Taichung Veterans General Hospital reviewed 17,294 CSU patients (1 January 2013 to 31 May 2024) and included 409 who moved to a second-line drug after failing antihistamines (2–4-fold for at least 2 weeks), excluding concomitant autoimmune disease [35]. Against hydroxychloroquine and adjusted for age and sex, omalizumab had a lower discontinuation risk (HR 0.65, 95% CI 0.50–0.85) while ciclosporin did not differ significantly (HR 0.85, 0.64–1.13) [35]. With total IgE >114 kU/L, median survival was 19.0 months for omalizumab versus 6.7 and 4.6 months for ciclosporin and hydroxychloroquine (omalizumab HR 0.48, 0.32–0.72); there was no difference with normal IgE [35]. Discontinuation reasons were often unknown (omalizumab 47.2%, ciclosporin 86.2%, hydroxychloroquine 95.5%), UAS7 was not recorded and confounding by indication is likely; hydroxychloroquine is off-label and not a standard second-line option in the international guideline [1][35].

Provocation testing in inducible urticaria

Aims: exclude mimics, identify the subtype and define thresholds; thresholds and the UCT also track treatment [1]. Methods: dermographism by stroking with a blunt object, a dermographometer (36 g/mm²) or FricTest [5]; cold urticaria with an ice cube in a thin plastic bag on the volar forearm for 5 minutes, or TempTest for the critical temperature [4][14]; delayed pressure with 7 kg on a 3 cm shoulder strap for 15 minutes, read over 24 hours [14]; cholinergic by exercise to sweating then 15 more minutes, or pulse-controlled graded ergometry [1][14]; heat with a 45°C test tube or metal cylinder for 5 minutes [14]; aquagenic with a 35–37°C compress for up to 40 minutes [5]; solar with UVA 6 J/cm², UVB 60 mJ/cm² and visible light on normally covered skin [14]; vibratory with a vortex mixer [5][14]. Most are read within 5–10 minutes [4]. Antihistamines are withheld beforehand, from several days to at least 5 days depending on the topic [20][21]; challenges with generalised exposure can provoke systemic reactions and belong in specialist settings equipped for anaphylaxis [20][21]. For solar urticaria, see the Fitzpatrick Ch.96 notes.

Comorbidities: Taiwanese claims data and international studies

  • Chu 2017 (national claims): versus the general population, rheumatic disease SPR 2.74 (95% CI 2.70–2.77), thyroid disease 1.81 (1.78–1.83), inflammatory disease 1.57 (1.56–1.58), psychiatric disorders 1.87 (1.85–1.88); thyroid disease did not rise with disease duration, suggesting a link with onset rather than persistence [28].
  • Chu 2020: with severity graded by medication, adjusted psychiatric RR was 1.43 (mild), 1.50 (moderate) and 2.32 (severe); 20–30% had used psychiatric medication, and the authors recommend routine mental-health assessment [29].
  • Chiu 2018 (one-million sample): adjusted OR for atopic dermatitis 1.94, allergic rhinitis 1.52, SLE 1.58, autoimmune thyroid disease 1.31, and lower for psoriasis (0.54); some findings lost significance after correction for multiple testing [30].
  • Chen 2012: in 12,720 patients with chronic urticaria on long-term antihistamines, overall cancer SIR was 2.2 (95% CI 2.0–2.3) and haematological malignancy 4.1 (3.1–5.4); the highest SIR (4.3) in the first year suggests possible detection bias [31]. A Swedish cohort showed no increase (RR 0.88), and guidelines do not suggest extra cancer screening [16].
  • Yong 2018: for all urticaria (not only chronic), the adjusted HR for anaphylaxis was 2.529 (2.442–2.619), but the outcome codes include non-specific allergy codes and the control rate far exceeds the background incidence the authors cite, so the figure should not be used in patient education [32]. The authors also note that adrenaline auto-injectors are not standard pre-hospital equipment in Taiwan [32].
  • International: the commonest comorbidities are inducible urticaria, autoimmune disease, metabolic syndrome and allergies [1]; about 10–28% have at least one autoimmune disease, Hashimoto thyroiditis about 20% [4]. In US TriNetX data (264,680 matched pairs), 5-year all-cause mortality was 9.0% versus 5.14% (HR 1.69) and suicidal ideation or attempt HR 3.14; this is observational and cannot establish causation, and a Danish registry study found no excess mortality [11].

Paediatric acute urticaria and angioedema in Taiwan

Three Changhua Christian Hospital paediatric emergency studies come from one centre and team; Liu 2008 and Liu 2010 overlap in time and probably in patients, and all are univariable [36][37][39]. Liu 2008 (953 children): causes shifted with age, infection falling from 56.5% in infants to 17.1% in adolescents and food rising from 15.9% to 36.6%; 30.8% were admitted (local practice) [36]. Liu 2010 (1,120) defined severity by disposition: mild, moderate and severe 54.2%, 18% and 27.8%; adrenaline was used in only 1.3% [37]. Syue 2019 (406 first episodes of acute angioedema, HAE excluded): infection 25.4%, food 24.4%, drugs 22.9% (NSAIDs 41% of drug cases), unknown 22.4%; 26.8% admitted, 19.2% needed oxygen, 2% intubation and 5.2% intensive care, all surviving [39]. Wu 2009 (Kaohsiung Chang Gung, 65 admitted children versus 49 respiratory controls): 32% had Mycoplasma pneumoniae serology, and azithromycin-treated children improved faster, but treatment was given only to the seropositive group and was not randomised [38].

Acute urticaria and angioedema: key points for primary and emergency care
  • Antihistamines: a second-generation H1 antihistamine is the mainstay, starting at the standard dose and increased 2- to 3-fold if needed; H2 blockers and topical agents are not used routinely [14].
  • Steroids: added only for symptoms persisting after several days of regular antihistamines or for prominent angioedema, with doses differing by source: international literature gives prednisone 30–60 mg/day in adults and prednisolone 0.5–1 mg/kg/day in children (maximum 60 mg), tapered over 5–7 days, avoiding repeated courses [14]; the 2021 Taiwanese consensus suggests a short oral course for severe symptoms or failure of antihistamines [2].
  • Adrenaline: for anaphylaxis (breathing difficulty, wheeze, throat tightness, hypotension, vomiting or abdominal pain) or rapidly progressing angioedema that could involve the airway, give intramuscular epinephrine (1 mg/mL) into the mid-outer thigh at once: 0.3–0.5 mg in adults and 0.01 mg/kg in children (maximum 0.5 mg), repeated after 5 minutes if needed; antihistamines are not sufficient for anaphylaxis [14][24]. The 2021 Taiwanese consensus likewise makes it first line for serious respiratory, cardiac or gastrointestinal symptoms or when anaphylaxis cannot be excluded, and places it after H1 and H2 antihistamines and systemic steroids for severe angioedema alone [2].
  • Bradykinin-mediated (ACE inhibitor, HAE): adrenaline is unlikely to help, so the airway comes first; on-demand HAE treatments (C1 inhibitor, icatibant and others) take about 30 minutes or more to work [24].
  • Observation and discharge: observe until clear improvement; isolated angioedema resolves within hours if mild and 3–5 days if severe [24]. After anaphylaxis or with a suspected high-risk food (in adults, peanuts, tree nuts, fish or shellfish), prescribe self-injectable adrenaline and refer to an allergist [14][24]. Recurrent idiopathic angioedema without wheals can be given a trial of a twice-daily second-generation antihistamine with referral; in the largest series, 86% of 294 patients improved markedly or resolved [24].

When to refer

  • UCT still <12 after 2–4 weeks of high-dose second-generation antihistamines; repeated oral steroid courses; or symptoms beyond 6 weeks that are hard to control [4][14][18].
  • Wheals lasting ≥24 hours with pigmentation, antihistamine-refractory angioedema without wheals lasting more than 2–5 days, or extracutaneous symptoms such as fever, arthralgia or abdominal pain [4].
  • Suspected IgE-mediated allergy (onset minutes to 2 hours after exposure), possible anaphylaxis or a high-risk food: refer to allergy and consider self-injectable adrenaline [14]; cold urticaria with systemic or atypical symptoms [21]. In children with recurrent peripheral or upper-airway oedema or unexplained recurrent abdominal pain, consider HAE testing regardless of family history [13].
